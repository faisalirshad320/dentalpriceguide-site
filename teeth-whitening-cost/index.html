Teeth Whitening Cost: What Professional and At-Home Whitening Really Costs

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

Teeth whitening cost at a dental office runs $40 to $700 per in-office bleaching visit out of network, according to Delta Dental. Laser whitening averages $792 and UV whitening $583 per CareCredit. Insurance usually does not pay because whitening is cosmetic. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

  • Dental insurance usually does not cover whitening because it is a cosmetic procedure.

How much does teeth whitening cost?

Teeth whitening costs $40 to $700 per in-office bleaching visit when billed out of network, based on 2024 data from Delta Dental. Light-activated treatments sit toward the upper end: CareCredit's national cost study lists laser in-office whitening at $792 on average and UV whitening at $583.

Teeth whitening prices span such a wide range because "whitening" covers very different services. The low end often reflects a simple bleaching application or a take-home kit dispensed by the office. The high end reflects a longer chair-side session with a stronger gel, gum protection, a light or laser, and sometimes custom trays to keep up results at home.

Teeth whitening sold over the counter costs much less than any professional option, which is why many people start there. Strips, trays and whitening toothpastes are covered in more detail further down this page. Keep in mind that every figure here is a national average; dentists in large cities often charge more than offices in smaller towns.

Ask whether a quoted whitening price is per visit or for the full treatment. Some people need two or more sessions to reach their target shade, and that changes the real total.

What is teeth whitening?

Teeth whitening is a cosmetic treatment that uses a peroxide-based gel to break down stain molecules inside the enamel and dentin, making teeth look lighter. The two active ingredients used in the US are hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide, which releases hydrogen peroxide more slowly.

Teeth whitening works best on yellowish stains from coffee, tea, red wine, tobacco and ageing. Grayish or brownish discoloration, such as staining from certain antibiotics taken in childhood or from a tooth that has died after trauma, responds less predictably and may need a different approach such as internal bleaching, bonding or veneers.

Teeth whitening also differs from "whitening" toothpaste and polishing. Those products remove surface stains with mild abrasives or chemical agents, but they do not change the tooth's internal color in the same way bleaching does. Cleveland Clinic's overview of cosmetic dentistry places whitening alongside bonding, veneers and other appearance-focused treatments.

Teeth whitening does not change the color of crowns, veneers, bonding or tooth-colored fillings. If you have restorations on your front teeth, they may look darker than your natural teeth after bleaching and might need replacing to match, which adds to the total cost.

What are the teeth whitening options and how do they compare on cost?

Teeth whitening options fall into four main groups: in-office bleaching, light-activated in-office whitening, dentist-supervised take-home trays and over-the-counter products. Each trades price against speed and supervision.

  • In-office bleaching: A dentist or hygienist protects your gums and applies a high-strength gel, usually in several short rounds during one visit. Delta Dental puts this at $40 to $700 per visit out of network.
  • Laser or UV whitening: A light is aimed at the gel during the session. CareCredit reports averages of $792 for laser whitening and $583 for UV whitening. Whether the light adds much whitening beyond the gel itself is still debated, so ask your dentist what you are paying for and how many sessions the quote covers.
  • Take-home trays from a dentist: Custom-fitted trays and a lower-strength gel worn at home over one to two weeks. Results are often similar to in-office whitening, just slower, and the trays can be reused for touch-ups.
  • Over-the-counter products: Strips, generic trays, pens and toothpastes cost the least and use lower peroxide levels. Our roundup of the best at-home teeth whitening kits compares the main formats.

Teeth whitening strips are the most popular home option. If you use them, timing your brushing matters for comfort and results; see our guide to whether you brush your teeth after whitening strips. Whitening toothpaste is the cheapest way to manage surface stains between treatments, and our list of the best teeth whitening toothpaste explains what to look for.

Do whitening gum, charcoal and DIY methods work?

Whitening gum, charcoal powders and DIY remedies can remove some surface stains, but they do not bleach teeth the way peroxide products do. They are cheap, which is their main appeal, and the results are usually modest.

Whitening chewing gum works mainly by increasing saliva and, in some products, adding mild stain-lifting ingredients. Sugar-free gum after meals is good for your teeth in general, but it will not produce a noticeable shade change on its own.

Charcoal toothpastes and powders are abrasive. They can scrub off surface stain, but frequent use can wear enamel and irritate gums, and worn enamel lets the yellower dentin underneath show through, which makes teeth look darker over time. Baking soda is milder and is a common ingredient in whitening toothpastes, though it also works only on the surface.

Lemon juice, vinegar and other acidic home remedies are best avoided. Acid softens enamel, and brushing right after acid exposure can wear it away. If you want a low-cost first step, a professional cleaning followed by a whitening toothpaste is safer and usually more effective than kitchen remedies.

What changes the price of teeth whitening at the dentist?

The price of teeth whitening at the dentist depends mostly on the method used, the number of sessions you need, your location and whether take-home trays are bundled in. Starting shade matters too, because heavily stained teeth often need more than one round.

  • Method: A simple bleaching application costs less than a laser or UV session with a longer chair time.
  • Number of visits: Many quotes are per visit. Deep or stubborn stains may need two or more appointments.
  • Bundled extras: Some offices include custom trays and refill gel for home maintenance; others charge separately.
  • Exam and cleaning: Most dentists want a checkup and cleaning first so they can bleach clean enamel and rule out cavities or gum problems. Those are usually billed on their own, though insurance often covers them.
  • Location and practice type: Fees in big cities and high-end cosmetic practices tend to run higher than in smaller towns.
  • Sensitivity management: Desensitizing gels or fluoride treatments may be added if your teeth are sensitive.

Teeth whitening quotes are easiest to compare when you ask each office the same questions: how many sessions, which method, whether trays are included, and what a touch-up costs later.

Is teeth whitening covered by insurance?

Teeth whitening is usually not covered by dental insurance because it is considered a cosmetic procedure, according to Delta Dental. Plans pay for care that keeps teeth healthy or restores function, and bleaching is done to change appearance.

Teeth whitening may sit next to covered care, though. The exam and cleaning many dentists want before whitening are typically covered as preventive services. In rare cases, internal bleaching of a single darkened tooth after a root canal may be handled differently, so it is worth asking your plan. Our page on whether teeth whitening is covered by insurance goes through the edge cases and what to ask your insurer.

Teeth whitening is also generally not an eligible expense for HSA or FSA funds, because tax rules exclude purely cosmetic procedures. Check with your plan administrator before paying with those accounts.

How can you pay less for teeth whitening?

You can pay less for teeth whitening by choosing take-home trays over a chair-side session, looking for dental school clinics, bundling whitening with a covered cleaning visit and protecting results so you need fewer touch-ups.

  1. Start with a cleaning. A professional cleaning, usually covered by insurance, removes surface stains. Some people find their teeth are bright enough afterwards.
  2. Ask about take-home trays. Dentist-supervised trays are often priced below a single in-office session and can be reused for years.
  3. Check dental schools. Teaching clinics sometimes offer cosmetic services at reduced fees, with students working under faculty supervision.
  4. Ask about in-network discounts. Even when whitening is not covered, some plans give members a reduced fee at in-network offices.
  5. Use payment plans if needed. Many offices offer payment plans or third-party financing for cosmetic work. Read the interest terms before signing.
  6. Protect your results. Rinsing after coffee or red wine, not smoking and using a whitening toothpaste can stretch the time between touch-ups.

Teeth whitening deals that seem very cheap are worth a second look. Confirm that a licensed dentist examines your teeth first, because bleaching over untreated decay or gum disease can cause pain.

What happens during professional teeth whitening?

Professional teeth whitening usually takes 60 to 90 minutes in the chair and follows the same basic steps in most offices. Your dentist records your starting shade, protects your gums and lips, then applies the whitening gel in timed rounds.

  1. Exam and shade check, often with a cleaning first.
  2. Cheek retractor placed and a protective barrier painted over the gums.
  3. Whitening gel applied to the front surfaces of the teeth, sometimes with a light or laser.
  4. Gel removed and reapplied for several rounds, depending on the product.
  5. Final rinse, shade comparison and, in some offices, a desensitizing treatment.

Teeth whitening results can look brighter right after the visit because teeth are slightly dehydrated, then settle over a day or two. Our teeth whitening before and after page shows typical results and why they vary from person to person.

Teeth whitening aftercare usually means avoiding strongly colored food and drinks for the first day or two. Follow your dentist's instructions, since they may differ depending on the product used.

Is teeth whitening safe, and does it cause sensitivity?

Teeth whitening is generally safe for healthy adult teeth when used as directed, but temporary tooth sensitivity and gum irritation are common side effects. Cleveland Clinic notes that a dental exam beforehand helps identify who is a good candidate.

Teeth whitening sensitivity usually shows up as short, sharp twinges to cold or air and fades within a few days of stopping treatment. Using a toothpaste for sensitive teeth for a couple of weeks before and during whitening, shortening wear time, or spacing sessions further apart often helps. Our guide to teeth whitening for sensitive teeth explains these options.

Teeth whitening is usually not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, for children whose teeth are still developing, or when there is untreated decay, exposed roots or gum disease. Overusing high-strength products can make sensitivity worse without making teeth meaningfully whiter.

Stop whitening and contact your dentist if you have severe or lasting tooth pain, gum blistering or white patches on the gums, or swelling. Seek urgent care for facial swelling or fever, which point to a problem other than normal whitening sensitivity.

How long does teeth whitening last?

Teeth whitening results typically last from several months to a few years, depending on your habits and the method used. Coffee, tea, red wine, dark sauces and tobacco bring stains back fastest.

Teeth whitening results fade gradually rather than all at once, so most people plan occasional touch-ups. Reusable custom trays make touch-ups cheaper, because you only buy refill gel. Our guide on how long teeth whitening lasts explains how to stretch the time between treatments and when a touch-up makes sense.

Teeth whitening value is best judged by cost over time, not the first price you see. A cheaper option that needs frequent repeats can end up costing as much as a professional treatment that lasts longer.

Frequently asked questions

How much is teeth whitening at the dentist?

Teeth whitening at the dentist costs $40 to $700 per in-office bleaching visit when billed out of network, according to Delta Dental's 2024 data. Laser whitening averages $792 and UV whitening averages $583 nationally per CareCredit. Prices vary by city, method and the number of sessions you need, so ask for a full quote.

How much does professional teeth whitening cost?

Professional teeth whitening costs $40 to $700 per in-office visit out of network, based on Delta Dental data, with light-activated options averaging $583 for UV whitening and $792 for laser whitening per CareCredit. Dentist-supervised take-home trays are another professional option and are often priced below a chair-side session.

Does insurance cover teeth whitening?

Dental insurance usually does not cover teeth whitening because it is considered cosmetic, according to Delta Dental. The exam and cleaning done before whitening are usually covered as preventive care. Some plans offer reduced member fees for whitening at in-network offices, so it is worth calling your insurer to ask.

Is in-office whitening better than whitening strips?

In-office whitening works faster than whitening strips because it uses a stronger gel under professional supervision, often reaching a noticeable change in one visit. Strips use lower peroxide levels and take longer, but they cost much less. Both can lighten natural teeth, and neither changes the color of crowns, veneers or fillings.

Can whitening damage your teeth or gums?

Whitening used as directed does not usually damage healthy enamel, but it commonly causes temporary sensitivity and gum irritation. Overusing products, poorly fitting trays or whitening over untreated cavities can cause pain. A dental exam before whitening helps avoid these problems. Stop and call your dentist if pain is severe or lasting.

Can I use my HSA or FSA for teeth whitening?

Teeth whitening is generally not an eligible HSA or FSA expense because tax rules exclude purely cosmetic procedures. The exam and cleaning before whitening are usually eligible. Check with your plan administrator before using these accounts, since paying for an ineligible expense can lead to taxes or penalties.
