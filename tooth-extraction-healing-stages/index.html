Normal Socket After Tooth Extraction: What Healing Looks Like Day by Day

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A normal socket after tooth extraction holds a dark red blood clot on day 1, then turns creamy white or yellow as granulation tissue forms over days 2 to 4. The gum usually closes over 1 to 3 weeks, while bone keeps filling in for months. Cleveland Clinic outlines the process.

Key takeaways
  • A dark red or maroon clot filling the socket in the first 24 hours is normal and protects the bone.
  • White or yellowish tissue appearing around days 2 to 4 is usually healing granulation tissue, not pus.
  • Soreness and swelling usually peak in the first two to three days, then ease steadily.
  • Pain that suddenly gets worse around days 3 to 5, an empty-looking socket or a foul taste can signal dry socket.
  • Your dentist's or oral surgeon's aftercare instructions always take priority over general timelines.

What does a normal socket look like after tooth extraction?

A normal socket after tooth extraction looks like a hole filled with a dark red, jelly-like blood clot during the first day, with pink, mildly swollen gum around the edges. That clot is the foundation of healing: it seals the bone and nerve endings and gives new tissue a frame to grow on.

A healing tooth extraction site changes color as the days pass. By day 2 or 3, the top of the clot often turns creamy white, grey or pale yellow. Many people worry this is infection, but in most cases it is granulation tissue, a soft layer of new connective tissue and blood vessels. Granulation tissue is usually smooth, does not smell bad and does not get more painful each day.

A tooth extraction site after 3 days should look smaller and calmer than on day 1, even if the area is still tender. Small white strands of food or a little bleeding when you brush near it are also common. What should not happen is an empty-looking socket with visible bone, a foul smell, or pain that keeps climbing. Those changes are covered in the warning signs section below.

Tooth extraction healing stages, day by day

Tooth extraction healing happens in overlapping stages: clot formation in the first day, granulation tissue in the first week, gum closure over the following weeks, and bone fill over several months. The ranges below are typical for a simple extraction; surgical extractions and wisdom teeth often take longer.

  1. Hours 0 to 24, clot formation: bleeding slows and a clot forms. Light oozing that tints saliva pink is expected.
  2. Days 2 to 4, inflammation and granulation: swelling and stiffness peak, then start to ease. White or yellow granulation tissue covers the clot.
  3. Days 5 to 10, early closure: gum tissue grows in from the edges and the opening shrinks. Stitches, if used, dissolve or are removed.
  4. Weeks 2 to 4, soft tissue closure: the gum is largely closed. A small dip where the tooth was is normal.
  5. Months 1 to 6, bone remodeling: bone fills the socket under the gum and the jaw ridge reshapes.

How long a tooth extraction takes to heal therefore depends on what you mean by healed. Most people feel close to normal within about a week, but full bone healing takes months. That matters if you plan an implant later, because your dentist may wait for bone to mature or place a bone graft at the time of extraction.

How long to keep gauze in after tooth extraction

Gauze after tooth extraction is usually kept in place with firm, steady biting pressure for about 30 to 60 minutes, then replaced with fresh gauze only if bleeding continues. Most people can stop using gauze within the first several hours, once bleeding has slowed to light pink oozing.

Tooth extraction aftercare with gauze works best when the pad sits directly over the socket. Fold it into a firm square, place it over the site and bite down without chewing or talking. Changing it too often can disturb the forming clot. If bleeding persists, a moistened black tea bag held with pressure can help, because tea contains tannins that support clotting.

When to stop using gauze is the point where a fresh pad comes out only lightly tinged. Some streaking in saliva for the first day is normal. Bleeding that soaks through gauze repeatedly after a few hours of firm pressure is not normal and needs a call to your dentist.

What to do after tooth extraction to help the socket heal

The most important thing to do after tooth extraction is protect the blood clot for the first several days. That means avoiding suction, keeping the area clean gently, and giving the body what it needs to heal.

  • No suction: avoid straws, spitting forcefully and smoking or vaping for at least the first few days. Our guide to smoking after tooth extraction explains why tobacco raises the risk of dry socket.
  • Gentle rinsing: skip rinsing on the first day, then use warm salt water gently after meals as your dentist directs.
  • Brushing: brush other teeth normally and be careful near the socket.
  • Diet: choose soft, cool or lukewarm foods and chew on the other side. See what to eat after tooth extraction for ideas.
  • Swelling control: cold packs on the cheek during the first day, and sleeping with your head raised, can help.
  • Pain relief: take pain medicine only as directed by your dentist or pharmacist.

Pain after tooth extraction usually peaks within the first two to three days and then fades. How long pain lasts depends on the difficulty of the extraction, but steady improvement each day is the pattern to expect. Your dentist's or surgeon's written instructions take priority over any general advice.

Signs a tooth extraction site is not healing normally

A tooth extraction site is not healing normally if pain gets worse after day 3, the socket looks empty with visible bone, or you notice pus, a foul taste, fever or spreading swelling. These signs can point to dry socket or infection, which need a dentist's care.

Dry socket happens when the clot dissolves or is dislodged before the bone is covered. Cleveland Clinic notes it typically shows up a few days after the extraction, with throbbing pain that can spread to the ear, bad breath and a socket that looks dry or greyish. Treatment is usually a medicated dressing placed by your dentist.

Infection looks different from normal white granulation tissue. Pus is thicker, may be yellow-green, often smells, and comes with increasing swelling, warmth and tenderness. Learn more about an infected tooth extraction site and how it is treated.

Call your dentist the same day, or seek urgent care, for bleeding that will not stop with pressure, fever, swelling that spreads toward the eye or neck, trouble swallowing or breathing, or severe pain that pain medicine does not control.

What does a tooth extraction cost if healing needs extra care?

A tooth extraction itself costs $150 to $310 per tooth for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction out of network, according to Delta Dental's 2024 internal data. CareCredit's 2023–2024 cost survey reports averages of $177 for a simple extraction and $363 for a surgical one. Delta Dental's ranges reflect out-of-network claims, while CareCredit's are survey averages, so they differ slightly. All are national averages; local prices vary.

Healing problems can add costs. A dry socket visit for a medicated dressing is usually billed as a separate office visit, and antibiotics for infection add a prescription cost. If you plan to replace the tooth with an implant, a bone graft at the time of extraction can preserve the socket; CareCredit lists an allograft bone graft after extraction at an average of $811. Cleveland Clinic explains how grafts work.

Insurance helps here. Delta Dental notes that basic procedures such as extractions are often covered at 80% on a common 100-80-50 plan, as described in its guide to what dental insurance covers. For a full breakdown, see our tooth extraction cost guide.

Frequently asked questions

What does a blood clot after tooth extraction look like?

A blood clot after tooth extraction looks like a dark red or maroon, jelly-like plug filling the socket. Over the next two to three days, its surface often turns white, grey or yellowish as granulation tissue forms. A clot that disappears early, leaving an empty socket with visible bone, may mean dry socket.

Is white stuff in my tooth extraction site normal?

White stuff in a tooth extraction site is usually normal granulation tissue, which forms over the clot from about day 2 onward. Healthy granulation tissue is soft, smooth and not foul-smelling, and pain improves each day. Thick yellow-green discharge, a bad smell, fever or worsening swelling suggest infection and need a dentist's check.

How long does it take for a tooth extraction to heal?

A tooth extraction usually feels mostly healed within about one to two weeks, when the gum has largely closed. Complete healing of the bone underneath takes several months. Surgical extractions and wisdom teeth often take longer than simple extractions, and your dentist can tell you what to expect for your case.

How long does pain last after tooth extraction?

Pain after tooth extraction usually peaks in the first two to three days and then eases steadily, with most people feeling much better within a week. Pain that suddenly worsens around days 3 to 5, especially with bad breath or an empty-looking socket, may be dry socket and needs a dentist visit.

When can I stop using gauze after tooth extraction?

You can usually stop using gauze after tooth extraction once bleeding slows to light pink oozing, often within the first several hours. Bite firmly on each pad for about 30 to 60 minutes. If bleeding soaks through gauze after a few hours of steady pressure, call your dentist.

What should a tooth extraction look like after 3 days?

A tooth extraction site after 3 days typically shows a clot partly covered by white or yellowish granulation tissue, with pink gum edges that look less swollen than before. Mild tenderness is normal. An empty socket, visible bone, a foul taste or rising pain are signs to contact your dentist.
