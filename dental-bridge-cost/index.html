Dental Bridge Cost: What a Tooth Bridge Costs With and Without Insurance

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Dental bridge cost for a standard three-unit bridge (two crowns and one false tooth) averages about $3,600 out of network, according to Delta Dental. A Maryland bridge averages $1,569 in CareCredit's cost survey. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • Delta Dental puts a three-unit bridge (two crowns and a pontic) at about $3,600 out of network.
  • CareCredit's survey lists a single-tooth traditional bridge at an average of $5,197 and a single-tooth Maryland bridge at $1,569.
  • Dental insurance typically covers 50% to 80% of bridge cost, according to Delta Dental, subject to your deductible and annual maximum.
  • Bridge type, number of units, crown material and any extra work such as extractions or root canals drive the final price.
  • In-network dentists, dental schools, HSA or FSA funds and payment plans can lower what you pay.

How much does a dental bridge cost?

A dental bridge costs about $3,600 for a standard three-unit bridge billed out of network, according to Delta Dental's 2025 internal data. A three-unit bridge replaces one missing tooth using two crowns on the neighboring teeth and one false tooth, called a pontic, in between.

Dental bridge prices from a second source run higher for a traditional bridge and lower for a bonded one. The CareCredit procedure cost list, drawn from the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study for 2023–2024, puts a single-tooth traditional bridge at an average of $5,197 and a single-tooth Maryland bridge at $1,569.

The two sources measure different things. Delta Dental's number reflects out-of-network claims, meaning what dentists billed for the bridge itself. CareCredit's figure is a survey average that can capture more of the total treatment a patient finances, which helps explain why its traditional bridge figure is higher. Neither is wrong; they are two views of a market where prices vary widely.

Every figure here is a national average. Your own quote depends on your city, the dentist, the materials and whether you need any preparatory work. A written treatment plan after an exam and X-rays is the only reliable price for your situation. Dental bridge cost by type

Dental bridge cost depends heavily on the type of bridge, because each design needs a different amount of tooth preparation, lab work and chair time. Four main types are used in the United States, as outlined by Cleveland Clinic.

  • Traditional bridge: crowns on the teeth on both sides of the gap hold a pontic in place. Delta Dental's about $3,600 three-unit figure and CareCredit's $5,197 single-tooth average both describe this type.
  • Maryland bridge: a false tooth is held by metal or ceramic wings bonded to the backs of the neighboring teeth. Little or no enamel is removed, which keeps the price lower; CareCredit lists it at $1,569. See our guide to the maryland bridge dental option for when it works best.
  • Cantilever bridge: the pontic is supported by a crown on only one side. It is used when there is a healthy tooth on just one side of the gap. Our page on the cantilever bridge dental design explains its limits.
  • Implant-supported bridge: dental implants, rather than natural teeth, anchor the bridge. This is usually the most expensive option because it includes implant surgery, but it does not rely on neighboring teeth.

Longer bridges cost more. Each extra unit is another crown or pontic, so a bridge replacing two or three missing teeth, such as a 4 teeth bridge, carries a higher total. For a unit-by-unit look at the most common design, see 3 tooth bridge cost.

What affects the price of a tooth bridge?

The price of a tooth bridge is shaped mainly by the number of units, the material, the location of the gap and any extra treatment needed before the bridge goes in. Here is what tends to move a quote up or down.

  • Number of units: each crown and each pontic is billed as a unit. More missing teeth means more units and a higher price.
  • Material: porcelain fused to metal, all-ceramic and zirconia bridges have different lab fees. Tooth-colored materials for front teeth often cost more than metal-based options used further back.
  • Location: front teeth demand careful shade matching, while back teeth need stronger materials to handle chewing forces.
  • Preparatory work: extractions, root canals on supporting teeth, core build-ups, gum treatment or bone grafting all add separate fees.
  • Temporary bridge: most offices fit a temporary bridge while the lab makes the final one; some bill it separately.
  • Provider: a general dentist usually charges less than a prosthodontist, a specialist in tooth replacement who may handle complex cases.
  • Region: fees in large cities and high-cost areas tend to be higher.

Ask for an itemized estimate that lists every procedure code. Comparing line by line shows whether two offices are quoting the same treatment.

Does dental insurance cover a bridge?

Dental insurance usually covers a dental bridge as a major procedure, typically paying 50% to 80% of the cost according to Delta Dental. The exact share depends on your plan, your network and how much of your yearly benefits you have used.

Bridge coverage often comes with these limits:

  • Annual maximum: most dental plans cap yearly payouts. A multi-unit bridge can reach that cap quickly, leaving you to pay the rest.
  • Waiting periods: new policies often delay coverage for major work such as bridges and crowns.
  • Missing tooth clause: some plans will not pay to replace a tooth that was lost before your coverage started.
  • Replacement rules: plans commonly limit how often they will pay to replace an existing bridge.
  • Material downgrades: an insurer may pay only the amount for a less costly material and leave the difference to you.

Request a pre-treatment estimate before you start. Your dentist sends the treatment plan to the insurer, and the response shows what the plan will pay. If you do not have coverage, our guide to dental bridge cost without insurance lays out self-pay options in detail.

Dental bridge cost with insurance vs without insurance

Dental bridge cost with insurance is usually much lower than without it, because plans typically pay 50% to 80% of the cost, according to Delta Dental. Without insurance, you pay the full fee, which is why the about $3,600 out-of-network average for a three-unit bridge is a useful self-pay benchmark.

Dental bridge cost with insurance depends on more than the coverage percentage. Your deductible comes off first, and the plan stops paying once it reaches its annual maximum. On a long bridge, the annual maximum often matters more than the coverage share. In-network dentists also bill a lower negotiated fee, so your share is calculated on a smaller total than an out-of-network quote.

Dental bridge cost without insurance can still be managed. Ask for the office's self-pay fee, which is sometimes lower than its standard fee, and ask whether the price includes the exam, X-rays, temporary bridge and follow-up adjustments. Discount dental plans, which are not insurance, give members reduced fees at participating dentists for an annual membership fee and have no waiting periods; compare the savings against the membership cost before joining.

Dental bridge quotes are easiest to compare when you know exactly what each one covers. Bring each written estimate to your consultation and ask the dentist to walk through any line you do not understand, especially charges for build-ups, root canals or extractions on the supporting teeth.

How to pay less for a dental bridge

The most reliable ways to pay less for a dental bridge are choosing an in-network dentist, getting treatment at a dental school, and using pre-tax HSA or FSA money. A few other steps can help as well.

  1. Use an in-network dentist. In-network fees are negotiated by your insurer, so the total is usually lower than out-of-network averages and your share shrinks with it.
  2. Check dental schools. Students supervised by licensed faculty often provide bridges at reduced fees. Expect longer and more frequent appointments.
  3. Pay with HSA or FSA funds. A bridge is a qualified dental expense, so these accounts let you pay with pre-tax dollars.
  4. Ask about payment plans. Many dental offices offer monthly plans or accept medical credit cards. Check whether interest is deferred, because unpaid balances can be charged back interest.
  5. Ask for a cash discount. Some practices lower the fee for patients paying in full without insurance.
  6. Compare bridge types. If your bite and teeth allow, a Maryland bridge can cost far less than a traditional one. Ask your dentist whether it is a sound option for your gap.
  7. Split treatment across benefit years. If you need preparatory work, your dentist may schedule it so it falls into two plan years, using two annual maximums.

Choosing the cheapest bridge is not always the cheapest outcome. A bridge that fails early costs more to replace than a well-made bridge placed on healthy supporting teeth.

Is a bridge cheaper than a dental implant?

A dental bridge often costs less up front than a single dental implant with a crown, and it is usually finished faster because no implant surgery or bone healing time is needed. The comparison is closer when you consider long-term upkeep.

Dental bridges rely on the neighboring teeth, which are usually reshaped for crowns. Those supporting teeth carry extra load and can develop decay at the crown edges, so future repairs are possible. An implant replaces the root and leaves neighboring teeth untouched, but it involves surgery, healing time and a higher initial fee.

Bridge or implant is a clinical decision as much as a financial one. Bone volume, gum health, the condition of the neighboring teeth and your overall health all matter. Healthline summarizes the trade-offs, and your dentist can tell you which fits your mouth and budget.

What does getting a dental bridge involve?

Getting a dental bridge usually takes two main appointments a couple of weeks apart: one to prepare the teeth and take impressions, and one to fit and cement the final bridge. Extra visits are needed if you need extractions, root canals or gum treatment first.

  1. Exam and planning: your dentist checks the gap, takes X-rays and confirms the supporting teeth are healthy.
  2. Tooth preparation: for a traditional bridge, the teeth beside the gap are numbed and reshaped to make room for crowns. A Maryland bridge needs far less preparation.
  3. Impressions or digital scan: a model of your teeth is sent to a dental lab.
  4. Temporary bridge: a temporary protects the prepared teeth while the lab makes the final bridge.
  5. Fitting: the final bridge is checked for fit, bite and color, then cemented or bonded in place.

Mild sensitivity and gum soreness for a few days are common after preparation. To see how results typically look, browse our before and after dental bridge examples.

Contact your dentist promptly if a bridge feels loose, your bite feels high after a few days, or you have severe pain, swelling, fever or a bad taste around a supporting tooth. These can signal a problem that is cheaper to fix early.

How long does a dental bridge last and what does upkeep cost?

A dental bridge can last many years with good home care and regular checkups, and the main ongoing costs are routine cleanings and any repairs to the supporting teeth. How long yours lasts depends on your oral hygiene, bite forces, habits such as grinding and the health of the anchor teeth.

Dental bridge care at home centers on cleaning under the pontic, where food and plaque collect. Floss threaders, water flossers and small interdental brushes help reach that space. Decay or gum disease around a supporting tooth is the most common reason a bridge has to be replaced.

Bridge upkeep also includes protecting the restoration. If you grind your teeth, a night guard can reduce wear on the crowns. Avoid chewing ice or hard objects on the bridge. Keeping up with checkups lets your dentist spot small problems, such as a loose crown margin, before they turn into a full replacement.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a bridge for teeth?

A bridge for teeth costs about $3,600 for a standard three-unit bridge billed out of network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit's survey puts a single-tooth traditional bridge at $5,197 and a single-tooth Maryland bridge at $1,569. These are national averages, and your price depends on location, materials and extra treatment.

How much does a 4 teeth bridge cost?

A 4 teeth bridge costs more than a three-unit bridge because each extra crown or false tooth is billed as another unit. National averages for four-unit bridges are not in our verified sources, so ask your dentist for an itemized estimate listing every unit and any preparatory work such as extractions.

Does insurance pay for a dental bridge?

Insurance usually pays for a dental bridge as a major service, typically covering 50% to 80% of the cost, according to Delta Dental. Waiting periods, annual maximums and missing tooth clauses can reduce what the plan pays, so ask your dentist to request a pre-treatment estimate first.

Why is a Maryland bridge cheaper?

A Maryland bridge is cheaper because it bonds a false tooth to the backs of neighboring teeth with small wings instead of placing full crowns. Less tooth preparation and lab work keeps costs down; CareCredit lists the average at $1,569. It works best for front teeth with healthy neighbors and light bite forces.

Is a bridge cheaper than an implant?

A bridge is often cheaper up front than a single implant with a crown, and treatment is faster because no surgery or bone healing is needed. Over time, repairs to the supporting teeth can narrow the gap. Your dentist can compare both options for your teeth, bone and budget.

Can I use my HSA or FSA for a dental bridge?

You can usually use an HSA or FSA for a dental bridge because it is a qualified dental expense. Paying with these accounts means using pre-tax money, which lowers your effective cost. Check your plan's rules and keep the itemized receipt from your dentist for your records.
