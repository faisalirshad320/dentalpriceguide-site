Dental Bridge vs Implant: Which Is Better for Replacing a Missing Tooth?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated November 1, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

A dental bridge replaces a missing tooth by anchoring a false tooth to crowns on the neighbouring teeth, while a dental implant replaces the root with a post in the jawbone and needs no support from other teeth. Bridges are usually faster and cheaper upfront; implants usually last longer and protect bone. A single implant costs $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits (Delta Dental).

Key takeaways
  • Bridges rely on neighbouring teeth; implants stand alone in the jawbone.
  • Bridges are usually quicker and less expensive upfront.
  • Implants usually last longer and help preserve jawbone.
  • Implants may need a bone graft first if bone is thin.
  • Insurance often covers bridges; implant coverage varies by plan.

How do a bridge and an implant work?

A traditional dental bridge works by filling the gap with a false tooth, called a pontic, that is attached to crowns on the teeth either side. Those neighbouring teeth, called abutment teeth, are filed down so the crowns fit over them, and the whole unit is cemented in place.

A dental implant works by replacing the missing tooth's root. A titanium or zirconia post is placed in the jawbone, the bone bonds to it over several months, and then an abutment and crown are attached. The implant does not need any support from the neighbouring teeth. Healthline's implant versus bridge guide explains both in detail.

Is a bridge cheaper than an implant?

A bridge is usually cheaper than an implant upfront for replacing a single tooth. Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits, and CareCredit reports a $2,143 average for a single full-size implant and $4,344 for a standard endosteal implant per tooth.

The long-term comparison is closer. Bridges commonly need replacing after 10 to 15 years, and if an abutment tooth fails, the bridge may need to be redone with additional treatment. A well-maintained implant can last decades. Implants may also need a bone graft first, which CareCredit averages at $811 for an allograft and $2,779 for an autograft, plus a cone-beam CT scan averaging $466.

How do bridges and implants affect neighbouring teeth?

Bridges and implants affect neighbouring teeth very differently. A traditional bridge requires reshaping the teeth on either side of the gap to hold crowns, which removes healthy enamel permanently. If those teeth already have large fillings or need crowns, this may not be a drawback; if they are healthy and untouched, it is a significant trade-off.

An implant leaves the neighbouring teeth alone. It is also easier to clean between an implant and its neighbours with normal floss, while bridges require floss threaders or special brushes to clean under the false tooth.

Which is better for bone health?

An implant is better for bone health because it replaces the tooth root and transmits chewing forces to the jawbone, which helps maintain it. After a tooth is lost, the bone in that area naturally shrinks over time.

A bridge sits above the gum and does not stimulate the bone under the false tooth, so some bone loss continues beneath it. Over many years, this can create a visible dip under the pontic. The Cleveland Clinic's bone graft guide explains how bone loss affects future implant options.

Which takes longer, a bridge or an implant?

A bridge takes much less time than an implant. A bridge is typically completed in two visits over a few weeks: one to prepare the teeth and take impressions, and one to fit the final bridge.

An implant usually takes several months from start to finish. After placement, the implant needs time to bond with the bone before the crown is attached. If a bone graft is needed first, the timeline is longer. A temporary tooth can usually be worn during healing so you are not left with a visible gap.

Who should choose a bridge, and who an implant?

A bridge may be the better choice if the neighbouring teeth already need crowns, if you cannot have surgery for medical reasons, if bone is insufficient and you do not want grafting, or if you need a faster result. Bridges are also widely covered by insurance.

An implant may be the better choice if the neighbouring teeth are healthy, you want the longest-lasting option, you want to preserve bone, and you are healthy enough for minor surgery. Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes and some medications raise implant risk. The Cleveland Clinic's prosthodontics guide describes how specialists plan these decisions.

Does insurance cover bridges and implants equally?

Insurance often covers bridges more consistently than implants. Many plans treat both as major services, which Delta Dental describes as typically covered at 50% up to the annual maximum, using $1,500 as an example, in its coverage guide.

Some plans exclude implants or pay only what a bridge would cost, leaving you to pay the difference. Request a pre-treatment estimate for both options. See what insurance covers for dental implants.

Are there other options besides a bridge or an implant?

Other options besides a bridge or an implant include a resin-bonded (Maryland) bridge, which uses small wings bonded to the backs of neighbouring teeth with less tooth removal, and removable partial dentures. Leaving a back-tooth gap empty is occasionally acceptable with monitoring.

For multiple missing teeth, implant-supported bridges or dentures may be options. Healthline's dentures versus implants guide compares removable options. See dental implant cost, types of dental implants and the cost of full mouth dental implants.

How do you care for a bridge compared with an implant?

Caring for a bridge and an implant both rely on daily brushing and cleaning, but the techniques differ. With a bridge, plaque collects under the false tooth and around the crowned supporting teeth. You need a floss threader, a special floss with a stiff end, a water flosser or small interdental brushes to clean under the pontic every day. Decay on a supporting tooth is one of the most common reasons bridges fail.

An implant can be cleaned much like a natural tooth, with brushing and regular floss between the implant crown and its neighbours. Implants cannot get cavities, but the gum and bone around them can become inflamed and infected if plaque builds up, a condition called peri-implantitis.

Both need regular dental check-ups and cleanings. If you grind your teeth, a night guard protects both bridges and implant crowns from chipping and excessive stress.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bridge or implant better?

Neither is better for everyone. Implants usually last longer, protect bone and leave neighbouring teeth untouched. Bridges are faster, cost less upfront and avoid surgery, and make sense when the neighbouring teeth already need crowns. Your dentist can advise based on your teeth and health.

Is a dental bridge cheaper than an implant?

A dental bridge is usually cheaper upfront than an implant. Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits. Over many years, bridge replacements and repairs can narrow the difference.

How long does a bridge last compared with an implant?

A dental bridge commonly lasts around 10 to 15 years, while a well-maintained implant can last decades, though its crown may need replacing. The health of the supporting teeth largely determines how long a bridge lasts.

Can you get an implant after having a bridge?

You can often get an implant after having a bridge, but bone may have shrunk under the false tooth over time, which might require a bone graft. The former supporting teeth may also need new crowns.

Does insurance cover a bridge or an implant?

Many dental plans cover bridges as major services, often at about 50%. Implant coverage varies more; some plans exclude implants or pay only what a bridge would cost. A pre-treatment estimate shows your coverage for each option.
