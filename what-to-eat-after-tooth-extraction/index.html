What to Eat After Tooth Extraction: Soft Foods, Timing and When Solids Return

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

What to eat after tooth extraction is cool liquids and soft, no-chew foods for the first 24 hours, such as yogurt, applesauce and lukewarm soup. Move to mashed and tender foods over the next few days, and return to most solid foods after about a week, as Cleveland Clinic describes. Your dentist's instructions come first.

Key takeaways
  • Eat only cool liquids and very soft foods on day one, and chew on the opposite side.
  • Soft foods like eggs, pasta and mashed vegetables usually return by day three.
  • Most people can eat normal solid food after about one week, longer after surgical or wisdom tooth removal.
  • Skip straws, crunchy foods, spicy food, alcohol and very hot drinks while the socket heals.
  • Your own dentist's or oral surgeon's instructions always take priority over general guidance.

What can I eat after tooth extraction on the first day?

On the first day after a tooth extraction you can eat cool or lukewarm liquids and very soft foods that need no chewing. The goal on day one is to protect the blood clot forming in the socket, because that clot is the foundation of healing.

Wait until the numbness wears off before eating, usually a few hours, so you do not bite your cheek, lip or tongue. Keep the gauze in place as your dentist instructed, and take your first food only once the bleeding has slowed to light oozing.

Good first-day choices include:

  • Yogurt, pudding and smooth cottage cheese
  • Applesauce and mashed banana
  • Smoothies and protein shakes eaten with a spoon, without seeds
  • Lukewarm broth or blended soups, not hot
  • Jell-O and ice cream without nuts, candy pieces or a cone
  • Meal-replacement drinks if you cannot manage food

Drink plenty of water, but sip from a glass. The suction from a straw can pull the clot out of the socket and lead to dry socket, a painful complication that Cleveland Clinic describes in detail.

What can I eat 3 days and 7 days after tooth extraction?

Three days after a tooth extraction most people can eat soft foods that need only gentle chewing, and by seven days most can add tender solid foods back in. Healing speed varies with the tooth, how difficult the removal was, and your general health.

From about day two or three, try soft foods such as:

  • Scrambled eggs and omelets
  • Mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes and well-cooked squash
  • Soft pasta, mac and cheese, and soft rice
  • Oatmeal and cream of wheat, cooled to warm
  • Flaky fish, soft tofu and refried beans
  • Avocado, hummus and soft-cooked vegetables
  • Soft bread without a hard crust

Around day seven, many people can return to tender meats, soft sandwiches and cooked vegetables, still chewing on the other side. Keep avoiding chips, nuts, seeds, popcorn and very chewy foods until the socket has closed over, since sharp or small pieces can lodge in the healing area.

After a surgical extraction, wisdom tooth removal or a bone graft, the soft-food phase usually lasts longer. Your surgeon may ask you to stay on soft foods for a week or more. You can compare what you see with our guide to a normal socket after tooth extraction.

When can I eat solid food after tooth extraction?

Most people can eat solid food again about one week after a simple tooth extraction, and closer to two weeks after a surgical extraction. The safe point is when you can chew without pain and the gum over the socket has started to close.

Bring solid food back gradually. Start with tender foods cut into small pieces, chew on the opposite side, and stop if chewing causes sharp pain. Hard, crunchy and very chewy foods, such as nuts, crusty bread, steak and hard candy, are usually the last to return.

Rinse gently after meals once your dentist allows it, often from the day after the extraction, using warm salt water. Rinsing keeps food out of the socket without the force of vigorous swishing.

Call your dentist promptly if you have bleeding that will not stop with firm gauze pressure, pain that gets worse after day three, swelling that increases after the first two or three days, fever, pus or a bad taste from the socket. Seek emergency care for trouble swallowing or breathing. These can be signs of dry socket or an infected tooth extraction site.

Soft foods to eat after tooth extraction: a meal list

Soft foods to eat after tooth extraction should be easy to swallow, low in small hard pieces, and filling enough to keep your energy up while you heal. Protein matters, because the body uses it to repair tissue, so build meals around eggs, dairy, fish, beans or protein shakes where you can.

  • Breakfast: scrambled eggs, smooth yogurt, oatmeal cooled to warm, cottage cheese, mashed banana, a spoonable smoothie without seeds.
  • Lunch: blended vegetable soup, hummus with soft pita torn small, egg salad on soft bread, avocado mash, refried beans.
  • Dinner: mashed potatoes, soft polenta, flaky baked fish, meatloaf, soft pasta with mild sauce, well-cooked carrots or squash.
  • Snacks: pudding, Jell-O, applesauce, ripe pears, soft cheese, ice cream without mix-ins.

Prepare a few of these before the appointment so you are not cooking while sore. Keep everything warm rather than hot for the first couple of days, and take small bites using the side away from the extraction.

What foods and drinks should you avoid after tooth extraction?

After a tooth extraction, avoid foods and habits that can disturb the clot, irritate the socket or leave debris behind. Most of these limits apply for the first several days, and some for a week or longer.

  • Straws and spitting. Suction can dislodge the clot.
  • Hot drinks and hot food on the first day, since heat can restart bleeding.
  • Crunchy and hard foods such as chips, nuts, popcorn, pretzels and crusty bread.
  • Small seeds and grains like sesame seeds, berries with seeds and quinoa, which can lodge in the socket.
  • Spicy and acidic foods, including citrus and tomato sauce, which can sting.
  • Alcohol, which slows healing and can interact with pain medicine.
  • Carbonated drinks in the first day or two.

Tobacco is the biggest risk. Smoking and vaping combine suction with chemicals that slow healing, and they raise the chance of dry socket. Our page on smoking after tooth extraction covers how long to wait.

Some dentists also suggest avoiding dairy on the first day. That advice is mostly about nausea after sedation or strong pain medicine, not about harming the socket. If you had only local anesthesia and feel fine, plain yogurt or ice cream is usually acceptable. Follow your own dentist's instructions.

What does it cost if healing goes wrong after an extraction?

A tooth extraction itself usually costs $150 to $310 for a simple extraction or $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction, according to Delta Dental out-of-network data. CareCredit reports averages of $177 for a simple extraction and $363 for a surgical one. The two sources measure different things, insurer claims versus a patient cost survey, so the figures differ, and local prices vary.

Eating carefully helps you avoid extra bills. Dry socket or an infection means return visits for cleaning the socket, medicated dressings or antibiotics, and those visits add to what you paid for the extraction.

If your dentist placed a bone graft to preserve the site for a future implant, CareCredit lists an allograft bone graft at an average of $811. A graft site needs extra care with food, so follow your surgeon's diet instructions closely. Cleveland Clinic explains what bone graft recovery involves.

Insurance helps. Delta Dental notes that basic procedures such as extractions are typically covered at 80% on a 100-80-50 plan. Our full guide to tooth extraction cost breaks down fees by tooth type and insurance status.

Frequently asked questions

How long after tooth extraction can I eat?

You can usually eat once the numbness wears off and the bleeding has slowed, often a few hours after a tooth extraction. Start with cool liquids and very soft foods such as yogurt, applesauce or lukewarm soup, chew on the other side, and avoid hot food on the first day. Follow your dentist's specific timing.

Can I eat ice cream after tooth extraction?

Ice cream is usually fine after a tooth extraction, and the cold can feel soothing. Choose a smooth flavor without nuts, chocolate chips or candy pieces, eat it from a bowl with a spoon rather than a cone, and never use a straw for milkshakes. Skip it if your dentist has advised against dairy.

Why no dairy after tooth extraction?

Some dentists advise skipping dairy for the first day after a tooth extraction mainly because milk products can worsen nausea after sedation or strong pain medicine. There is little evidence that dairy harms the socket itself. If you had only local anesthesia and feel well, soft dairy is usually fine unless your dentist says otherwise.

What can I eat 3 days after tooth extraction?

Three days after a tooth extraction most people can eat soft foods that need light chewing, such as scrambled eggs, mashed potatoes, soft pasta, oatmeal, flaky fish and soft-cooked vegetables. Keep chewing on the opposite side and continue avoiding straws, crunchy snacks, seeds and spicy foods until the socket has healed further.

What can I eat 7 days after tooth extraction?

Seven days after a simple tooth extraction many people can return to most normal foods, including tender meats, sandwiches and cooked vegetables, cut into small pieces. Hard, crunchy and very chewy foods should be added back last. After surgical or wisdom tooth removal, the soft-food phase often lasts longer, so follow your surgeon's advice.

Can I eat on the side of my tooth extraction?

Avoid chewing on the side of your tooth extraction for at least the first several days. Chewing there can push food into the socket and disturb the blood clot. Once the area is no longer tender, usually after about a week for a simple extraction, you can gradually use that side again with softer foods first.
