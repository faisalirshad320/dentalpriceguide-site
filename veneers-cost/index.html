Veneers Cost in 2026: Price Per Tooth, Full Sets and How to Pay Less

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Veneers cost $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth for porcelain laminate veneers without insurance, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit puts a single porcelain veneer at $1,765 on average and a full set of six to eight at $15,486. These are national averages; local prices vary.

  • Porcelain veneers run $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, based on Delta Dental's 2024 data.
  • A full set of six to eight porcelain veneers averages $15,486 in CareCredit's cost study.

How much do veneers cost per tooth?

Porcelain veneers cost $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, according to Delta Dental's 2024 internal data. That range covers a porcelain laminate veneer, the thin ceramic shell most people picture when they hear the word veneer.

CareCredit's cost study lands in the middle of that range. CareCredit reports a national average of $1,765 for a single porcelain veneer, based on the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study for 2023–2024.

The price of one veneer usually bundles several steps: the consultation and shade planning, light reshaping of the tooth, impressions or a digital scan, a temporary veneer in some offices, lab fabrication, and the final bonding visit. Ask whether your quote includes all of these, because some offices list the exam, X-rays or a trial smile separately.

A single veneer is a common request when one front tooth is chipped, discolored or slightly out of line with its neighbors. Matching one veneer to natural teeth is skilled work, and dentists sometimes charge toward the upper end of the range for a single-tooth case because the shade has to blend perfectly with untreated teeth on either side. If you are still deciding whether a veneer is the right fix, our guide to what are veneers explains how they work and who they suit.

How much is a full set of veneers?

A full set of porcelain veneers costs $15,486 on average for six to eight teeth, according to CareCredit's cost study. Those six to eight teeth are the ones that show when you smile, usually the upper front teeth from canine to canine and sometimes the first premolars.

Multiplying the per-tooth range by the number of teeth gives a rough check, but full-set pricing does not always work that way. Some dentists offer a lower per-tooth rate for larger cases, while others charge more because a full smile makeover takes extra planning, a diagnostic wax-up and more chair time.

Many people ask whether they need veneers on the lower teeth too. A full set for both arches is less common, because the lower front teeth are often hidden by the upper lip when you talk and smile. Your dentist can show you which teeth are visible in your own smile line, which is the main factor in how many veneers make sense.

Seeing real results helps set expectations before you commit to a large case. Browse our veneers before and after page to understand what a six-tooth case looks like compared with an eight-tooth or ten-tooth case.

Why do veneer price figures differ between sources?

Veneer price figures differ between sources because each publisher measures something slightly different. Delta Dental's range of $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth comes from the insurer's own internal claims data for porcelain laminate veneers billed without insurance. CareCredit's $1,765 average comes from a separate cost survey run for its lender, Synchrony.

Both figures are national averages, and both are useful. The Delta Dental range shows the spread you are likely to see in quotes, from lower-cost areas to expensive city practices. The CareCredit average gives a single midpoint figure for budgeting.

Neither source is a price list for your local dentist. Veneer fees in large coastal cities often sit above the national average, while fees in smaller towns and lower-cost states often sit below it. The only number that matters for your budget is the written treatment plan from the office you choose.

Tip: When you collect quotes, ask each office for the procedure codes and the number of teeth included. Comparing per-tooth prices only works when every quote covers the same steps.

What affects the cost of veneers?

The cost of veneers depends mainly on the material, the number of teeth, the dentist's experience and your location. Several other factors can push a quote up or down:

  • Material: porcelain is the most expensive option; composite resin costs less per tooth.
  • Number of teeth: one veneer costs less overall than a full set, though large cases can lower the per-tooth rate.
  • Dentist and lab: cosmetic dentists with advanced training and premium dental labs usually charge more.
  • Location: fees track local rents, wages and demand, so city practices tend to quote higher.
  • Preparation work: treating decay, gum disease or an old filling before veneers adds separate fees.
  • Planning extras: digital smile design, a diagnostic wax-up or a trial smile may be billed separately.
  • Sedation: nitrous oxide or oral sedation, if you choose it, is usually an added charge.

Teeth that are badly broken, heavily filled or very crooked may not suit veneers at all. In those cases, a crown or orthodontic treatment can be the better plan, and the cost comparison changes. Our guide on veneers vs crowns walks through when each option fits.

Porcelain vs composite vs pop-on veneers: which costs less?

Porcelain veneers cost the most, composite veneers cost less, and removable pop-on veneers are usually the cheapest option. Each type trades price against durability and appearance.

Porcelain veneers are made in a dental lab from thin ceramic. Porcelain resists staining, reflects light much like natural enamel and usually lasts the longest, which is why porcelain is the benchmark behind the $1,180 to $2,185 per-tooth range from Delta Dental. Most porcelain veneers need a small amount of enamel removed, so the decision is not easily reversed.

Composite veneers are built directly on the tooth from tooth-colored resin, often in a single visit. Composite costs less per tooth and is easier to repair, but the resin can stain and chip sooner than porcelain. See our breakdown of composite veneers cost for the full comparison.

Pop-on veneers are removable cosmetic covers that clip over your natural teeth. Pop-on veneers are not a dental treatment in the usual sense, and quality varies widely between products. If you are curious about how much are pop on veneers, our guide covers the price range and the limits of these devices.

Dental bonding sits close to composite veneers. Bonding uses the same resin material to repair a chip or close a small gap, and CareCredit lists an average of $431 per tooth. Read teeth bonding vs veneers to see which problems bonding can fix on its own.

Does dental insurance cover veneers?

Dental insurance usually does not cover veneers, because insurers treat veneers as a cosmetic procedure. Delta Dental notes that veneers are usually treated as cosmetic and often not covered.

Exceptions do happen. When a veneer restores a tooth damaged by trauma or a structural defect, some plans will review the claim and may pay part of the fee, often under major restorative benefits. Coverage in those cases depends on your specific plan, your annual maximum and how the dentist documents the need.

Insurance can still help with the work around veneers. Exams, X-rays, cleanings and fillings needed to get your mouth healthy before veneers are often covered under preventive and basic benefits, which lowers your total bill even when the veneers themselves are not covered.

Ask your dentist to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer before you start. A pre-treatment estimate shows in writing what the plan will and will not pay. For a full walk-through of the claim process, read how much do veneers cost with insurance.

How can I pay less for veneers?

The most reliable ways to pay less for veneers are comparing quotes, choosing fewer teeth or a lower-cost material, and spreading payments over time. Small decisions add up on a case that can reach $15,486 for a full set.

  1. Get two or three written quotes. Compare the same number of teeth, the same material and the same included steps.
  2. Treat only the teeth that show. Your smile line decides how many veneers you need; more is not always better.
  3. Consider composite or bonding for minor fixes. A small chip may need $431 of bonding (CareCredit average) rather than a porcelain veneer.
  4. Look at dental schools. University dental clinics often charge less because supervised students do the work, though appointments take longer.
  5. Use in-network dentists. Even when veneers are not covered, an in-network office applies discounted fees to the exams, X-rays and fillings you need first.
  6. Ask about payment plans. Many offices offer in-house plans or third-party dental financing; check the interest rate and any deferred-interest terms carefully.
  7. Check HSA and FSA rules. Purely cosmetic veneers generally do not qualify, but a veneer that restores a damaged tooth may; ask your plan administrator before you pay.

Be cautious with very low advertised prices. A deal that skips the exam, uses an unnamed lab or rushes preparation can lead to veneers that chip, stain at the edges or irritate the gums, and replacing failed veneers costs more than doing them well once.

What does getting veneers involve?

Getting porcelain veneers usually takes two or three visits over a few weeks: a consultation, a preparation appointment and a bonding appointment. Composite veneers can often be done in a single longer visit.

  1. Consultation and planning. The dentist examines your teeth and gums, takes X-rays and photos, and discusses the shape and shade you want. Some offices create a mock-up so you can preview the result.
  2. Tooth preparation. The dentist numbs the area and removes a thin layer of enamel from the front of each tooth so the veneer sits flush. A scan or impression goes to the dental lab, and you may wear temporary veneers.
  3. Bonding. The dentist checks the fit and color, cleans and etches the tooth, and bonds each veneer in place with dental cement hardened by a curing light. Final adjustments to your bite follow.

Mild tooth sensitivity to hot and cold is common for a few days after preparation and bonding. Follow your dentist's instructions on eating and cleaning. Call your dentist promptly if you notice swelling, fever, severe pain or a veneer that feels loose or catches your tongue.

The Healthline guide to dental veneers and the Cleveland Clinic overview of cosmetic dentistry both cover the procedure and its risks in more detail.

Are veneers worth the cost over time?

Veneers can be worth the cost when you want a lasting change to the color or shape of several front teeth, but the true cost includes eventual replacement. Porcelain veneers are durable, yet they are not permanent, and most people will need to replace them at some point.

Veneer lifespan depends on the material, your bite, grinding habits and how well you care for your gums. A night guard, regular cleanings and avoiding habits like biting nails, ice or pens all help. Our guide to are veneers permanent explains typical lifespans for porcelain and composite.

Because porcelain veneers usually require enamel removal, the treated teeth will always need some kind of covering afterward. Factor future replacement into your decision, especially if you are young and may need several sets over a lifetime.

Veneers also only replace the front surface of a tooth. A missing tooth needs a different solution, such as an implant or a bridge. If you are weighing cosmetic work against tooth replacement, see veneers vs implants to understand where each option fits and why the costs are not directly comparable.

Before committing to a full set, ask your dentist whether whitening, bonding or orthodontic treatment could achieve a similar result with less enamel removal and a lower lifetime cost.

Frequently asked questions

How much is one veneer?

One porcelain veneer costs $1,180 to $2,185 without insurance, according to Delta Dental's 2024 data, and CareCredit reports a national average of $1,765. Prices vary by city, dentist and lab. A single veneer can cost toward the upper end because matching one tooth to natural neighbors takes careful shade work.

How much is a full set of veneers?

A full set of six to eight porcelain veneers averages $15,486, according to CareCredit's Synchrony cost study for 2023–2024. Your own price depends on how many teeth show in your smile, the material, your dentist's fees and your region. Some offices lower the per-tooth rate for larger cases.

Are veneers expensive?

Veneers are one of the more expensive cosmetic dental treatments, especially porcelain, which runs $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance according to Delta Dental. Insurance rarely helps because veneers are usually considered cosmetic. Composite veneers and bonding cost less but do not last as long as porcelain.

Does insurance pay for veneers?

Insurance usually does not pay for veneers because Delta Dental and most insurers treat them as cosmetic. A plan may review a claim when a veneer restores a tooth damaged by injury or a defect. Ask your dentist for a pre-treatment estimate so you know your share in writing before you start.

Can I use my HSA or FSA for veneers?

HSA and FSA funds generally cannot be used for purely cosmetic veneers. When a veneer restores a damaged tooth for a dental reason, the expense may qualify. Check with your plan administrator and keep your dentist's documentation before paying, because eligibility depends on why the treatment is needed.

Is bonding cheaper than veneers?

Bonding is much cheaper than porcelain veneers. CareCredit lists an average of $431 per tooth for dental bonding, compared with a $1,765 average for a single porcelain veneer. Bonding suits small chips and gaps, but the resin stains and wears faster, so it may need touch-ups sooner.
