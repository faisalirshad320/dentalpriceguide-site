Does Everyone Have Wisdom Teeth? Why We Have Them, How Many and Where They Grow

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

Not everyone has wisdom teeth. Most adults develop up to four wisdom teeth, one in each back corner of the mouth, but some people are born with fewer or none, and others have wisdom teeth that stay hidden in the jawbone. Cleveland Clinic explains that an X-ray is the only way to know for sure.

Key takeaways
  • Most people have up to four wisdom teeth, but some have fewer, none, or rarely an extra one.
  • Wisdom teeth are third molars at the very back of the mouth and usually come in between the late teens and mid-twenties.
  • They are leftovers from ancestors with larger jaws and a coarser diet that wore teeth down.
  • A dental X-ray shows whether you have wisdom teeth, even if none are visible.
  • Removal costs vary widely; Delta Dental puts four surgical removals with sedation at $3,120 out-of-network.

Does everyone get wisdom teeth?

Not everyone gets wisdom teeth. Many people develop all four, but it is common to have only one, two or three, and some people never form any at all. Missing wisdom teeth are a normal variation, not a health problem.

Being born without wisdom teeth is called third molar agenesis. The tooth buds simply never form. Genetics plays a large part, so if a parent is missing wisdom teeth, their children are more likely to be missing some too.

Many people who think they have no wisdom teeth actually do. The teeth can sit fully inside the jawbone or under the gum and never break through. These are called impacted wisdom teeth, and Cleveland Clinic notes they can cause problems such as pain, infection or damage to the neighboring molar even when they cannot be seen.

A panoramic X-ray answers the question for certain. Dentists often take one in the mid-teens, when the wisdom teeth are developing, to check how many are present and whether they have room to come in.

Why do we have wisdom teeth?

We have wisdom teeth because our ancestors needed extra molars to chew a rough, uncooked diet with larger jaws that had room for them. Coarse plants, raw meat and grit wore teeth down quickly, so a third set of molars arriving in early adulthood gave more grinding surface when it was needed most.

Human diets changed with cooking, farming and food processing. Softer food means less chewing force and less tooth wear, and over many generations human jaws have become smaller on average. The genes that build wisdom teeth have not disappeared at the same pace, so many people now have more teeth than their jaws can comfortably hold.

That mismatch is why wisdom teeth so often come in crooked, partly erupted or not at all. It is also why removal is one of the most common oral surgery procedures. Not every wisdom tooth needs to come out, though. A wisdom tooth that is fully erupted, upright, cleanable and healthy can stay. Your dentist or oral surgeon will weigh your X-rays and symptoms, and their advice for your mouth takes priority over general rules.

How many wisdom teeth can you have?

Most people can have up to four wisdom teeth, one in each quarter of the mouth: upper left, upper right, lower left and lower right. Having fewer than four is common.

Rarely, a person develops more than four. Extra teeth are called supernumerary teeth, and an extra molar behind or beside a wisdom tooth is sometimes called a fourth molar. These extra teeth are uncommon and are often found by chance on an X-ray.

The number you have affects treatment. Someone with two impacted lower wisdom teeth faces a smaller procedure than someone with four, and the bill reflects that. If you are unsure how many you have, ask your dentist to show you on your X-ray.

Upper and lower wisdom teeth also behave differently. Lower wisdom teeth are more often impacted because the lower jaw has less space behind the second molar and the bone is denser. Upper wisdom teeth more often erupt fully, though they can tilt outward toward the cheek and rub against it. Having all four does not automatically mean all four need treatment; each tooth is judged on its own position and health.

Where are wisdom teeth located and what do they look like?

Wisdom teeth are located at the very back of the mouth, behind the second molars, at the end of each row of teeth in the upper and lower jaw. They are the third molars, which is the formal dental name.

A fully erupted wisdom tooth looks like the molars in front of it: a broad, flat chewing surface with several cusps. Wisdom teeth are often a little smaller and more irregular in shape, and their roots can be fused together, curved or hooked. Those unusual roots are one reason removal is sometimes harder than a typical extraction.

A wisdom tooth coming in may look like a small white point or ridge behind your last molar, sometimes with a flap of gum over part of it. That flap can trap food and bacteria and become sore and swollen. If you have wisdom teeth pain, a bad taste, or swelling at the back of the jaw, see a dentist.

Seek urgent care for swelling of the face or jaw, fever, trouble opening your mouth, or difficulty swallowing or breathing. These can be signs that an infection around a wisdom tooth is spreading.

Why are they called wisdom teeth and when do they come in?

Wisdom teeth are called wisdom teeth because they arrive much later than other teeth, in the late teens or early twenties, when a person is older and supposedly wiser. The name dates back centuries and comes from a similar Latin term.

The tooth buds usually start forming in late childhood, and the crowns develop through the early teens. Eruption, when the tooth breaks through the gum, typically happens between about 17 and 25, though some never erupt and some arrive later. Roots keep developing into the early twenties.

Timing is one reason dentists often recommend evaluating wisdom teeth in the late teens. Roots are shorter and the surrounding bone is softer at that age, which can make removal and recovery easier when removal is needed. What does it cost if your wisdom teeth need to come out?

Wisdom teeth removal costs depend on how many teeth are removed, whether they are impacted, and the type of anesthesia. Delta Dental reports out-of-network averages of $720 for four fully erupted teeth removed non-surgically, $550 per tooth for one surgical removal, and $3,120 for all four surgically with sedation or anesthesia.

CareCredit reports an average of $835 for a single impacted wisdom tooth and $3,340 for four impacted wisdom teeth. Delta Dental's figures come from insurance claims data, while CareCredit's come from a patient cost survey, so the two measure different things. Both are national averages, and local prices vary.

Delta Dental notes that insurance typically covers about 50% to 80% of the total cost. Sedation, the surgeon's fee and follow-up for complications such as dry socket can change the final bill. Frequently asked questions

Can you be born without wisdom teeth?

Yes, you can be born without wisdom teeth. The condition is called third molar agenesis, and it means the tooth buds never formed. It often runs in families and causes no health problems. A dental X-ray can confirm whether wisdom teeth are missing or simply hidden in the jawbone.

How many wisdom teeth does a person have?

A person usually has up to four wisdom teeth, one at the back of each side of the upper and lower jaw. Many people have fewer than four, some have none, and a small number develop an extra molar beyond the usual four. Your dentist can count them on a panoramic X-ray.

Do some people not have wisdom teeth?

Some people do not have wisdom teeth at all, and many others have only one, two or three. Missing wisdom teeth are a normal genetic variation. Other people believe they have none because the teeth are impacted below the gum, which only an X-ray will show.

Why do wisdom teeth exist if we don't need them?

Wisdom teeth exist because early humans had larger jaws and a coarse diet that wore down teeth, so a late third set of molars was useful. Cooking and softer food reduced that need, and jaws grew smaller over time, but the genes for wisdom teeth remain in most people.

Do all wisdom teeth need to be removed?

Not all wisdom teeth need to be removed. Healthy wisdom teeth that are fully erupted, upright, free of decay and easy to clean can often stay. Removal is usually advised for impacted, infected, decayed or crowding teeth. Your dentist or oral surgeon will base the recommendation on your X-rays and symptoms.

At what age do wisdom teeth come in?

Wisdom teeth usually come in between about 17 and 25 years of age, though timing varies widely. Some erupt earlier or later, and some never break through the gum. Dentists often check them with an X-ray in the late teens to see whether they have enough room to erupt normally.
