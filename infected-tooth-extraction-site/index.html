Infected Tooth Extraction Site: How to Tell, What to Do and How to Prevent It

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 31, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

An infected tooth extraction site usually shows pain and swelling that get worse after the third day instead of better, pus or discharge, a foul taste, fever, or red hot gum tissue. Contact your dentist promptly, because infections are treated with cleaning, drainage and sometimes antibiotics. Spreading facial swelling or trouble swallowing or breathing needs emergency care (Cleveland Clinic).

Key takeaways
  • Normal healing improves after day two or three; infection often gets worse.
  • Pus, fever, foul taste and spreading swelling are key warning signs.
  • Dry socket is a different problem: severe pain with an empty socket, usually without pus.
  • Treatment includes cleaning and draining the site, and sometimes antibiotics.
  • Good aftercare and not smoking lower the risk of infection.

How do you tell normal healing from an infected extraction site?

Normal healing after a tooth extraction steadily improves after the first two or three days, while an infection usually makes things worse over time. Some pain, swelling and oozing in the first day or two is expected as the body forms a clot and starts repairing the socket.

A blood clot in the socket can look dark red at first, then a whitish or yellowish layer of healing tissue may appear over the next few days. That pale tissue is normal and is not pus. Our guide to what a normal socket looks like after tooth extraction shows the expected changes day by day.

The pattern matters more than any single sign. Improvement over time points to healing; worsening after the third day points to a problem that needs a dentist.

What are the signs of an infected tooth extraction site?

The signs of an infected tooth extraction site include symptoms in the mouth and sometimes the whole body.

  • Pain that increases after the third day.
  • Swelling that grows or spreads into the cheek, jaw or neck.
  • Pus or discharge from the socket.
  • A foul taste or bad breath that does not improve with gentle rinsing.
  • Red, hot, very tender gums around the site.
  • Fever, chills or feeling unwell.
  • Swollen lymph nodes under the jaw.
  • Difficulty opening the mouth.

Go to an emergency department for facial or neck swelling that spreads, swelling under the tongue, fever with swelling, or trouble swallowing or breathing. These may signal a spreading infection.

Is it infection or dry socket?

Infection and dry socket can both cause pain after an extraction, but they are different problems. Dry socket happens when the blood clot is lost, leaving bone exposed. It usually causes severe, throbbing pain two to four days after the extraction, often spreading to the ear, with an empty-looking socket and bad taste, but typically without pus, fever or major swelling.

Infection more often brings increasing swelling, pus, fever and red, hot tissue. Both need a dentist's attention. The Cleveland Clinic's dry socket guide explains the difference and treatment.

What causes an extraction site to become infected?

An extraction site becomes infected when bacteria multiply in the healing socket. Some risk factors increase the chance.

  • Food debris trapped in the socket.
  • Poor oral hygiene during healing.
  • Smoking or vaping, which slows healing; see smoking after tooth extraction.
  • Removing a tooth that was already badly infected.
  • Surgical or difficult extractions, especially impacted teeth.
  • Diabetes, a weakened immune system or certain medicines.
  • Not following aftercare instructions.

How is an infected tooth extraction site treated?

An infected tooth extraction site is usually treated by your dentist with a quick office visit. The dentist examines the area, may take an X-ray to check for leftover root fragments or bone problems, and cleans and flushes the socket.

If there is pus, the dentist drains it. Antibiotics may be prescribed when the infection is spreading, you have a fever, or you have health conditions that raise your risk. Take any antibiotics exactly as prescribed. In rare cases, more extensive treatment is needed. Most infections improve quickly once treated.

What can you do at home while waiting to see the dentist?

While waiting to see the dentist, you can keep the site clean and manage discomfort, but home care does not replace professional treatment for an infection.

  • Rinse gently with warm salt water after meals, unless told otherwise.
  • Take over-the-counter pain relievers as directed on the label.
  • Eat soft foods and chew on the other side; see what to eat after tooth extraction.
  • Avoid smoking, alcohol and straws.
  • Do not poke the socket or try to squeeze out pus.

How can you prevent infection after a tooth extraction?

You can lower the risk of infection after a tooth extraction by protecting the clot and keeping the area clean. Follow your dentist's written instructions, which take priority over general advice.

  1. Bite on gauze as instructed to control bleeding.
  2. Avoid rinsing hard, spitting forcefully or using straws for the first day or longer.
  3. From the next day, rinse gently with warm salt water after meals.
  4. Brush the rest of your teeth normally, carefully around the site.
  5. Do not smoke for as long as possible.
  6. Take any prescribed medicines as directed.
  7. Keep your follow-up appointment.

Does an infection add to the cost of an extraction?

An infection can add to the cost of an extraction if extra visits, X-rays or medicines are needed, though many dentists include post-operative checks in the extraction fee. Ask when you book.

For context, Delta Dental lists $150 to $310 per tooth for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction out-of-network, according to its extraction cost guide. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363, and $811 for an allograft bone graft. Delta Dental's coverage guide notes basic procedures such as extractions are often covered at 80%. Can a bone graft site get infected?

A bone graft placed in an extraction socket can occasionally become infected, with similar signs: increasing pain and swelling, discharge, and graft particles coming out. Contact your dentist or surgeon promptly if you notice these.

A few small granules escaping in the first days can be normal, but continuous loss of material with discharge is not. The Cleveland Clinic's bone graft guide describes normal recovery. Not smoking and careful cleaning greatly improve graft success.

When should you call the dentist after an extraction?

You should call the dentist after an extraction whenever healing is not following the expected pattern. Calling early is better than waiting, because infections and dry socket are easier and faster to treat when caught promptly. Call if bleeding continues after an hour of firm gauze pressure, if pain or swelling increases after the third day, if you notice pus or a persistent foul taste, if you develop a fever, or if numbness has not worn off by the next day.

Also call if you feel a sharp fragment in the socket or the stitches come loose early with bleeding. Most offices keep an emergency line for patients after surgery, and a quick check usually gives peace of mind even when everything turns out to be normal.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my tooth extraction site is infected?

Your tooth extraction site may be infected if pain and swelling get worse after the third day, you see pus or discharge, you have a foul taste or bad breath, the gums are red and hot, or you develop a fever. Contact your dentist promptly.

Is white stuff in my extraction site infection?

White or yellowish tissue in an extraction site a few days after the procedure is usually healthy granulation tissue, not infection. Infection is more likely if the white material comes with increasing pain, swelling, pus that drains, a foul taste or fever.

Can an infected tooth extraction site heal on its own?

An infected tooth extraction site may not heal on its own and can spread if untreated. A dentist can clean and drain the area and decide whether antibiotics are needed. Most infections clear quickly with treatment.

How long after tooth extraction can infection occur?

Infection after tooth extraction most often appears within the first week, typically from around the third day onward, but it can occur later. Any worsening pain, swelling or discharge after initial improvement should be checked by your dentist.

Is infection after tooth extraction an emergency?

Infection after tooth extraction is an emergency when it causes spreading facial or neck swelling, swelling under the tongue, fever with swelling, or trouble swallowing or breathing. Go to an emergency department right away in these cases.
