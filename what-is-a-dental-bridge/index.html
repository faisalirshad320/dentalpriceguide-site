What Is a Dental Bridge? How Tooth Bridges Work, the Main Types, Lifespan and Cost

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A dental bridge is a fixed replacement for one or more missing teeth: a false tooth, called a pontic, held in place by crowns or bonded wings on the neighboring teeth or by implants. A common three-unit bridge costs about $3,600 out-of-network, according to Delta Dental, though local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • A dental bridge uses neighboring teeth or implants to hold a false tooth in a gap.
  • The main types are traditional, cantilever, Maryland (resin-bonded) and implant-supported bridges.
  • A three-unit bridge costs about $3,600 out-of-network (Delta Dental); insurance typically covers 50% to 80%.
  • Bridges are fixed in place but not lifelong; the health of the supporting teeth decides how long they last.

What is a dental bridge?

A dental bridge is a fixed dental restoration that replaces one or more missing teeth by bridging the gap with a false tooth attached to the teeth or implants on either side. Unlike a removable partial denture, a bridge is cemented or bonded in place, and only a dentist can take it off.

A traditional bridge has three parts: two crowns, called abutments, that fit over the teeth beside the gap, and the false tooth, called a pontic, joined between them. Pontics are usually made of porcelain, porcelain fused to metal, or zirconia, shaded to match your other teeth.

Dental bridges are made and placed by general dentists and by prosthodontists, the specialists who focus on replacing teeth. Cleveland Clinic groups bridges with dentures and implants as the main ways to replace missing teeth.

Replacing a missing tooth matters for more than looks. A gap can let neighboring teeth tip or drift and the opposing tooth grow down into the space, which changes your bite and makes cleaning harder.

What are the types of dental bridges?

The four main types of dental bridges are traditional, cantilever, Maryland (resin-bonded) and implant-supported bridges, and each suits a different gap and budget.

  • Traditional bridge: crowns on the teeth on both sides of the gap hold the pontic. This is the most common design and is strong enough for back teeth. The abutment teeth must be filed down to fit the crowns.
  • Cantilever bridge: the pontic is supported by a crown on one side only. It is used when there is a healthy tooth on just one side, usually toward the front where chewing forces are lighter.
  • Maryland bridge: the pontic has metal or ceramic wings bonded to the back of the neighboring teeth. Little or no drilling is needed, which makes it a conservative choice for a missing front tooth. See our guide to the Maryland bridge dental option for pros, cons and prices.
  • Implant-supported bridge: dental implants, not natural teeth, hold the bridge. It is often used when several teeth in a row are missing.

A dental bridge for front teeth is often a Maryland or cantilever design, because appearance matters most there and bite forces are lower. Your dentist will recommend a type based on the health of the neighboring teeth, your bite and the size of the gap.

What is the dental bridge procedure?

The dental bridge procedure for a traditional bridge usually takes two visits a couple of weeks apart: one to prepare the teeth and take impressions, and one to fit and cement the final bridge.

  1. Exam and planning: X-rays check the health and roots of the abutment teeth. Decay or gum disease is treated first.
  2. Tooth preparation: under local anesthetic, the abutment teeth are reshaped to make room for crowns.
  3. Impressions or digital scan: a model of your teeth goes to a dental lab, which makes the bridge.
  4. Temporary bridge: a temporary protects the prepared teeth and fills the gap while you wait.
  5. Fitting: the dentist checks fit, color and bite, then cements the bridge.

Some offices with in-house milling can make a bridge in fewer visits. Implant-supported bridges take months longer because the implants must fuse with the jawbone first. Mild gum soreness and sensitivity of the prepared teeth are common for a few days. Follow your dentist's instructions on eating and cleaning. Seek prompt care for facial swelling, fever, bleeding that will not stop, or severe pain.

Is a dental bridge permanent?

A dental bridge is permanent in the sense that it is fixed in place and cannot be removed at home, but it is not a lifelong restoration. With good care a bridge commonly lasts many years, yet most will need replacing at some point.

A bridge's lifespan depends mostly on the health of the abutment teeth. The usual reasons a bridge fails are decay under the crowns, gum disease around the supporting teeth, and a cracked or loosened abutment. Maryland bridges can come unbonded, though they can often be re-bonded.

  • Clean under the pontic every day with a floss threader, super floss or a water flosser.
  • Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste.
  • Avoid chewing ice, hard candy or pens.
  • Keep regular checkups and cleanings so problems are caught early.

Call your dentist if the bridge feels loose, your bite changes, or a supporting tooth becomes sore. Catching a problem early can sometimes save the bridge or the abutment tooth.

Who is a good candidate for a dental bridge?

A good candidate for a dental bridge is someone missing one or a few teeth in a row who has healthy, well-supported teeth or implants on either side of the gap and healthy gums. The supporting teeth carry the load of the false tooth, so their roots and bone level matter most.

A bridge may not suit you if the neighboring teeth are loose, have advanced gum disease, or are too weak to take a crown. Heavy grinding can also shorten a bridge's life, and a night guard may be advised. In those cases your dentist may suggest an implant, a partial denture, or treating the gums first.

Dental bridge vs implant: which is better?

A dental bridge is usually faster and less invasive than an implant, while an implant replaces a tooth without relying on the neighboring teeth. The better choice depends on the health of the adjacent teeth, the jawbone, your general health and your budget.

A bridge makes sense when the neighboring teeth already have large fillings or need crowns anyway, when surgery is not a good option, or when you want the gap closed within weeks. An implant may be preferred when the neighboring teeth are healthy and untouched, because a traditional bridge requires filing them down. Healthline compares the two in more detail, including treatment time and longevity.

Implants also help preserve the jawbone where the tooth was lost, which a bridge does not do. Ask your dentist to explain the trade-offs for your specific gap, and follow their recommendation for your situation.

How much is a dental bridge?

A dental bridge costs about $3,600 for a three-unit bridge (two crowns and a pontic) out-of-network, according to Delta Dental's 2025 data. The CareCredit cost study reports an average of $5,197 for a single-tooth traditional bridge and $1,569 for a single-tooth Maryland bridge.

The two sources measure different things. Delta Dental's figure reflects out-of-network fees, while CareCredit's comes from a survey of procedure costs. Both are national averages; your local price may be higher or lower depending on materials, the number of units and your dentist.

Insurance typically covers 50% to 80% of a bridge, according to Delta Dental, though bridges are often classed as major services and annual maximums limit the payout. Frequently asked questions

What is a tooth bridge?

A tooth bridge is another name for a dental bridge: a fixed false tooth anchored to the teeth or implants on either side of a gap. The supporting teeth usually get crowns, and the false tooth is joined between them. A bridge stays in your mouth full time and is cleaned like natural teeth, with extra care under the false tooth.

How much is a dental bridge?

A three-unit dental bridge costs about $3,600 out-of-network, according to Delta Dental's 2025 data. CareCredit's cost study puts a single-tooth traditional bridge at $5,197 and a Maryland bridge at $1,569 on average. Insurance typically covers 50% to 80%, and prices vary by location, materials and the number of teeth involved.

Is a dental bridge permanent?

A dental bridge is fixed in place and can only be removed by a dentist, so it feels permanent day to day. It is not lifelong, though. Bridges often last many years with good cleaning and checkups, but decay or gum disease in the supporting teeth, or wear and damage, can mean it needs replacing.

Does getting a dental bridge hurt?

Getting a dental bridge usually does not hurt during treatment because the teeth are numbed with local anesthetic before they are prepared. Afterward, sore gums and some sensitivity to hot and cold are common for a few days. Pain that keeps getting worse, swelling or fever are reasons to contact your dentist promptly.

Can you get a bridge for front teeth?

Yes, bridges are commonly used for front teeth. A Maryland bridge, which bonds to the back of the neighboring teeth, or a cantilever bridge is often chosen because front teeth face lighter chewing forces and appearance matters most. Your dentist will match the color of the false tooth to your natural teeth.

How do you clean under a dental bridge?

Clean under a dental bridge every day using a floss threader, super floss, interdental brushes or a water flosser to reach the space beneath the false tooth. Brush the bridge and supporting teeth twice daily with fluoride toothpaste. Plaque left under the pontic can cause gum disease and decay in the teeth that hold the bridge.
