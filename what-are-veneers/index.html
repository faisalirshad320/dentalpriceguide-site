What Are Veneers? How Dental Veneers Work, Types and What They Cost

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

What are veneers? Veneers are thin, tooth-colored shells of porcelain or composite resin bonded to the front of teeth to change their color, shape or size. A porcelain veneer costs $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, according to Delta Dental; local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • Veneers are thin porcelain or composite shells bonded to the front surface of teeth.
  • Traditional porcelain veneers usually need a thin layer of enamel removed, which cannot be undone.
  • No-prep and minimal-prep veneers remove little or no enamel but are not right for every smile.
  • Veneers are usually treated as cosmetic and often not covered by dental insurance.
  • Your own dentist's assessment and instructions take priority over general guidance.

What are dental veneers?

Dental veneers are thin, custom-made shells that a dentist bonds to the front surface of teeth to improve how they look. Veneers cover the visible side of the tooth only, unlike a crown, which wraps around the whole tooth.

Dentists use veneers to fix problems that whitening or simple bonding cannot fully solve, according to Healthline and Cleveland Clinic. Common reasons include deep stains, chipped or worn edges, small gaps, slightly crooked or uneven teeth, and teeth that look too short or misshapen.

Veneers are a cosmetic treatment rather than a fix for decay, gum disease or a bad bite. A dentist will usually treat cavities and gum problems first, because veneers need a healthy tooth and healthy gums underneath to last. If your main concern is a damaged tooth that needs strength, our comparison of veneers vs crowns explains when each one fits.

What types of veneers are there?

The main types of veneers are porcelain veneers, composite resin veneers, and no-prep or minimal-prep veneers. Each type differs in how much enamel is removed, how long it lasts and how much it costs.

  • Porcelain veneers: Made in a dental lab from thin ceramic. Porcelain dental veneers resist staining, reflect light much like natural enamel and usually last the longest. They typically take at least two visits.
  • Composite resin veneers: Built up directly on the tooth or made in a lab from tooth-colored resin. Composite veneers cost less and are easier to repair, but they stain and chip more readily than porcelain.
  • No-prep and minimal-prep veneers: Very thin porcelain shells that need little or no enamel removal. No-prep veneers can sometimes be removed later, but they can look bulky on teeth that already stick out, so they suit small or narrow teeth best.

No-prep veneers cost is usually in a similar range to other porcelain veneers, because the lab work and materials are comparable. The best type for you depends on your tooth position, enamel thickness, bite and goals, which is a decision to make with your dentist.

How do veneers work, step by step?

Veneers work by bonding a thin shell to a lightly prepared tooth surface so the shell becomes the new visible front of the tooth. The veneers process for porcelain usually takes two or three visits spread over a few weeks.

  1. Consultation and planning: The dentist checks your teeth and gums, takes X-rays and photos, and talks through the shape and shade you want. Some offices make a mock-up so you can preview the result.
  2. Tooth preparation: For traditional veneers, the dentist numbs the area and removes a thin layer of enamel from the front of each tooth so the veneer sits flush. Teeth prepped for veneers look slightly smaller and rougher than before.
  3. Impressions and temporaries: The dentist takes an impression or digital scan and may place temporary veneers while the lab makes the final ones.
  4. Bonding: At the fitting, the dentist checks the fit and color, cleans and etches the tooth, and bonds each veneer with a resin cement hardened by a curing light.
  5. Bite check and follow-up: The dentist adjusts the bite and may schedule a short review visit.

Composite veneers are often done in a single visit because the resin is shaped directly on the tooth. To see the range of results from these steps, browse our veneers before and after examples.

What happens to teeth under veneers?

Teeth under veneers stay in place as living teeth, with a thin layer of enamel usually removed from the front surface so the veneer can be bonded. The tooth underneath still needs brushing, flossing and checkups, because decay can develop at the edges of a veneer.

What teeth look like under veneers depends on how much they were prepared. After traditional preparation, the front surface is slightly reduced and roughened, and the natural shade still shows. Teeth under no-prep veneers look almost unchanged.

Because enamel does not grow back, traditional porcelain veneers are usually considered a permanent commitment. If a veneer comes off or wears out, the tooth will need a new veneer or another restoration to cover the prepared surface. Our guide on whether veneers are permanent covers lifespan and replacement in more detail.

Follow your dentist's instructions after veneers are placed. Call your dentist if a veneer feels loose, chips, or your bite feels off. Seek urgent care for facial swelling, fever, bleeding that will not stop, or severe tooth pain.

Are veneers bad for your teeth?

Veneers are not bad for your teeth when they are planned and placed well on healthy teeth, but they do carry trade-offs. The main one is that traditional preparation removes enamel permanently.

  • Sensitivity: Some people notice sensitivity to hot and cold for a short time after preparation.
  • Chipping or debonding: Biting nails, chewing ice or grinding can chip or loosen veneers. A night guard helps if you grind.
  • Decay at the edges: The tooth under a veneer can still decay, especially where the veneer meets the tooth.
  • Overtreatment: Placing more veneers than needed, or removing more enamel than needed, adds risk without benefit. Ask why each tooth is included in the plan.

A good candidate for veneers has healthy gums, enough enamel and no untreated decay. People with heavy grinding, active gum disease or very thin enamel may be steered toward other options first.

Are veneers worth it?

Veneers are worth it for many people who want a lasting change to the color or shape of their front teeth and accept that the treatment is usually permanent and expensive. Whether veneers are worth it for you depends on your goals, budget and the condition of your teeth.

Veneers make the most sense when whitening cannot fix the color, when several teeth have chips or uneven edges, or when you want a uniform smile that bonding alone cannot achieve. Veneers make less sense if a simpler option, such as whitening, bonding or orthodontics, would solve the main problem with less enamel loss.

Ask your dentist to show you options side by side. A mock-up, digital smile preview or photos of past cases can help you judge whether the expected result justifies the cost and commitment.

What do veneers cost per tooth?

Porcelain veneers cost $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, according to Delta Dental. A separate cost study published by CareCredit puts a single porcelain veneer at a national average of $1,765 and a full set of six to eight porcelain veneers at an average of $15,486.

These two sources measure different things: Delta Dental's range reflects its own 2024 data on veneer fees, while CareCredit reports averages from the Synchrony procedure cost study. Both are national figures, and local prices vary with the dentist, the lab and the number of teeth.

Delta Dental notes that veneers are usually treated as cosmetic and often not covered by insurance. For a lower-cost alternative on minor chips or gaps, CareCredit lists dental bonding at an average of $431 per tooth. Our full veneers cost guide breaks down pricing, financing and ways to pay less.

Frequently asked questions

What are veneers made of?

Veneers are made of either porcelain (a thin dental ceramic) or composite resin (a tooth-colored plastic material). Porcelain veneers are made in a lab, resist staining and usually last longer. Composite veneers can often be shaped directly on the tooth in one visit and cost less, but they chip and stain more easily.

Do they shave your teeth for veneers?

Traditional porcelain veneers usually require the dentist to remove a thin layer of enamel from the front of each tooth so the veneer sits flush. That enamel does not grow back. No-prep and minimal-prep veneers remove little or no enamel, but they are not suitable for every tooth or smile.

What is the difference between veneers and crowns?

Veneers cover only the front surface of a tooth and are mainly cosmetic. Crowns cover the entire tooth and are used to strengthen and protect teeth that are broken, heavily filled or treated with a root canal. Crowns remove more tooth structure, while veneers suit teeth that are healthy but look uneven or stained.

Can you get veneers on just one tooth?

Yes, a dentist can place a veneer on one tooth, often to fix a chipped, stained or misshapen front tooth. Matching a single veneer to the neighboring natural teeth takes careful color work, so the dentist may suggest whitening first or bonding as an alternative if the problem is small.

Does insurance cover veneers?

Veneers are usually treated as cosmetic and often not covered by dental insurance, according to Delta Dental. A plan may help in some cases where a veneer repairs damage from an injury. Ask your dentist to request a pre-treatment estimate from your insurer before treatment begins.

Can veneers be removed?

Traditional porcelain veneers are not considered removable, because the enamel taken off during preparation does not grow back and the tooth needs covering. A dentist can replace an old veneer with a new one. No-prep veneers can sometimes be removed with less impact on the tooth.
