Cost of Full Mouth Dental Implants: All-on-4, Per-Tooth Implants and Insurance

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

The cost of full mouth dental implants starts with an All-on-4 full-arch average of $15,176 per arch, according to CareCredit. A full mouth means two arches, so the total is roughly double. Bone grafts, CT scans and extractions add more. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • All-on-4 implants average $15,176 per full arch nationally (CareCredit); a full mouth is two arches.
  • Replacing teeth one implant at a time costs $2,800 to $5,600 per tooth without dental benefits (Delta Dental), which adds up fast for a full mouth.
  • Bone grafts ($811 allograft or $2,779 autograft on average) and a cone-beam CT scan ($466 average) are common extra costs.
  • Dental insurance typically pays about 50% for major work, but only up to the annual maximum, often around $1,500.
  • Get an itemized treatment plan that lists surgery, temporary teeth, final teeth and follow-up visits separately.

How much does a full mouth of dental implants cost?

A full mouth of dental implants usually costs about twice the per-arch price, and the national average for an All-on-4 full arch is $15,176, according to CareCredit's Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study. An arch is either the upper or the lower jaw, so replacing every tooth means paying for two arches.

Full mouth dental implant quotes can sit well above or below that figure. The final price depends on how many implants are placed per arch, whether you need extractions or bone grafting first, the material of the final bridge and the region you live in. Practices in large cities generally charge more than those in smaller towns.

Full mouth implant pricing also depends on whether the quote covers one stage or the whole treatment. Some advertised prices include only the surgery and a temporary set of teeth, with the permanent bridge billed months later. Ask for one written plan that shows every phase, from scans to the final teeth.

A full mouth implant quote should list: consultation and imaging, extractions, bone grafts, implant posts, abutments, temporary teeth, final bridge or denture, sedation and follow-up visits. Anything not listed may be billed separately.

How much is a full set of dental implants with All-on-4 vs one implant per tooth?

A full set of dental implants usually costs far less with an All-on-4 approach than with one implant per tooth, because All-on-4 supports a whole arch of teeth on four posts instead of placing a separate implant for every missing tooth.

Individual implants are priced per tooth. Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600 for people without dental benefits, and CareCredit lists a standard endosteal implant at $4,344 per tooth on average. A separate CareCredit figure gives $2,143 as the national average for a single full-size implant; the gap reflects different definitions, since some figures cover only the post while others include the abutment and crown. Multiply any of these by the number of teeth in a full mouth and the total quickly passes the cost of two full-arch restorations.

Full-arch options such as All-on-4 use four (sometimes five or six) implants per jaw to hold a fixed bridge of replacement teeth. Implant-supported overdentures, which snap onto two to four implants and can be removed for cleaning, are generally the lower-cost implant option. Our guide to types of dental implants compares these designs, and our main page on dental implant cost covers single-tooth pricing.

Full mouth implants are not the only route. Bridges and conventional dentures cost less up front but have trade-offs in comfort, chewing and bone loss over time; see Healthline's comparison of dentures and implants and implants versus bridges.

What adds to the cost of full mouth dental implants?

The cost of full mouth dental implants rises most with bone grafting, extractions, imaging, sedation and the material used for the final teeth. These items can add thousands of dollars to the headline per-arch price.

  • Bone grafting: If the jawbone is too thin to hold implants, a graft rebuilds it first. CareCredit reports averages of $811 for an allograft (donor bone) and $2,779 for an autograft (your own bone). Cleveland Clinic explains the graft types and healing time.
  • 3D imaging: A cone-beam CT scan maps bone volume and nerve position before surgery. CareCredit lists the average at $466.
  • Extractions: Removing remaining teeth is often billed per tooth, and surgical extractions cost more than simple ones.
  • Sedation: IV sedation or general anesthesia for a long surgery is usually a separate charge.
  • Final teeth material: Acrylic bridges generally cost less than zirconia or porcelain, though harder materials may last longer.
  • Provider type: Oral surgeons, periodontists and prosthodontists may work as a team, with each billing for their part. Cleveland Clinic's prosthodontics overview explains the specialists involved.

Full mouth implant treatment also involves maintenance costs later, such as professional cleanings around the implants and eventual repair or replacement of the bridge. Ask how long the final teeth are expected to last and what a replacement would cost.

How much do full mouth dental implants cost with insurance?

Full mouth dental implants with insurance usually still leave you paying most of the bill, because dental plans cap benefits at an annual maximum. Delta Dental explains that many plans follow a 100-80-50 structure, paying about 50% for major work such as implants, but only up to the annual maximum, for example $1,500.

Full mouth implant coverage is therefore limited in practice. Once the plan has paid its yearly maximum, the rest is out of pocket. Some plans exclude implants entirely, pay only the amount they would pay for a denture (an "alternate benefit"), or impose waiting periods for major work. Medicare Part A and B do not generally cover routine dental implants, though some Medicare Advantage plans include limited dental benefits.

Full mouth implant treatment often spans more than one calendar year, which can let you use two annual maximums, one for the surgical phase and one for the final teeth. Ask your dentist whether staging treatment this way is clinically reasonable. Our page on what insurance covers for dental implants explains pre-treatment estimates and alternate benefits in more detail.

How can you lower the cost of full mouth dental implants?

You can lower the cost of full mouth dental implants by comparing itemized quotes, considering dental school programs, staging treatment across benefit years, using HSA or FSA funds and asking whether an implant-supported overdenture would meet your needs.

  1. Get two or three itemized quotes. Compare the same items: number of implants, graft needs, temporary teeth and final bridge material.
  2. Look at university clinics. Dental schools and residency programs often place implants at reduced fees under specialist supervision. Treatment may take longer.
  3. Use in-network providers. Agreed in-network fees can lower the price of covered parts such as extractions and imaging.
  4. Use pre-tax money. Implants are generally an eligible HSA and FSA expense.
  5. Ask about payment plans. Many implant centers offer financing. Compare the interest rate and what happens if a promotional period ends.
  6. Consider overdentures. Removable implant-supported dentures usually need fewer implants and can cost less than a fixed full-arch bridge.

Very low advertised prices deserve careful reading. Our guide to low-price dental implant offers explains what headline deals usually leave out, such as the abutment, the crown or the final teeth.

After implant surgery, follow your surgeon's instructions. Seek urgent care for bleeding that does not slow with pressure, increasing swelling after the third day, fever, pus, or severe pain that pain medicine does not control.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a full set of dental implants cost?

A full set of dental implants usually costs about twice the per-arch price, since a full mouth has two arches. CareCredit lists the national average for All-on-4 implants at $15,176 per arch. Bone grafts, CT scans, extractions and sedation can add to that total, and local prices vary by region and provider.

Is All-on-4 cheaper than individual implants for a full mouth?

All-on-4 is usually cheaper than individual implants for a full mouth, because four implants support a whole arch of teeth. Single implants cost $2,800 to $5,600 per tooth without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental, so replacing every tooth one by one generally costs far more than two full-arch restorations.

Does insurance cover full mouth dental implants?

Insurance may cover part of full mouth dental implants, but rarely much of the total. Delta Dental notes that many plans pay about 50% for major work up to an annual maximum, such as $1,500. Some plans exclude implants or pay only an alternate denture benefit, so request a pre-treatment estimate first.

Do I need a bone graft for full mouth implants?

Many people need a bone graft for full mouth implants, especially if teeth have been missing for years and the jawbone has shrunk. CareCredit lists average graft costs of $811 for an allograft and $2,779 for an autograft. All-on-4 designs are sometimes chosen because angled implants can reduce the need for grafting.

How long do full mouth dental implants take?

Full mouth dental implants usually take several months from start to finish. Many All-on-4 patients receive temporary teeth on the day of surgery, then wait three to six months for the implants to fuse with bone before the final bridge is made. Bone grafting or extractions can lengthen the timeline. Your surgeon will give you a specific schedule.

Are full mouth implants worth it compared with dentures?

Full mouth implants cost much more than conventional dentures but are fixed in place, chew more like natural teeth and help preserve jawbone. Dentures are cheaper up front but can slip and may need relining as bone shrinks. The right choice depends on your health, bone, budget and preferences, so discuss both with a prosthodontist.
