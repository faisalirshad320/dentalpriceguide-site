How Much Does a Tooth Filling Cost With Insurance?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 30, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

With dental insurance, fillings are typically covered at 50%–80% as a basic procedure, according to Delta Dental, so you pay the rest after your deductible. Full fees without insurance run $145 to $480 for a composite filling on a front tooth and $165 to $530 on a back tooth.

Key takeaways
  • Insurance typically covers fillings at 50%–80% after the deductible (Delta Dental).
  • Composite fillings cost $145 to $480 (front) and $165 to $530 (back) without insurance.
  • Amalgam (silver) fillings cost $110 to $455, depending on the number of surfaces.
  • Some plans pay only the amalgam rate for back-tooth composite fillings.
  • Your share depends on the deductible, coverage percentage and annual maximum.

How much does insurance cover for a filling?

Dental insurance typically covers 50%–80% of a filling, according to Delta Dental. Most plans classify fillings as basic services, and a common 100-80-50 plan pays 80% for basic care after the deductible.

The rest is your coinsurance. If you have not yet met your deductible this year, you pay that first. Once the plan reaches its annual maximum, you pay the full fee for any further work that year. Delta Dental's coverage guide explains these terms.

How do you estimate your filling cost with insurance?

You can estimate your filling cost with insurance in four steps, using your plan details and the dentist's fee.

  1. Get the fee: in-network offices charge the plan's negotiated fee.
  2. Apply the deductible: subtract anything you still owe toward it.
  3. Apply the coverage percentage: for example, the plan pays 80% and you pay 20%.
  4. Check the annual maximum: if it is nearly used up, you pay more.

The number of surfaces matters. A filling covering one surface of a tooth costs less than one covering two, three or more surfaces, because more tooth is being rebuilt. Ask the office how many surfaces your filling involves.

Why might insurance pay less for a white filling?

Insurance might pay less for a white (composite) filling on a back tooth because some plans apply an alternate benefit, paying only what a silver amalgam filling would cost. You then pay the difference between the amalgam allowance and the composite fee, plus your normal coinsurance.

Front-tooth composites are usually covered at the composite rate because silver fillings are not used where they would show. If you prefer tooth-coloured fillings on back teeth, check your plan's policy. Delta Dental lists amalgam fillings at $110 to $455 depending on surfaces, and CareCredit reports averages of $139 for amalgam and $226 for composite resin.

What do fillings cost before insurance?

Before insurance, a composite filling costs $145 to $480 on a front tooth and $165 to $530 on a back tooth, according to Delta Dental. Back teeth cost more because they are larger, harder to reach and take heavier chewing forces.

What other costs come with a filling?

A filling appointment can include other charges besides the filling itself. An exam and X-rays are usually needed to diagnose the cavity, though these are often covered at a high percentage as diagnostic care.

Local anesthetic is normally included in the filling fee. If a cavity turns out deeper than expected, the dentist may need to place a protective liner or, if the nerve is affected, recommend a root canal and crown instead. Ask about these possibilities before treatment so you are not surprised.

Which filling material is best value with insurance?

The best-value filling material with insurance depends on the tooth and your plan. Composite resin is tooth-coloured, bonds to the tooth and usually needs less drilling, which makes it the standard choice for front teeth and a popular choice for back teeth.

Silver amalgam is durable, has a long track record on back teeth and usually costs less, which is why some plans use it as the benchmark for back-tooth coverage. CareCredit's averages show the gap: $139 for amalgam compared with $226 for composite resin. Many dentists no longer place amalgam, so ask what your office offers.

Other materials such as glass ionomer, used near the gum line and in children, and ceramic or gold inlays for larger repairs, are covered differently. Inlays and onlays are often classed as major services. If your dentist recommends one of these, ask why a standard filling would not work and what your plan pays for each option. A slightly higher upfront cost can be worthwhile if the restoration is likely to last longer.

Do new insurance plans have waiting periods for fillings?

Some new dental insurance plans have waiting periods for basic services such as fillings, often a few months. During the waiting period, the plan may cover only preventive care like cleanings and exams.

Small cavities can usually wait a short time safely, but deep cavities can progress. If you have a painful tooth, ask your dentist whether delaying is safe. A small filling today costs far less than a root canal later.

How can you lower your filling cost with insurance?

You can lower your filling cost with insurance by staying in network, timing treatment to use benefits efficiently and preventing new cavities.

  • Use an in-network dentist to get the negotiated fee.
  • Get a pre-treatment estimate for several fillings at once.
  • Spread non-urgent fillings across benefit years if your maximum is low.
  • Use HSA or FSA funds for your share.
  • Keep preventive visits, usually covered at or near 100%, to catch cavities while small.

How can you avoid needing fillings?

You can avoid needing many fillings by preventing cavities in the first place. The Cleveland Clinic's cavity guide recommends brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between teeth, limiting frequent sugary snacks and drinks, and keeping regular dental visits.

Sealants on back teeth and fluoride treatments can also help, especially for children and people with a high cavity risk. Learn more about cavity fillings and what to expect, and about chipped tooth repair cost if a tooth is damaged rather than decayed.

What if the cavity is too big for a filling?

If a cavity is too big for a regular filling, your dentist may recommend an inlay, onlay or crown to rebuild the tooth. These are often classified as major services, which many plans cover at about 50%, so your share rises.

If decay has reached the nerve, a root canal may be needed before the tooth is restored. Treating cavities while they are small keeps your costs, and the amount of tooth lost, as low as possible.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a filling with insurance?

With insurance, you typically pay 20%–50% of the filling fee after your deductible, because plans commonly cover fillings at 50%–80% according to Delta Dental. Full fees for composite fillings run $145 to $480 on front teeth and $165 to $530 on back teeth.

Does insurance cover white fillings?

Most insurance plans cover white composite fillings on front teeth. For back teeth, some plans pay only the amalgam rate and you pay the difference for a composite. Check your plan for an alternate benefit or downgrade clause.

Are fillings covered at 100% by insurance?

Fillings are rarely covered at 100%. Most plans treat them as basic services covered at 50%–80% after the deductible. Preventive care like cleanings and exams is more often covered at or near 100%.

Why does a filling cost more on a back tooth?

Fillings cost more on back teeth because molars are larger, often need fillings across more surfaces, are harder to reach and must withstand heavy chewing forces. Delta Dental lists $165 to $530 for a back-tooth composite versus $145 to $480 for a front tooth.

Do I have to pay my deductible for a filling?

You usually have to pay your deductible before insurance pays for a filling, because fillings are basic services. Many plans waive the deductible for preventive care but not for basic or major work. Check your plan's details.
