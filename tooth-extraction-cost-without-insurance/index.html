How Much Does It Cost to Pull a Tooth Without Insurance?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS, M.Phil Chemical PathologyUpdated November 5, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Without insurance, pulling a tooth costs about $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363. X-rays, sedation and bone grafts are usually extra.

Key takeaways
  • Simple extraction: $150 to $310 per tooth (Delta Dental), $177 average (CareCredit).
  • Surgical extraction: $260 to $460 per tooth (Delta Dental), $363 average (CareCredit).
  • Exams, X-rays, sedation and bone grafts are typically billed separately.
  • Community clinics, dental schools and membership plans can lower the cost.
  • Ask whether replacing the tooth later will be needed and budget for it.

How much does it cost to pull a tooth without insurance?

Pulling a tooth without insurance usually costs $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth, according to Delta Dental's out-of-network data. CareCredit's procedure cost list reports national averages of $177 for a simple extraction and $363 for a surgical extraction.

A simple extraction is for a tooth that is fully visible and can be loosened and lifted out with standard instruments. A surgical extraction is needed when a tooth is broken at the gumline, impacted, has curved roots or must be removed in pieces. Your dentist decides which applies after an exam and X-ray. What extra fees come with a tooth extraction?

Extra fees often come with a tooth extraction on top of the extraction itself. Asking about each one in advance helps you avoid surprises.

  • Exam: a limited or emergency exam to diagnose the problem.
  • X-rays: at least one image of the tooth and roots, sometimes a 3D scan for complex cases.
  • Sedation: nitrous oxide, oral sedation or IV sedation, billed separately.
  • Bone graft: to preserve the socket for a future implant; CareCredit averages an allograft at $811.
  • Prescriptions: pain relief or antibiotics if needed.
  • Follow-up visits: often included, but confirm.

Is it cheaper at a general dentist or an oral surgeon?

A simple extraction is often cheaper at a general dentist than at an oral surgeon. Many general dentists perform routine extractions in their own office.

Oral surgeons tend to handle difficult extractions, impacted teeth and patients who want IV sedation or general anesthesia. Their fees may be higher, but they are equipped for complex cases. If your dentist refers you, ask why and request a written estimate from the surgeon.

How can you get a cheap tooth extraction without insurance?

You can often get a cheaper tooth extraction without insurance by looking beyond a standard private practice. Several options are widely available.

  • Community health centers with sliding-scale fees based on income.
  • Dental schools, where supervised students and residents charge lower fees.
  • In-house membership plans that include discounts on treatment.
  • Discount dental plans offering reduced fees at participating dentists.
  • Cash-pay discounts for paying at the visit.
  • Free dental days and charity events in some areas.

What if you need an emergency extraction?

If you need an emergency extraction because of severe pain, swelling or infection, call a dentist as soon as possible; many keep same-day slots for emergencies. An emergency exam fee may be added.

Go to an emergency department for facial or neck swelling that spreads, swelling with fever, or trouble swallowing or breathing. These signs can mean a spreading infection that needs immediate care.

Hospital emergency departments can treat pain and infection but often cannot extract teeth, so you may still need a dentist afterward.

How does the cost compare with insurance?

With insurance, extractions are often basic procedures covered at 80% on a typical 100-80-50 plan, according to Delta Dental's coverage guide, after the deductible. That means you pay about a fifth of the allowed fee for basic care.

If you are considering buying insurance for an upcoming extraction, check the waiting period for basic services. Many new plans make you wait several months, so insurance bought today may not help with a tooth that needs removing now.

What should you expect after an extraction?

After an extraction, expect some bleeding for the first few hours, and soreness and swelling for a few days. Bite on gauze as instructed, rest, use cold packs on the cheek and eat soft foods.

Protect the blood clot by avoiding smoking, straws and vigorous rinsing for the first days to lower the risk of dry socket, which the Cleveland Clinic explains. See what to eat after tooth extraction, smoking after tooth extraction and what a normal socket looks like after tooth extraction.

Should you plan to replace the extracted tooth?

You should plan whether to replace an extracted tooth, because the cost of replacement can be much larger than the extraction. A missing front tooth is usually replaced; a missing back tooth may or may not be, depending on your bite.

Options include an implant, a bridge or a partial denture. If you plan an implant, a socket bone graft at the time of extraction can preserve bone; the Cleveland Clinic's bone graft guide explains why. The Cleveland Clinic's extraction guide covers what to expect from the procedure itself.

Is extraction always the cheapest choice?

Extraction is usually the cheapest choice on the day, but not always over time. If the tooth could be saved with a filling or root canal and crown, saving it avoids the cost of replacing it and protects your bite.

When budget is tight, ask your dentist to compare the full cost of each path, including any replacement tooth. Sometimes extraction is the sensible choice, especially for back teeth with a poor outlook, but it should be an informed decision.

What happens during a tooth extraction?

During a tooth extraction, the dentist first numbs the tooth and surrounding gum with local anesthetic, so you feel pressure but not sharp pain. For a simple extraction, an instrument called an elevator loosens the tooth in its socket, and forceps lift it out. The whole process can take just a few minutes once you are numb.

For a surgical extraction, the dentist may make a small cut in the gum, remove a little bone, and divide the tooth into sections so each piece comes out separately. The socket is cleaned, a bone graft may be placed if you plan an implant, and stitches may close the gum. You bite on gauze to control bleeding before going home with written aftercare instructions.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to pull a tooth without insurance?

Pulling a tooth without insurance costs about $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363. X-rays and sedation are extra.

Why is a surgical extraction more expensive?

A surgical extraction is more expensive because it involves opening the gum, sometimes removing bone, and sectioning the tooth to remove it in pieces. It takes more time and skill than a simple extraction, which lifts out a fully visible tooth.

Where can I get a tooth pulled cheaply?

Cheaper tooth extractions are often available at community health centers with sliding-scale fees, dental school clinics, and through in-house membership plans, discount dental plans or cash-pay discounts at private offices.

Do I need X-rays before a tooth extraction?

You usually need at least one X-ray before a tooth extraction so the dentist can see the roots, surrounding bone and nearby structures. Complex extractions may need a 3D scan. X-rays are usually billed separately.

Should I replace a pulled tooth?

Replacing a pulled tooth is often recommended, especially front teeth, because neighbouring teeth can drift and your bite can change. Options include an implant, bridge or partial denture. Discuss whether replacement is needed before the extraction.
