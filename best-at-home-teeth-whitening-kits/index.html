Best At Home Teeth Whitening Kits: How to Choose a Safe Option That Works for You

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

The best at home teeth whitening kits are the ones that suit your teeth: peroxide strips or trays for visible color change, a sensitivity formula if your teeth react, and a product with the ADA Seal where available. If home kits fail, in-office bleaching costs $40 to $700 per visit, per Delta Dental.

Key takeaways
  • Peroxide is what bleaches teeth: hydrogen peroxide works directly, and carbamide peroxide breaks down into hydrogen peroxide more slowly.
  • Strips and trays change tooth color; whitening toothpaste mainly removes surface stains; purple products only mask yellow tones temporarily.
  • Look for the ADA Seal of Acceptance and a sensitivity formula, and never exceed the label directions.
  • Whitening does not change the color of crowns, veneers or fillings, so check with your dentist first if you have dental work on front teeth.
  • Insurance usually does not cover whitening because it is cosmetic; in-office bleaching runs $40 to $700 per visit per Delta Dental.

What types of at home teeth whitening kits are there?

At home teeth whitening kits come in five main types: strips, trays with gel, pens, LED light kits and whitening toothpastes. Each type delivers a whitening agent to the teeth in a different way, and the right choice depends on how much color change you want and how sensitive your teeth are.

  • Whitening strips. Flexible strips coated with peroxide gel, worn daily for a set number of minutes over a course of treatment. Strips are easy to use and give visible results for many people. Our guide to whitening strips, including whether to brush before or after, covers timing and technique.
  • Teeth whitening trays. Store kits use one-size or moldable trays; dentist-made trays are custom fitted, which keeps gel on the teeth and off the gums.
  • Whitening pens. A brush-on teeth whitening gel for spot touch-ups. Pens are handy but deliver less gel than strips or trays.
  • LED kits. A peroxide gel paired with a mouthpiece light. The gel does the whitening; evidence that the light adds much benefit at home is limited.
  • Whitening toothpaste. Removes surface stains from coffee, tea or tobacco but does not change the natural shade of teeth much.

For most people, strips or a well-fitting tray kit give the most noticeable change, while toothpaste and pens are better for keeping results.

Does hydrogen peroxide teeth whitening work, and what about carbamide peroxide?

Hydrogen peroxide teeth whitening works by breaking down stain molecules inside the enamel and the layer underneath, which lightens the tooth color itself. Carbamide peroxide works the same way, but it breaks down into hydrogen peroxide more slowly, so it is often used in longer-wear tray products.

Products with peroxide change tooth shade. Products without peroxide usually remove only surface stains. Peroxide-free kits may use ingredients such as mild abrasives or enzymes, and they tend to produce subtle changes at best.

Higher peroxide strength does not automatically mean better results. Stronger gels can work faster but raise the chance of sensitivity and gum irritation. Using a gentler product for the full recommended course often gives similar results with fewer side effects.

Some popular trends do not bleach teeth at all:

  • Purple teeth whitening products. Purple serums and toothpastes use color correction to cancel yellow tones for a short time. The effect fades, and tooth color is not changed.
  • Charcoal powders. Charcoal is abrasive, and there is little good evidence it whitens. Overuse can wear enamel.
  • Natural teeth whitening hacks. Baking soda is a mild abrasive found in many toothpastes and can lift surface stains. Rubbing teeth with lemon, vinegar or strawberries exposes enamel to acid and can do harm.

What is the best teeth whitening product for you?

The best teeth whitening product for you is one with a proven whitening agent, clear instructions, a fit that keeps gel off your gums, and a formula that matches your sensitivity level. No single product suits everyone, so use these checks rather than marketing claims.

  1. ADA Seal of Acceptance. The American Dental Association awards its Seal to some whitening products after reviewing safety and effectiveness evidence. Not every good product carries the Seal, but the Seal is a useful sign.
  2. Active ingredient listed. The label should name hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide. Vague claims without an ingredient list are a warning sign.
  3. Sensitivity support. Formulas with potassium nitrate or fluoride, or lower-strength gels, help sensitive teeth. Our guide to teeth whitening for sensitive teeth explains how to choose the best whitening strips for sensitive teeth and how to space out sessions.
  4. Good fit. Strips that cover the visible teeth and trays that do not leak reduce gum irritation.
  5. Realistic claims. Results vary with your starting shade and stain type. Yellow stains usually respond better than gray or brown stains.

For maintaining results, a whitening toothpaste helps. Our page on the best teeth whitening toothpaste explains what to look for in ingredients and abrasiveness when choosing what is the best toothpaste for whitening teeth.

Is teeth whitening at home safe?

Teeth whitening at home is generally safe for healthy adult teeth when products are used exactly as directed. The Cleveland Clinic notes that the most common side effects are tooth sensitivity and gum irritation, which usually fade after treatment stops.

See a dentist before whitening if any of these apply:

  • You have untreated cavities, exposed roots, gum disease or worn enamel.
  • You have crowns, veneers, bonding or fillings on front teeth. Whitening will not change their color, so they may stand out afterward.
  • Your teeth are already sensitive, or the discoloration is from medication, injury or a dead tooth, which usually needs a different treatment.
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding, or the user is a child or young teen. Most product labels advise against use in these groups.

Use these safe-use habits:

  • Follow the wear time and number of days on the label; leaving products on longer does not improve results.
  • Wipe away gel that gets onto your gums.
  • Take breaks between courses and avoid back-to-back kits.

Stop whitening and call your dentist if you have severe or lasting tooth pain, white or blistered gums, swelling, or sensitivity that does not settle within a few days of stopping. Follow your dentist's instructions if they advise a different product or schedule.

When is professional teeth whitening better value than a home kit?

Professional teeth whitening is better value than a home kit when you want faster or bigger color change, have sensitivity that makes home kits uncomfortable, or have tried several kits without success. In-office bleaching costs $40 to $700 per visit out-of-network, according to Delta Dental.

The Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study published by CareCredit lists laser in-office whitening at an average of $792 and UV whitening at an average of $583. Delta Dental's range is per visit based on claims data, while CareCredit's figures are survey averages for specific methods, which is why the numbers do not line up exactly. Local prices vary.

Best professional teeth whitening options include in-office bleaching, where a dentist protects your gums and applies a stronger gel, and dentist-supervised take-home trays, which pair custom trays with professional gel. Take-home trays from a dentist often cost less than an in-office session and are easier on sensitive teeth.

Dental insurance usually does not cover whitening because it is cosmetic, so plan to pay out of pocket. Our full guide to teeth whitening cost compares every option, and the Cleveland Clinic explains how whitening fits with other cosmetic dental treatments such as bonding and veneers.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best teeth whitening product?

The best teeth whitening product is one that contains hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide, fits well, matches your sensitivity level and ideally carries the ADA Seal of Acceptance. Strips or tray kits usually give the most visible change at home. Ask your dentist which approach suits your teeth and stain type.

What is the best toothpaste for whitening teeth?

The best toothpaste for whitening teeth is a fluoride toothpaste with gentle stain-removing ingredients, ideally with the ADA Seal. Whitening toothpaste removes surface stains but changes natural tooth shade only a little. Choose a low-abrasion formula if you have sensitive teeth or exposed roots, and use it to maintain results.

Do purple teeth whitening products work?

Purple teeth whitening products do not bleach teeth. Purple serums and toothpastes use color correction to cancel yellow tones, so teeth can look brighter for a short time. The effect fades within hours, and the natural tooth color does not change. Peroxide products are needed for lasting whitening.

How long do at home whitening results last?

At home whitening results typically last several months to a few years, depending on diet, smoking and oral hygiene. Coffee, tea, red wine and tobacco bring stains back faster. Whitening toothpaste and occasional touch-ups help maintain the shade, but follow label limits on how often to repeat treatment.

Does insurance cover teeth whitening?

Insurance usually does not cover teeth whitening because it is considered cosmetic, according to Delta Dental. Expect to pay out of pocket for home kits and professional treatment. Delta Dental lists in-office bleaching at $40 to $700 per visit out-of-network, and local prices vary.

Can whitening strips damage teeth?

Whitening strips used as directed are not known to damage healthy enamel, but they can cause temporary sensitivity and gum irritation. Problems are more likely with overuse, stacked courses or untreated cavities. Stop and see a dentist if pain is severe, gums blister, or sensitivity lasts after stopping.
