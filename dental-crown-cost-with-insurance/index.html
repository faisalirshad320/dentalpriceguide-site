How Much Is a Crown With Insurance?

How much does insurance pay for a crown?

Insurance usually pays about 50% of a dental crown after your deductible, because crowns are classified as major procedures on a typical 100-80-50 plan, according to Delta Dental. You pay the other half, called coinsurance.

The plan pays only until you reach your annual maximum for the year. If a crown and other treatment exceed that maximum, you pay the rest. Some plans also have waiting periods for major procedures, so new members may not be covered right away.

How do you estimate your crown cost with insurance?

You can estimate your crown cost with insurance by working through your plan details and the dentist's fee.

  1. Get the fee: in-network dentists charge the plan's negotiated fee; out-of-network fees can be higher.
  2. Subtract any unmet deductible.
  3. Apply the coverage percentage, often 50% for crowns.
  4. Check your remaining annual maximum.
  5. Add separate items such as a core build-up, which some plans cover differently.

Full out-of-network fees for a permanent crown run $1,100 to $2,000 according to Delta Dental. Your dental office can give a pre-treatment estimate so you see the exact figure.

Does crown material affect what insurance pays?

Crown material can affect what insurance pays. CareCredit reports averages of $1,399 for porcelain crowns, $1,211 for metallic crowns and $697 for resin-based or temporary crowns on its procedure cost list.

Some plans apply an alternate benefit for back teeth, paying only what a less expensive metal crown would cost, even if you choose porcelain or zirconia. You then pay the difference. Front-tooth crowns are usually covered at the tooth-coloured rate. See types of dental crowns and cost and how much a gold tooth is.

When will insurance pay for a crown?

Insurance usually pays for a crown when it is needed to restore a damaged or weakened tooth, such as after a large cavity, a crack or a root canal. Plans often require X-rays or notes showing why a filling would not be enough.

Crowns placed purely for cosmetic reasons, such as changing the shape or colour of a healthy tooth, may be denied. Many plans also limit how often the same tooth can receive a new crown, commonly once every several years. The Cleveland Clinic's dental crowns guide explains the main reasons crowns are recommended.

Is a front tooth crown covered the same way?

A front tooth crown is usually covered the same way as other crowns, as a major procedure at about 50%, when it is needed for a damaged tooth. Because front teeth are visible, the dentist will normally use a tooth-coloured material.

See front tooth crown cost for more on materials and appearance. Learn what a dental crown is if you are new to crowns.

What if you need several crowns in one year?

If you need several crowns in one year, your annual maximum may run out quickly. Annual maximums are often modest compared with the cost of crowns, so they can be reached with a small number of major procedures.

When treatment can safely wait, you may split crowns across two benefit years to use two maximums. Ask your dentist which teeth are most urgent; a cracked tooth or one with a temporary filling should not wait long.

How can you lower your crown cost with insurance?

You can lower your crown cost with insurance by staying in network, getting a pre-treatment estimate, timing treatment across benefit years when safe, and using HSA or FSA funds for your share.

Ask about in-office milled same-day crowns, which some practices price competitively and which avoid a second visit. If cost is a challenge, ask whether an onlay, which covers only part of the tooth, would work instead of a full crown.

What happens when you get a crown?

Getting a crown usually takes two visits, or one with same-day technology. The dentist numbs the tooth, removes decay, reshapes it and takes a scan or impression. A temporary crown protects the tooth while the lab makes the permanent one.

At the second visit, the permanent crown is checked and cemented. Mild sensitivity for a few days is common. With good care, crowns often last many years. Insurance commonly limits replacement to once every several years per tooth, so look after your crown.

Does insurance cover temporary crowns?

Temporary crowns are usually included in the fee for the permanent crown and not billed separately. CareCredit's $697 average for resin-based or temporary crowns reflects cases where a resin crown is used as a longer-term or stand-alone restoration.

If your dentist recommends a long-term temporary, ask how it will be billed and covered.

What if your insurance denies a crown?

If your insurance denies a crown, ask the dental office for the reason code on the explanation of benefits. Common reasons include a frequency limit (the tooth had a crown recently), a waiting period that has not ended, a missing X-ray or narrative, or the insurer deciding a filling would have been adequate.

Many denials can be appealed. The office can resubmit with X-rays, photos and a written explanation showing the extent of the damage, a crack, or the root canal that made a crown necessary. If the denial stands, ask whether a lower-cost option such as an onlay would be covered, and compare that with paying for the crown yourself.

Before treatment, a pre-treatment estimate reduces the chance of a surprise denial. It is not a guarantee, but it shows how the insurer is likely to treat the claim and gives you a chance to address problems in advance.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a crown with insurance?

With insurance, you usually pay about half of the crown fee after your deductible, because crowns are major procedures typically covered at 50%, according to Delta Dental. Full out-of-network fees for a permanent crown run $1,100 to $2,000.

Does dental insurance cover crowns?

Most dental insurance plans cover crowns when they are needed to restore a damaged, weakened or root canal treated tooth, usually at about 50% as a major service. Cosmetic crowns may be excluded, and plans often limit replacement frequency.

Why is my crown cost higher than expected with insurance?

Your crown cost may be higher than expected if your deductible was not met, your annual maximum was reached, the plan pays only a metal crown rate for back teeth, the dentist is out of network, or separate charges such as a core build-up were added.

Are porcelain crowns covered by insurance?

Porcelain crowns are often covered, especially on front teeth. For back teeth, some plans apply an alternate benefit and pay only what a metal crown would cost, leaving you to pay the difference for porcelain or zirconia.

How often will insurance pay for a new crown?

Many dental plans limit replacement crowns on the same tooth to once every several years. Check your plan's frequency limits before replacing an existing crown, unless it has failed and needs urgent replacement.
