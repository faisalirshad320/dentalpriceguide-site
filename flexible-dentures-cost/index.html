Flexible Dentures Cost in 2026: What a Flexible Partial Costs and Whether It Is Worth It

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources

How much do flexible dentures cost?

Flexible dentures cost about $1,700 for a flexible nylon partial denture without insurance, according to GoodRx. That figure is a national estimate, and your local price may be higher or lower depending on the practice and dental lab.

For comparison, Delta Dental 2024 internal data lists standard partial dentures at $1,425 to $2,490 for a lower partial and $1,435 to $2,785 for an upper partial, out-of-network. A flexible partial therefore sits within the normal range for partial dentures rather than at a large premium.

The two sources measure different things. Delta Dental reports out-of-network fee ranges for standard partials, which are often made with a metal framework or acrylic base, while GoodRx gives a single typical self-pay price for a flexible nylon partial. Use both as a reality check when you compare quotes.

What are flexible dentures and silicone dentures?

Flexible dentures are partial dentures made from a thin, bendable nylon-type thermoplastic instead of rigid acrylic or a cast metal frame. The gum-colored base also forms the clasps, which grip around natural teeth near the gumline, so there are no visible metal hooks.

Flexible dentures are mostly used as partials for people who still have some natural teeth. Full flexible dentures exist but are less common, because a full denture needs a rigid base to spread biting pressure across the gums.

People searching for silicone dentures are usually looking for one of two things. Some mean flexible nylon partials, which are often described loosely as silicone. Others mean a soft liner, a cushioning silicone or resilient layer added to the inside of a standard denture to make it gentler on sore gums. Soft liners can be useful but need replacing more often than hard denture bases.

Advantages of flexible partials include:

  • No metal clasps showing when you smile.
  • A lighter, thinner feel that many people find comfortable.
  • Less chance of cracking if dropped.
  • A metal-free option for people with metal sensitivities.

Drawbacks include:

  • Relining and repair are harder than with acrylic, and some labs cannot do them.
  • Adding a tooth later, if another natural tooth is lost, is often impossible, so a new partial may be needed.
  • Less rigid support can mean less stability for chewing in some mouths.
  • The material can stain or roughen over time without careful cleaning.

What is the price of a flexible denture per tooth?

The price of a flexible denture per tooth is not usually quoted, because dentists price flexible partials as one appliance rather than tooth by tooth. A partial replacing one tooth and a partial replacing several teeth on the same arch may cost a similar amount, since most of the cost is the impressions, design, base material and lab work.

A small flexible partial that replaces a single front tooth is sometimes called a flexible flipper. Flippers are often used as a temporary tooth while an implant site heals, and a small appliance may cost less than a larger partial. Ask your dentist for a written quote for the specific design you need.

Price can still rise with size when more teeth, more clasps or a larger base are required, or when both upper and lower partials are made. What affects the cost of flexible dentures?

The cost of flexible dentures depends mainly on the size of the partial, the lab and materials used, the dentist's location and experience, and any treatment needed before the partial is made.

  • Size and design. More replacement teeth and clasps mean more material and lab time.
  • Lab quality. Flexible partials are made by specialized dental labs, and lab fees vary.
  • Location. Practices in large metro areas generally charge more than those in smaller towns.
  • Provider type. A prosthodontist may charge more than a general dentist for complex cases.
  • Preparation. Extractions, fillings or gum treatment on remaining teeth are billed separately.
  • Follow-up visits. Adjustment appointments are usually included for a period, but ask how long.
  • Future upkeep. GoodRx lists a chairside reline at about $350 or more for dentures; flexible partials may not be relinable at all, which affects long-term cost.

Because a flexible partial may need replacing rather than modifying when your mouth changes, consider the lifetime cost, not just the first bill.

Does insurance cover flexible dentures?

Dental insurance often covers part of flexible dentures as a major service, but some plans pay only the amount they would pay for a standard acrylic or metal partial and leave the difference to you. Coverage is limited by the plan's annual maximum, which can be used up quickly by a denture.

Before you commit, check these points with your plan or ask the office for a pre-treatment estimate:

  • Whether flexible partials are covered or replaced with a standard partial benefit, sometimes called an alternate benefit clause.
  • Any waiting period for major services on a new plan.
  • How often the plan will pay for a new denture.
  • Whether your dentist is in-network, which usually lowers the negotiated fee.

Original Medicare generally does not cover dentures, though some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits. Medicaid adult dental coverage varies by state. Self-pay patients should ask for an itemized quote that lists exams, X-rays, extractions and the partial itself.

How can you pay less for flexible dentures or choose a better option?

You can pay less for flexible dentures by using an in-network dentist, getting a quote from a dental school clinic, paying with HSA or FSA funds, and comparing flexible partials against standard partials for your situation.

  • Dental schools. Supervised students often make dentures at reduced fees, with more appointments.
  • HSA or FSA. Dentures are an eligible expense, so pre-tax money lowers the real cost.
  • Payment plans. Many offices offer in-house plans or financing; read the interest terms carefully.
  • Compare designs. A metal-frame partial may cost similar but be easier to repair and add to, which can save money later.

If you need more stability than a partial can give, our guide to snap in dentures explains implant-retained options. GoodRx lists implant-supported full dentures at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance. Healthline compares dentures and implants, and our overview of types of dentures and cost covers every option.

See your dentist if a flexible partial causes sore spots that do not heal within a few days, rubs gums raw, or feels loose. Get prompt care for swelling, fever or a sore that bleeds or lasts more than two weeks. Follow your dentist's cleaning and wear instructions.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a flexible partial denture?

A flexible partial denture costs about $1,700 without insurance, according to GoodRx. Delta Dental lists standard partial dentures at $1,425 to $2,490 for a lower partial and $1,435 to $2,785 for an upper partial out-of-network. Your local price depends on the dentist, lab and size of the partial.

Are flexible dentures better than acrylic or metal partials?

Flexible dentures are not better in every case. They are comfortable, light and have no metal clasps, but they are harder to reline, repair or add teeth to than acrylic or metal-frame partials. Your dentist can advise which design suits your remaining teeth, bite and budget.

How long do flexible dentures last?

Flexible dentures often last several years with good care, but they may need replacing sooner than a metal-frame partial because the material can be difficult to reline or repair. Changes in your gums or loss of another natural tooth may also mean a new partial is needed.

Are silicone dentures the same as flexible dentures?

Silicone dentures and flexible dentures are often confused. Flexible dentures are usually made from a nylon-type thermoplastic. Silicone more often refers to a soft liner added inside a standard denture for comfort. Ask your dentist which material is being used and how it is maintained.

Does insurance pay for flexible partial dentures?

Insurance often pays part of a flexible partial denture as a major service, but some plans pay only the standard partial benefit and leave the difference to you. Annual maximums and waiting periods apply. Ask your dentist to request a pre-treatment estimate before the partial is made.
