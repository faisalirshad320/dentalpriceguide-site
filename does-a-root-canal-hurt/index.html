Root Canal Pain: Does a Root Canal Hurt, and How Long Does Soreness Last?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Root canal pain during treatment is usually no worse than getting a filling, because the tooth is fully numbed with local anesthetic. Mild soreness or tenderness when biting is common for a few days afterward and often eases with over-the-counter pain relief. Delta Dental explains the procedure step by step.

Key takeaways
  • A root canal is done under local anesthesia, so most people feel pressure rather than sharp pain.
  • Tenderness and mild throbbing for a few days after a root canal are common and usually ease steadily.
  • Pain that worsens after several days, swelling or fever needs a prompt call to your dentist.
  • Root canals often relieve the severe toothache caused by an infected or inflamed pulp.
  • A molar root canal averages $1,337 (CareCredit), not including the final crown or filling.

Does a root canal hurt?

A root canal usually does not hurt more than a routine filling, because the dentist numbs the tooth and surrounding gum with local anesthetic before starting. Most people feel pressure, vibration and the sensation of instruments working, but not sharp pain.

The reputation of root canals as painful comes largely from the toothache that leads to one. An infected or inflamed pulp, the soft tissue inside the tooth, can cause intense throbbing, sensitivity to hot and cold, and pain when biting. The root canal removes that pulp, so the procedure often relieves pain rather than causing it.

A very inflamed or infected tooth can sometimes be harder to numb fully. If you feel pain during the procedure, tell your dentist right away; extra anesthetic can usually be given. Sedation such as nitrous oxide may also be available for anxious patients.

To understand each step and why it is done, read our guide on what is a root canal procedure. Knowing how long a root canal takes can also make the appointment feel more manageable.

How much pain is normal after a root canal?

Mild to moderate soreness for a few days after a root canal is normal, especially tenderness when biting and some aching in the gum around the tooth. The discomfort usually improves day by day and often fades within about a week.

Soreness after a root canal comes from inflammation in the tissues around the root tip and from the gum and jaw being held open during treatment. The tooth itself no longer has a living nerve, but the ligament and bone around it can still feel sore while they settle.

Typical post-treatment experiences include:

  • Tenderness when chewing on the treated side for several days.
  • A dull ache that responds to over-the-counter pain relief.
  • Jaw stiffness from keeping your mouth open.
  • Mild gum soreness where the rubber dam clamp sat.

Avoid chewing hard foods on the treated tooth until it has its permanent crown or filling. A tooth with a temporary filling is more fragile and can crack.

How do I stop throbbing pain after a root canal?

Throbbing pain after a root canal can usually be eased with over-the-counter pain relievers as your dentist recommends, rest, a cold compress and avoiding chewing on the tooth. If throbbing is severe or getting worse, call your dentist rather than waiting.

  1. Take pain relief as directed. Many dentists suggest an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen, sometimes alternated with acetaminophen, if those are safe for you. Follow your dentist's dosing advice and the label.
  2. Use a cold compress on the cheek for short periods during the first day or two.
  3. Keep your head raised at night with an extra pillow to reduce pressure.
  4. Chew on the other side and choose soft foods until the tooth is restored.
  5. Check your bite. If the tooth feels like it hits first when you close, the temporary filling may be slightly high; a quick adjustment often relieves the pain.
  6. Take any prescribed antibiotics exactly as directed if your dentist gave them for an infection.

Call your dentist promptly if you have swelling inside or outside the mouth, fever, pain that gets worse after the first few days, a temporary filling that falls out, or signs of an allergic reaction to medication. Seek emergency care for swelling that affects breathing or swallowing.

How can I make a root canal less painful?

A root canal is usually less painful when the tooth is treated early, the anesthetic has time to work fully, and you tell your dentist about any discomfort straight away. A few practical steps help before, during and after the visit.

  • Book promptly. A tooth treated before a large abscess forms is often easier to numb and settles faster afterward.
  • Share your anxiety. Dentists can explain each step, agree on a hand signal for breaks, and offer nitrous oxide or other sedation where appropriate.
  • Ask about pain relief beforehand. Some dentists suggest taking an anti-inflammatory before the appointment if it is safe for you; follow their advice rather than guessing.
  • Eat before the appointment. Numbness can last a few hours afterward, so a meal beforehand helps you avoid biting your cheek or tongue later.
  • Plan a quiet rest of the day. Rest helps the jaw recover from being held open.

Root canal treatment is sometimes split into two visits, especially when infection is present. The tooth may be cleaned and filled with a medicated dressing first, then sealed at a second visit. A tooth can feel slightly tender between visits, which is usually normal. Follow your dentist's instructions on medication and eating during that time.

Many root canals are done by general dentists, while complex cases, such as curved roots or retreatment, may be referred to an endodontist, a dentist who specializes in root canals. Specialist care can make difficult cases more comfortable and predictable.

How painful is a root canal compared with an extraction?

A root canal is generally similar in comfort to an extraction during the procedure, since both use local anesthetic, but a root canal keeps your natural tooth and avoids the gap that an extraction leaves.

After an extraction, the socket has to heal, and the missing tooth may later need an implant, bridge or partial denture. After a root canal, the tooth usually needs a crown, especially a back tooth, to protect it from cracking. The Cleveland Clinic guide to dental crowns explains why.

Some people fear a root canal more than the toothache itself and delay treatment. Waiting can let an infection spread, lead to an abscess and make the tooth harder to save. If you notice lingering sensitivity to hot or cold, pain on biting or a pimple-like bump on the gum, see a dentist soon. What does a root canal cost if you need one?

A root canal costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, about $1,300 for a premolar and about $1,500 for a molar out-of-network, according to Delta Dental's 2025 internal data. These fees do not include the final crown or filling.

CareCredit's cost study reports $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar. Pearl dental cost data, based on ADA fee surveys, gives a national range of $685 to $1,800, with an average of $1,050. The sources differ because Delta Dental reports out-of-network fees from its claims data, while CareCredit and Pearl rely on separate cost surveys. All are national averages, and local prices vary.

Insurance commonly covers 50%–80% after the deductible, according to Delta Dental. Treating a tooth early, before an infection spreads, can also avoid the added cost of an extraction and replacement tooth. See our full guide to root canal cost for details by tooth and payment options.

Frequently asked questions

Is a root canal painful?

A root canal is usually not painful during treatment because the tooth is numbed with local anesthetic, and most people describe it as similar to a filling. Afterward, mild soreness and tenderness when biting are common for a few days. The procedure often relieves the severe toothache caused by an infected pulp.

How long does pain last after a root canal?

Pain after a root canal usually lasts a few days and often fades within about a week, improving a little each day. Tenderness when biting may linger slightly longer until the tooth is fully restored. Pain that gets worse instead of better, or comes with swelling or fever, needs a call to your dentist.

Why does my tooth throb after a root canal?

A tooth can throb after a root canal because the tissues around the root are inflamed from the infection and the treatment. A temporary filling that sits slightly high can also cause pain when biting. Mild throbbing usually settles in days; severe or worsening throbbing should be checked by your dentist.

Can I go to work after a root canal?

Most people can go back to work or school the same day or the next day after a root canal. Your lip and cheek may stay numb for a few hours, so avoid eating until feeling returns. If you had sedation, follow your dentist's instructions about driving and resting for the rest of the day.

When should I worry about pain after a root canal?

Worry about pain after a root canal if it gets worse after the first few days, does not respond to pain relief your dentist recommended, or comes with swelling, fever, pus or an uneven bite. Contact your dentist promptly. Seek emergency care if swelling affects your breathing or swallowing.

Is a root canal worse than a filling?

A root canal usually feels similar to a filling during treatment because both use local anesthetic, though a root canal takes longer. Afterward, a root canal is more likely to leave the tooth tender for a few days. Most people find the relief from toothache outweighs the short-term soreness.
