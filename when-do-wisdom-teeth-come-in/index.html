When Do Wisdom Teeth Come In? Typical Age, First Signs and What Happens Next

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

Wisdom teeth usually come in between ages 17 and 25, making them the last permanent teeth to erupt, according to Cleveland Clinic. Some people get them earlier or later, some never get them, and many have wisdom teeth that stay partly or fully trapped under the gum.

Key takeaways
  • Wisdom teeth (third molars) typically erupt between ages 17 and 25, behind the second molars at the very back of the mouth.
  • First signs include gum tenderness or swelling behind the last molar, jaw ache, and a small white tip breaking through the gum.
  • Wisdom teeth do not grow back after removal, though rarely an extra (supernumerary) tooth can develop.
  • Many wisdom teeth become impacted because the jaw lacks room, which can lead to pain, infection or damage to nearby teeth.
  • Your dentist's X-rays and advice decide whether to monitor or remove them.

What age do wisdom teeth come in?

Wisdom teeth usually come in between ages 17 and 25, which is why they are linked with the start of adulthood. They are the third molars, the last of the 32 permanent teeth, and they develop long after the other molars have settled into place.

Wisdom teeth timing varies a great deal from person to person. Some teenagers see them break through at 16, while others notice nothing until their late twenties. Wisdom tooth buds start forming in the jaw in late childhood, and dentists can often see them on X-rays in the early teens, well before they erupt.

Wisdom teeth do not always come in at all. Some people are born without one or more third molars. Others have wisdom teeth that form fully but never break through because there is no room. In those cases, the teeth may sit quietly for years or cause problems later, so regular dental X-rays matter even if you feel nothing.

Where do wisdom teeth grow?

Wisdom teeth grow at the very back of the mouth, behind the second molars, with one in each corner: upper left, upper right, lower left and lower right. Because they are last in line, they often run out of space in the jaw.

Wisdom teeth can grow in several directions. Ideally they come in upright and line up with the other molars. More often, they tilt forward toward the second molar, tilt backward, lie sideways in the bone, or stay partly covered by gum. Teeth that cannot fully erupt are called impacted, and Cleveland Clinic explains the different impaction types.

Wisdom tooth position matters for both comfort and cost. A tooth angled into the neighboring molar can trap food and lead to decay or gum infection on both teeth. A lower wisdom tooth that sits deep in the bone may be close to the nerve that supplies feeling to the lip and chin, which a dentist checks on imaging before any surgery.

How do I know if my wisdom teeth are coming in?

The most common first signs of wisdom teeth coming in are soreness, swelling or tenderness in the gum behind your last molar, plus a dull ache in the jaw. Many people also see or feel a small white tip of tooth pushing through the gum.

Signs your wisdom teeth are coming in can include:

  • Tender, red or puffy gum at the back of the mouth
  • A dull ache or pressure in the jaw, sometimes spreading toward the ear
  • Headaches or jaw stiffness, especially when chewing
  • A bad taste or bad breath from food trapped under a gum flap
  • Mild difficulty opening the mouth fully
  • A visible tooth edge behind the second molar

Wisdom teeth symptoms like these can come and go over months as the tooth moves. A gum flap over a partly erupted tooth can become inflamed or infected, a condition called pericoronitis. For relief strategies and what different kinds of discomfort can mean, see our guide to wisdom teeth pain.

The only way to know for sure how your wisdom teeth are developing is a dental exam with X-rays. Your dentist can see whether the teeth have room to erupt, how they are angled and whether any are impacted.

See a dentist promptly if you have swelling in the cheek or jaw, fever, pus, trouble opening your mouth, or pain when swallowing. These can be signs of an infection around a wisdom tooth that needs treatment.

When do wisdom teeth stop growing?

Wisdom teeth generally stop growing once their roots are fully formed, which usually happens by the mid-twenties. After that point, the tooth may still shift slightly, but it no longer develops further.

Wisdom teeth root development is one reason dentists often recommend evaluating them in the late teens or early twenties. When roots are still short, removal tends to be simpler and recovery often goes more smoothly. Fully formed roots can be longer, curved or close to nerves, which can make surgery more involved.

Wisdom teeth that are stuck do not keep growing indefinitely, but they can still cause trouble later in life. Cysts can form around impacted teeth, and decay or gum disease can affect the neighboring molar. That is why your dentist may keep watching wisdom teeth that are not causing symptoms.

Wisdom teeth monitoring usually means a panoramic X-ray every few years, or sooner if you notice pain or swelling. Your dentist will compare images over time to see whether the teeth are moving, whether the surrounding bone looks healthy and whether the neighboring molars are being affected. Follow your dentist's advice on how often to check.

Can wisdom teeth grow back?

Wisdom teeth cannot grow back once they have been removed, because each permanent tooth forms only once. When a dentist or oral surgeon removes a wisdom tooth, the root and crown are gone for good.

A wisdom tooth growing back is usually one of three things. Rarely, a person has an extra tooth, called a supernumerary or fourth molar, which can show up later. Sometimes a small piece of root was intentionally left in place during surgery to protect a nerve, and it can work its way toward the surface. And occasionally, a sharp bone fragment near the socket can feel like a new tooth.

Wisdom teeth that were never removed can also seem to appear suddenly. A tooth that sat under the gum for years can partly erupt in adulthood. If you feel something new and hard at the back of your mouth, a quick dental exam and X-ray will tell you what it is.

What does it cost if your wisdom teeth need to come out?

Removing all four wisdom teeth averages $720 out of network when they are fully erupted and $3,120 when they need surgery with sedation, according to Delta Dental's 2021 data. One surgical wisdom tooth averages $550. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Impacted wisdom teeth tend to cost more. The CareCredit 2023–2024 cost survey puts a single impacted wisdom tooth at an average of $835 and four impacted teeth at $3,340. Delta Dental's figures reflect out-of-network claims, while CareCredit's come from a survey of average prices, so they are not directly comparable. Delta Dental notes that insurance typically covers about 50% to 80% of the total cost.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do wisdom teeth come in?

Wisdom teeth usually come in between ages 17 and 25, according to Cleveland Clinic. Timing varies widely, so some people notice them a little earlier or later. Some people never develop wisdom teeth, and others have teeth that stay trapped under the gum or bone and never fully erupt.

How do you know if your wisdom teeth are coming in?

You may notice tenderness or swelling in the gum behind your last molar, a dull jaw ache, mild headaches, a bad taste from trapped food, or a small white tooth tip breaking through. A dental exam with X-rays is the only way to confirm how the teeth are positioned and whether they have room.

Can wisdom teeth come in at 30?

Wisdom teeth can occasionally erupt or partly break through after age 25, including around 30, especially if a tooth sat under the gum for years. Late eruption is less common and often means the tooth is angled or lacks room. A dentist should check it with an X-ray to rule out problems.

Do wisdom teeth always need to be removed?

Wisdom teeth do not always need to be removed. Healthy teeth that erupt fully, line up properly and can be cleaned well may stay, with regular monitoring. Removal is usually advised for impacted teeth, repeated infections, decay, cysts or damage to neighboring teeth, based on your dentist's exam.

Is it normal for wisdom teeth to hurt when coming in?

Mild soreness and gum tenderness are common while wisdom teeth come in, and the discomfort may come and go. Severe pain, facial swelling, fever, pus or trouble opening your mouth are not normal and may mean infection. See a dentist promptly if you notice any of those signs.

Can you have more than four wisdom teeth?

Some people do have more than four wisdom teeth, though it is uncommon. Extra teeth beyond the normal set are called supernumerary teeth, and an extra molar behind a wisdom tooth is sometimes called a fourth molar. A panoramic X-ray shows exactly how many teeth are developing.
