Types of Dental Crowns and Cost: What a Crown Really Costs in 2026

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Types of dental crowns and cost vary widely: Delta Dental puts a permanent crown at $1,100 to $2,000 out-of-network, while CareCredit reports averages of $1,399 for porcelain, $1,211 for metal and $697 for resin or temporary crowns. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • A permanent crown typically costs $1,100 to $2,000 before insurance, according to Delta Dental.
  • Porcelain crowns average more than metal crowns, and resin or temporary crowns cost the least.
  • Many dental plans treat crowns as a major procedure and pay 50% on a 100-80-50 plan.
  • Extra work such as a core buildup, root canal or same-day milling can raise the total bill.
  • Ask for an itemized treatment estimate with procedure codes before you agree to a crown.

How much does a dental crown cost?

A dental crown usually costs between $1,100 and $2,000 per tooth before insurance, based on Delta Dental data on out-of-network permanent crowns. That range covers the crown itself, the tooth preparation and the impressions or digital scan that go into making it.

A crown cost survey from CareCredit lands in a similar place. CareCredit lists an average of $1,399 for a porcelain crown, $1,211 for a metallic crown and $697 for a resin-based or temporary crown. All of these are national averages, so the quote from a dentist in your town may be higher or lower.

The price of a crown for a tooth depends heavily on what else that tooth needs. A crown placed on a healthy, previously filled tooth is a single visit fee plus a lab fee. A crown that follows a root canal, or one that needs a core buildup first, comes with separate charges that are billed under their own procedure codes.

Quick rule of thumb: when someone asks "how much is a crown," the honest answer is a range, not a single number. Ask your dentist for an itemized estimate that lists the crown, any buildup, any temporary crown and any X-rays separately.

Crown cost also depends on whether your dentist is in your insurance network. In-network dentists agree to a set fee schedule with the insurer, so the same crown can cost less at an in-network office than at an out-of-network office down the street.

Types of dental crowns and cost by material

The main types of dental crowns are porcelain or ceramic, zirconia, metal (including gold), porcelain fused to metal, and resin or temporary crowns, and each material sits at a different price point. Material choice is one of the biggest drivers of what you pay, along with where the tooth sits in your mouth.

  • Porcelain and all-ceramic crowns: CareCredit reports an average of $1,399 for a porcelain crown. All-ceramic crowns match natural tooth color well, which is why dentists often recommend them for teeth that show when you smile.
  • Zirconia crowns: Zirconia is a very strong ceramic that can be milled in a lab or in the office. The zirconia crown price usually sits in the same general band as other ceramic crowns, though it varies by lab, by brand and by whether the crown is layered with porcelain for a more lifelike look.
  • Metal crowns: CareCredit lists an average of $1,211 for a metallic crown. Metal crowns need less tooth removal and resist chipping, so they are a common choice for back molars that take heavy chewing forces.
  • Gold crowns: Gold alloy crowns are a type of metal crown. Their price moves with the alloy content and the market price of precious metals; our guide on how much a gold tooth costs covers that in detail.
  • Porcelain fused to metal (PFM): A PFM crown has a metal base covered with tooth-colored porcelain. PFM crowns are strong and look natural from a distance, but a dark line can show at the gumline over time.
  • Resin and temporary crowns: CareCredit lists an average of $697 for a resin-based or temporary crown. Resin crowns wear faster and are often used short term while a permanent crown is being made.

The cheapest crown is not always the best value. A resin crown that needs replacing sooner can end up costing more over time than a porcelain or metal crown that lasts for many years. Your dentist will weigh the tooth's position, your bite and any grinding habit when suggesting a material.

Why do dental crown price estimates differ?

Dental crown price estimates differ because each source measures something different. Delta Dental's $1,100 to $2,000 range comes from its own claims data for out-of-network permanent crowns, while CareCredit's averages come from a cost study of what patients paid for specific crown types.

Claims data reflects what dentists actually bill an insurer for a crown procedure in a given year. A cost survey such as the Synchrony study behind the CareCredit list reports average procedure prices by type, which is why it can break out porcelain, metal and resin crowns separately.

Neither number is wrong. Use the Delta Dental range as a realistic band for a permanent crown when you pay full price out-of-network, and use the CareCredit averages to compare materials against each other. Then check both against the written estimate from your own dentist, because local fees are what you will pay.

What changes the price of a crown?

The price of a dental crown changes with the material, the tooth's location, the lab or milling method, extra treatment the tooth needs, and where you live. Two people can get a crown on the same day and pay very different amounts for these reasons.

  • Tooth location: Front teeth usually need a more cosmetic, closely color-matched crown. See our guide to front tooth crown cost for what that means for price.
  • Material: Porcelain and zirconia generally cost more than resin, and gold prices track the metal market.
  • Lab-made vs same-day: Some offices mill a ceramic crown in a single visit. Same-day crowns save a second appointment and the temporary crown, but the in-office equipment cost can be built into the fee.
  • Core buildup: If a large part of the tooth is missing, the dentist may rebuild the core before placing the crown. A buildup is billed separately.
  • Root canal treatment: A tooth with infected or inflamed pulp may need a root canal first, which adds a separate fee on top of the crown.
  • Region and office type: Fees in large cities and high-cost areas tend to run higher than in smaller towns. Specialist prosthodontists usually charge more than general dentists.
  • Imaging: X-rays or a 3D scan before treatment may be billed on their own line.

Ask your dentist which of these items apply to your tooth before treatment starts. A crown quote that leaves out the buildup or temporary crown can look cheaper than it is.

How much is a crown with insurance?

A crown with insurance usually costs noticeably less than the full fee, because many dental plans classify crowns as a major procedure and pay 50% on a 100-80-50 plan, according to Delta Dental. Under that structure, preventive care is paid at the highest level, basic care at the middle level and major care such as crowns at the lowest level.

Your actual share depends on four plan details: the coinsurance level for major services, your remaining deductible, your annual maximum, and any waiting period on major work. Many plans have an annual maximum that a single crown plus a root canal can use up quickly.

Insurers may also apply a missing tooth clause, a replacement rule for crowns placed within a certain number of years, or a downgrade clause. A downgrade clause means the plan pays the rate for a less expensive material, such as metal, even if you choose porcelain on a back tooth, and you pay the difference.

Our full guide to tooth crown cost with insurance walks through how deductibles, annual maximums and downgrade clauses change your out-of-pocket bill, with worked examples you can apply to your own estimate.

Ask your dentist's office for a pre-treatment estimate (sometimes called a predetermination). The insurer reviews the planned crown and tells you in writing what it expects to pay before any work begins.

How much does a tooth crown cost without insurance?

A tooth crown without insurance typically costs $1,100 to $2,000 per tooth based on Delta Dental's out-of-network data, with CareCredit reporting averages of $1,399 for porcelain, $1,211 for metal and $697 for resin or temporary crowns. Without insurance, you pay the dentist's full fee for every line on the estimate.

Paying cash is not always the most expensive route. Some offices offer a cash discount, and many run in-house membership plans that bundle cleanings and exams with a discount on restorative work such as crowns. Ask the front desk whether either option exists before you book.

Discount dental plans are another option for people without coverage. A discount plan is not insurance; you pay an annual fee and get reduced prices at participating dentists, usually with no waiting period and no annual maximum. Compare the discounted crown fee with the plan's yearly cost before signing up.

If you are quoted a crown fee well above the national ranges, it is reasonable to ask why. The tooth may need a buildup, a specialist may be involved, or the office may use a premium lab. A second opinion from another dentist is a normal step for any major dental work.

How can you pay less for a dental crown?

You can pay less for a dental crown by using in-network dentists, timing treatment around your insurance year, using pre-tax health accounts, and considering dental schools or payment plans. Several of these can be combined on the same crown.

  1. Stay in-network. In-network dentists charge the insurer's negotiated fee, which is usually lower than their standard fee. Check your plan's online directory before booking.
  2. Time treatment across plan years. If you need several crowns, splitting the work across two plan years can let you use two annual maximums. Only do this if your dentist agrees the delay is safe for the teeth involved.
  3. Use an HSA or FSA. Crowns are a qualified medical expense, so you can pay with pre-tax money from a health savings account or flexible spending account.
  4. Look at dental schools. Accredited dental schools often charge less because students treat patients under faculty supervision. Appointments take longer and may need more visits.
  5. Ask about payment plans. Many offices offer in-house installment plans, and some accept healthcare credit cards. Read the terms carefully, since deferred-interest offers can charge back interest if the balance is not paid in full on time.
  6. Ask about material options. On a back molar, a metal crown may cost less than porcelain and perform well. Let your dentist explain the trade-offs rather than picking on price alone.

Avoid putting off a crown that your dentist says is needed to save money in the short term. A cracked or heavily filled tooth that breaks further may need a root canal or an extraction and replacement, both of which cost more than the original crown.

What happens during a dental crown procedure?

A dental crown procedure usually takes two visits: one to shape the tooth and fit a temporary crown, and a second to cement the permanent crown, as Cleveland Clinic describes. Offices with in-house milling can sometimes complete a ceramic crown in one appointment.

  1. Exam and X-rays: The dentist checks the tooth's roots and surrounding bone and confirms a crown is the right treatment.
  2. Numbing: A local anesthetic numbs the tooth and gum.
  3. Tooth preparation: The dentist removes decay and reshapes the outer layer of the tooth so the crown can fit over it. A buildup may be placed if too little tooth remains.
  4. Impression or scan: A physical impression or digital scan records the shape of the prepared tooth and your bite.
  5. Temporary crown: A temporary crown protects the tooth while the lab makes the permanent one.
  6. Final fitting: At the second visit, the dentist removes the temporary, checks the fit and color of the permanent crown, adjusts the bite and cements it in place.

If you want the basics first, our explainer on what a dental crown is covers why dentists recommend crowns and how they compare with fillings and onlays.

Mild sensitivity and gum soreness for a few days after a crown visit are common. Follow your dentist's instructions on eating, cleaning around the temporary crown and any pain relief.

When should you call a dentist about a crown?

You should call your dentist promptly if a crown or temporary crown comes loose, feels high when you bite, or causes pain that gets worse instead of better. A loose temporary crown leaves the prepared tooth exposed, so it should be re-cemented quickly.

Keep a dislodged crown in a clean container and bring it to the appointment. Do not try to glue a crown back on with household adhesive, because that can damage the crown and the tooth.

Seek urgent dental or medical care for facial swelling, fever, a bad taste with swelling around the tooth, bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure, or severe pain that over-the-counter pain relief does not control. These can be signs of infection that needs prompt treatment.

Dental crowns can last for many years with good care. Brush twice a day, clean between teeth daily, avoid chewing ice or hard objects, and wear a night guard if your dentist says you grind your teeth. Regular checkups let your dentist spot wear or decay at the crown edge early, when repairs cost less.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a crown for a tooth?

A crown for a tooth typically costs $1,100 to $2,000 out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports national averages of $1,399 for porcelain, $1,211 for metal and $697 for resin or temporary crowns. Local prices vary, and extra work such as a buildup or root canal is billed separately.

What is the cheapest type of dental crown?

The cheapest type of dental crown is usually a resin-based crown, which CareCredit lists at an average of $697. Resin crowns wear faster than porcelain or metal, so dentists often use them as temporary crowns. A longer-lasting crown can be better value over time, so ask your dentist about the trade-offs.

Is a porcelain crown more expensive than a metal crown?

A porcelain crown usually costs more than a metal crown. CareCredit reports an average of $1,399 for porcelain and $1,211 for a metallic crown. Porcelain matches tooth color, while metal is very durable and needs less tooth removal, which makes it a common choice for back molars.

Does dental insurance cover crowns?

Dental insurance often covers crowns as a major procedure. Delta Dental notes that a 100-80-50 plan pays 50% for major procedures such as crowns. Your deductible, annual maximum, waiting periods and any downgrade clause also affect what you pay, so request a pre-treatment estimate first.

How much is a zirconia crown?

A zirconia crown is usually priced in the same general range as other ceramic crowns, and the Delta Dental range of $1,100 to $2,000 for a permanent crown is a reasonable reference. The exact price depends on the lab, whether the crown is layered with porcelain, and local fees.

Can I use my HSA or FSA to pay for a crown?

Yes, a dental crown is generally a qualified medical expense, so you can pay with money from a health savings account or flexible spending account. Using pre-tax funds lowers the effective cost. Keep your itemized receipt in case your account administrator asks for documentation.
