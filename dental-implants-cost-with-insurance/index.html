What Insurance Covers Dental Implants, and What You Will Still Pay

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 26, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

Some dental insurance plans cover implants, usually as major work at 50% after the deductible, but only up to the plan's annual maximum (for example $1,500), according to Delta Dental. With one implant costing $2,800 to $5,600 without benefits, most patients still pay a large share themselves.

Key takeaways
  • Many dental plans treat implants as major work and pay about 50% (100-80-50 structure).
  • The annual maximum, often around $1,500, caps what the plan pays each year.
  • A single implant costs $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits (Delta Dental).
  • Some plans exclude implants or cover only the crown on top.
  • Medical insurance pays only in limited cases such as accidents or certain medical conditions.

Does dental insurance cover dental implants?

Dental insurance covers dental implants on some plans but not all. Many modern plans now include implants as a major service, while older or lower-cost plans may exclude them entirely or pay only for the crown that sits on top of the implant.

When a plan does cover implants, it usually follows what Delta Dental calls a 100-80-50 structure: preventive care at 100%, basic care at 80%, and major work such as implants, crowns and dentures at 50%. That 50% applies after your deductible, and only until you reach the plan's annual maximum. Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example annual maximum.

Read your plan's benefits summary for the words "implants", "prosthodontics" and "missing tooth clause". A missing tooth clause means the plan will not pay to replace a tooth that was lost before your coverage began.

How much will you pay for an implant with insurance?

With insurance, you usually still pay most of the cost of a dental implant because the annual maximum is small compared with the fee. Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600 for a patient without dental benefits.

Here is how the arithmetic tends to work. Your plan pays its share of the allowed fee for each part of the treatment until it hits the annual maximum. Once the maximum is reached, you pay the rest of that year's work yourself. Because a single implant can exceed a typical yearly maximum, the plan often contributes a fixed chunk and you cover the balance.

In-network dentists charge negotiated fees, which can lower your share compared with out-of-network prices. Ask the office for a pre-treatment estimate from your insurer so you see the exact covered amount in writing. Our dental implant cost guide covers full pricing.

Can you spread implant treatment over two plan years?

Spreading dental implant treatment across two plan years can let you use two annual maximums instead of one. Implant treatment naturally happens in stages: extraction and grafting, implant placement, a healing period of several months, and then the abutment and crown.

Because of that healing gap, some patients have the implant placed late in one benefit year and the crown fitted early in the next. Each stage is billed when it is completed, so each year's maximum can apply to a different part of the work. Ask your dentist and insurer whether your plan allows this and how each stage will be billed.

Check waiting periods too. Many new plans make you wait six to twelve months before they pay for major services like implants, so buying a policy right before treatment rarely helps.

What parts of implant treatment are billed separately?

Dental implant treatment is billed in several parts, and insurance may treat each part differently. Knowing the pieces helps you read an estimate.

  • Imaging: a cone-beam CT scan averages $466, according to CareCredit.
  • Extraction: removing the old tooth if it is still present.
  • Bone graft: CareCredit reports averages of $811 for an allograft and $2,779 for an autograft. The Cleveland Clinic explains when grafts are needed.
  • Implant post: the titanium or zirconia screw placed in the bone.
  • Abutment and crown: the connector and the visible tooth.

Some plans cover the crown but not the post, or cover grafts under oral surgery benefits. Your estimate should list each code so you can see what is paid.

What should you ask your insurer before implant treatment?

Asking your insurer the right questions before implant treatment prevents surprise bills. Call the member services number on your card, or have the dental office do it, and get the answers in writing where possible.

  • Are implants a covered benefit, or only the crown on top?
  • What percentage is paid for major services, and does it apply to implants?
  • What is my annual maximum, and how much of it is left this year?
  • Is there a waiting period for major services, and has mine ended?
  • Is there a missing tooth clause for teeth lost before my coverage started?
  • Are bone grafts and scans covered, and under which category?
  • Does my plan apply an alternate benefit, paying only what a bridge or denture would cost?

The alternate benefit question is easy to overlook. Some plans cover implants only up to the amount they would pay for a cheaper option, such as a bridge, and leave you responsible for the difference. A pre-treatment estimate will reveal this before you commit.

Can medical insurance cover dental implants?

Medical insurance covers dental implants only in limited circumstances. Medical plans may help when teeth are lost in an accident, when reconstruction follows cancer surgery or other medical treatment, or when a congenital condition is involved.

Getting medical coverage usually requires a letter of medical necessity from your surgeon, supporting records and sometimes prior authorization. Even then, medical plans may cover only part of the surgery, such as the bone graft or hospital-based procedure, rather than the crown. Call your medical insurer and ask specifically about oral surgery and reconstructive benefits before treatment.

Original Medicare generally does not cover routine dental implants. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits, and their implant coverage varies widely.

What if your insurance will not pay for implants?

If your insurance will not pay for implants, compare the cost of other tooth replacement options that your plan may cover, and look at ways to lower the implant price. Bridges and dentures are often covered as major services even on plans that exclude implants.

Healthline's implant versus bridge guide and its dentures versus implants comparison outline the trade-offs. Dental schools, financing plans and health savings or flexible spending accounts can also make implants more manageable. Be cautious with ads for very cheap implants and read our guide to heavily discounted dental implant offers before booking. Frequently asked questions

Does dental insurance cover implants?

Some dental insurance plans cover implants, typically at about 50% as a major service after the deductible, up to the annual maximum. Others exclude implants or cover only the crown. Check your benefits summary for implant coverage, waiting periods and any missing tooth clause.

How much is a tooth implant with insurance?

A tooth implant with insurance usually still costs a few thousand dollars out of pocket, because plans often pay about 50% only up to an annual maximum such as $1,500. Without benefits, Delta Dental puts one implant at $2,800 to $5,600. A pre-treatment estimate gives your exact share.

Why won't my insurance cover implants?

Insurance may not cover implants because the plan excludes them, treats them as cosmetic, has a missing tooth clause for teeth lost before coverage began, or still has you in a waiting period for major services. The plan may cover a bridge or denture instead.

Can I get dental implants covered by medical insurance?

Medical insurance may cover dental implants when tooth loss results from an accident, cancer treatment or another medical condition. It usually requires a letter of medical necessity and prior authorization. Routine tooth loss from decay or gum disease is rarely covered by medical plans.

Is there dental insurance with no waiting period for implants?

Some dental plans advertise no waiting period for major services, but they often have lower annual maximums, higher premiums or exclusions for implants. Compare the yearly cost of the plan with the benefit it would actually pay for your treatment before you buy.
