How Much Are Braces? Average Braces Cost by Type, Insurance and Age

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

How much are braces? Traditional metal braces average $6,343 in the US, according to CareCredit, and Pearl's ADA-based data puts comprehensive treatment at $3,680 to $9,650 nationally. Ceramic braces and clear aligners usually average less than metal, lingual braces more. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • Metal braces average $6,343 nationally, ceramic braces $5,834, clear aligners $5,108 and lingual braces $9,221 (CareCredit).
  • Comprehensive orthodontic treatment ranges from $3,680 to $9,650 nationally, with an average of $5,645 (Pearl, ADA fee survey based).
  • Case complexity, treatment length, your region and the type of appliance drive most of the difference in price.
  • Orthodontic insurance coverage is often limited to children and capped by a separate lifetime maximum, so check your plan before you sign.
  • Payment plans, HSA/FSA funds and university orthodontic clinics can lower what you pay out of pocket.

How much do braces cost on average?

Braces cost about $6,343 on average for traditional metal brackets in the US, based on the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study published by CareCredit. That figure covers a full course of orthodontic treatment, not a single visit, and it is a national average rather than a quote you should expect from every office.

Braces pricing from a second data set lands in a similar zone. Pearl, which draws on ADA fee survey data, lists comprehensive braces (billing code D8080) at $3,680 to $9,650 nationally, with an average of $5,645. A third reference, the Delta Dental parent guide, puts traditional metal braces at $3,000 to $7,000.

Braces quotes in your own town can sit above or below all of these numbers. Big-city practices with high rent and staff costs tend to charge more, while smaller markets and teaching clinics often charge less. The most useful thing you can do with the averages is use them as a sense check: if a quote sits far outside these ranges, ask the orthodontist to walk you through exactly what it includes.

Most orthodontists quote one flat fee for the whole treatment. That fee usually bundles the brackets, wire changes and adjustment visits, but records, X-rays, emergency repairs and retainers may be billed separately. Always ask for the itemized list.

How much do braces cost by type?

Braces cost varies by type, with clear aligners averaging $5,108, ceramic braces $5,834, metal braces $6,343 and lingual braces $9,221, according to CareCredit's national cost study. The order surprises some people, because ceramic braces are often assumed to cost more than metal. National averages blend many different cases, so the gap between metal and ceramic in any one office may run the other way.

  • Metal braces: Stainless steel brackets bonded to the front of the teeth and joined by an archwire. Metal braces handle almost every type of bite problem and are the reference point most offices price against. Delta Dental lists them at $3,000 to $7,000.
  • Ceramic braces: Tooth-colored or clear brackets that work like metal braces but blend in better. Ceramic braces average $5,834 per CareCredit. The brackets are a little larger and can chip, so some orthodontists use them only on the upper front teeth.
  • Lingual braces: Brackets bonded to the back (tongue side) of the teeth so they are hidden from view. Lingual braces are custom-made and harder to adjust, which is why they cost the most: $9,221 on average per CareCredit and $8,000 to $10,000 per Delta Dental.
  • Clear aligners: A series of removable plastic trays changed every week or two. Aligners average $5,108 per CareCredit, but they depend on you wearing them most of the day.

Braces choice should follow the bite problem first and appearance second. Complex rotations, large gaps or jaw-growth issues are sometimes easier to treat with fixed brackets. Our guide to the types of braces compares how each option works, and the page on clear braces breaks down the less visible choices in more detail.

Why do braces cost estimates differ between sources?

Braces cost estimates differ between sources because each publisher measures something slightly different. The figures on this page are all real national data, but they come from different methods, years and billing definitions.

  • CareCredit reports averages from the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study (2023–2024), which looks at what patients paid for specific procedures such as metal braces, ceramic braces or clear aligners.
  • Pearl reports a national range for one billing code, D8080 (comprehensive orthodontic treatment), built on ADA fee survey data. A fee survey records what offices charge, which is not always what patients end up paying after discounts or insurance.
  • Delta Dental gives broad consumer ranges in a parent guide to help families plan, rather than a precise average.

Braces estimates also depend on what is bundled. One office may include records, X-rays and the first set of retainers in its fee while another bills them separately, so two quotes that look far apart can end up close once you add everything. When comparing offers, line up the same items side by side: total fee, down payment, monthly amount, number of months, what happens if treatment runs long, and whether retainers are included.

What changes the price of braces?

The price of braces changes mainly with how complex your bite problem is, how long treatment will take, where you live and which appliance you choose. Two people in the same city can receive quotes thousands of dollars apart for good reasons.

  • Case complexity: Mild crowding needs fewer adjustments than a deep bite, crossbite or open bite. Severe cases may need extra appliances such as expanders or headgear braces, which add to the fee.
  • Treatment length: Longer treatment means more adjustment visits and more chair time. Our page on how long braces take explains the typical timelines and what slows them down.
  • Age: Children whose jaws are still growing can sometimes be treated more efficiently. Adults may need more careful planning, especially if they have crowns, missing teeth or gum disease.
  • Location: Fees in large metro areas tend to run higher than in smaller towns, reflecting rent and staffing costs.
  • Provider: A board-certified orthodontist, a general dentist offering orthodontics and a university clinic will all price differently.
  • Add-ons: Elastics, power chains and bracket colors are usually included, but replacing lost aligners, broken brackets or retainers may cost extra.

Braces add-ons such as rubber bands for braces and power chain braces are normal parts of treatment that close gaps and correct the bite. Ask at the consultation whether they, and any repairs, are part of your flat fee.

Does dental insurance cover braces?

Dental insurance covers braces only when your plan includes orthodontic benefits, and many adult plans do not. Orthodontics is usually listed as a separate benefit from cleanings, fillings and major work, so a plan that pays well for crowns may pay nothing toward braces.

Braces coverage, when a plan offers it, commonly comes with limits worth reading closely. Typical features include an age cutoff (coverage for dependent children only), a lifetime orthodontic maximum that is separate from the yearly dental maximum, waiting periods before benefits start, and payments spread across the treatment months rather than paid all at once. Delta Dental's guide to what dental insurance covers explains how these plan categories are structured.

Braces without insurance means you pay the full orthodontic fee yourself, usually through a down payment and monthly installments. If you are weighing both routes, our page on how much braces cost without insurance compares the two in detail.

Before you sign a treatment contract, ask the orthodontist's office to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer. The response shows what the plan will pay, over what schedule, and what your share will be.

How much do braces cost for kids vs adults?

Braces for kids and adults fall within the same national price ranges, but the total often differs because of treatment length, insurance and the extra planning adults may need. There is no separate national average for each age group in the data we use, so treat any age-based quote as specific to that office.

Braces for children usually start with an early check. The American Association of Orthodontists recommends a first orthodontic evaluation no later than age 7, as noted by Delta Dental. That visit does not mean a child will get braces at 7; most children are simply monitored until more adult teeth come in. Some need an early phase of treatment, which can add a second fee later. Our guide on when kids should get braces covers the timing.

Braces for adults work the same way biologically, but adult bone is denser and teeth may move more slowly. Adults are also more likely to have fillings, crowns or gum recession that affect planning, and they are less likely to have orthodontic insurance. Read more on the cost of braces for adults, including options adults often choose for appearance at work.

How can you pay less for braces?

You can pay less for braces by using any orthodontic insurance you have, choosing an in-network provider, considering a university clinic, spreading payments with an in-house plan and paying with pre-tax HSA or FSA money. None of these change the clinical work, but together they can noticeably lower your real cost.

  1. Check your benefits first. Confirm whether your plan has orthodontic coverage, the age limit, the lifetime maximum and any waiting period.
  2. Stay in network. In-network orthodontists have agreed fees with your insurer, which usually leaves you with a lower balance than an out-of-network office.
  3. Ask about dental school clinics. University orthodontic residency programs treat patients under faculty supervision, often at reduced fees. Treatment can take longer and appointments are less flexible.
  4. Use an in-house payment plan. Many orthodontists split the fee into a down payment plus monthly payments over the treatment period, often without interest. Ask whether paying in full earns a discount.
  5. Pay with HSA or FSA funds. Orthodontic treatment for medical or functional reasons generally qualifies, which lets you pay with pre-tax dollars. Time FSA contributions to match the start of treatment.
  6. Get two or three consultations. Free consultations are common. Compare the treatment plan as well as the price, and ask each office what is and is not included.

Braces offered at a very low price deserve a closer look. Make sure the fee includes in-person monitoring by a licensed dentist or orthodontist, X-rays before treatment, and retainers at the end.

What happens when you get braces?

Getting braces starts with a consultation and records (photos, X-rays and impressions or a scan), followed by a bonding appointment where the brackets are glued on and the wire is placed. Bonding usually takes one to two hours and does not require needles or numbing.

Braces then work by applying steady, gentle pressure that remodels the bone around each tooth so it can shift. Our explainer on how braces work, and when braces were invented, covers the biology and history. Most patients return every several weeks for adjustments, when the orthodontist changes wires, adds elastics or swaps the small ties around each bracket. Many offices let you choose braces colors for those ties at each visit.

Braces soreness is common for a few days after bonding and after adjustments. Teeth feel tender to bite on, and the cheeks may rub on the brackets until they toughen up. Over-the-counter pain relievers used as directed, soft foods and orthodontic wax usually help. Our page on whether braces hurt explains what is normal.

How do you care for teeth with braces?

Caring for teeth with braces means brushing after meals, cleaning between brackets every day, avoiding hard and sticky foods and keeping every adjustment appointment. Plaque collects around brackets easily, and decay or white spots around them can be permanent.

  • Brush around each bracket, above and below the wire, with a soft brush and fluoride toothpaste.
  • Use a floss threader, orthodontic floss, interdental brushes or a water flosser. See our guide to floss for braces for step-by-step technique.
  • Skip hard, sticky and chewy foods such as hard candy, popcorn kernels, ice, caramel and whole apples bitten straight on. Our list of what you can not eat with braces covers the details.
  • Wear a mouthguard for contact sports.
  • Keep regular dental cleanings during treatment; the orthodontist does not replace your general dentist.

Broken brackets are a common reason for extra visits and, in some offices, extra fees. Careful eating protects both your teeth and your budget.

Call your orthodontist promptly for a broken bracket or a wire poking the cheek; orthodontic wax can cover it until then. Seek urgent dental or medical care for facial swelling, fever, a tooth that has been knocked loose, or severe pain that pain relievers do not control.

Follow your orthodontist's instructions on care; they know the details of your appliance and treatment plan.

What costs come after braces come off?

The main cost after braces come off is retainers, which hold teeth in their new positions and are needed long term. Teeth tend to drift back after treatment, especially in the first months, so skipping the retainer can undo results you paid for.

Retainers come as removable clear trays, removable wire retainers or a thin wire bonded behind the front teeth. Some orthodontists include the first set in the treatment fee, while others bill separately, and replacements for lost or broken retainers are almost always extra. Our guide to retainers after braces explains the options and how long to wear them.

Braces aftercare also includes follow-up retainer checks and your usual dental cleanings. Budget for an eventual replacement retainer, and keep removable retainers in their case rather than wrapped in a napkin, which is how many get thrown away.

Frequently asked questions

How much are braces on average?

Braces average $6,343 for traditional metal brackets in the US, according to CareCredit's national cost study. Pearl's ADA-based data puts comprehensive orthodontic treatment at $3,680 to $9,650, with an average of $5,645. These are national averages; your local quote may be higher or lower depending on your case, region and provider.

Can you get braces with a crown?

Yes, you can usually get braces with a crown. Brackets bond less reliably to porcelain or metal crowns than to natural enamel, so the orthodontist may use a special bonding technique or a band around the tooth. Crowned teeth move like natural teeth. Tell your orthodontist about any crowns, implants or bridges at the consultation, because implants cannot be moved.

How much do braces cost in Texas?

Braces in Texas fall within the same national ranges, but we do not have state-specific data. Nationally, metal braces average $6,343 (CareCredit) and comprehensive treatment runs $3,680 to $9,650 (Pearl). Prices in large metro areas tend to be higher than in smaller towns, so compare consultations from two or three local offices.

Are ceramic braces more expensive than metal braces?

Ceramic braces are not always more expensive than metal braces. CareCredit's national averages list ceramic braces at $5,834 and metal braces at $6,343, though many individual offices charge more for ceramic. The price in your area depends on the practice and your case, so ask each office to quote both options side by side.

How can I straighten my teeth without braces?

Clear aligners are the main way to straighten teeth without traditional brackets, averaging $5,108 nationally according to CareCredit. Veneers or bonding can change how mildly crooked teeth look but do not move them. Aligners are not suitable for every bite problem, so an orthodontic exam is the right first step before choosing any option.

Do payment plans for braces charge interest?

Many orthodontists offer in-house payment plans without interest, spreading the fee into a down payment and monthly installments over the treatment period. Third-party financing may charge interest, especially if a promotional period ends before the balance is paid. Read the terms carefully and ask whether paying in full earns a discount before choosing a plan.
