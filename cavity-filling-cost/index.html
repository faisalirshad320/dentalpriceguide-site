How Much Does a Filling Cost? Cavity Filling Prices by Material and Insurance in 2026

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A filling costs about $145 to $480 for a tooth-colored composite on a front tooth ($165 to $530 on a back tooth) without insurance, and $110 to $455 for a silver amalgam, according to Delta Dental. Back teeth and fillings that cover more tooth surfaces cost more. Insurance typically pays 50%–80% for fillings, and prices in your area may differ from these national figures.

Key takeaways
  • Composite (tooth-colored) fillings cost about $145 to $480 on front teeth and $165 to $530 on back teeth without insurance (Delta Dental).
  • Silver amalgam fillings cost $110 to $455 depending on surfaces, and average $139 in the CareCredit cost study.
  • Insurance typically covers 50%–80% of a filling as a basic procedure.
  • The more tooth surfaces a filling covers, the higher the price.
  • Treating a cavity early keeps it a filling; waiting can turn it into a crown or root canal.

How much does a filling cost?

A dental filling costs about $110 to $455 for silver amalgam and $145 to $480 (front) or $165 to $530 (back) for composite, without insurance, based on Delta Dental's 2024 data, depending on the material, the tooth and how many surfaces the cavity involves. These are national figures, and fees in your area may be higher or lower.

Tooth-colored composite fillings sit at the higher end: $145 to $480 for a front tooth and $165 to $530 for a back tooth. Silver amalgam fillings run $110 to $455, with the price rising as the filling covers more surfaces of the tooth.

The CareCredit cost study gives lower averages: $226 for a composite resin filling and $139 for a silver amalgam filling. The two sources measure different things. CareCredit reports survey averages across many patients, while Delta Dental's ranges stretch from small single-surface fillings up to large multi-surface ones.

Cost tip: ask how many surfaces your filling will cover. A one-surface filling and a three-surface filling on the same tooth are billed under different codes at very different prices.

How much do composite and silver fillings cost?

Composite fillings cost more than silver amalgam fillings, both in Delta Dental's ranges and in CareCredit's averages of $226 versus $139. Composite is the tooth-colored resin most offices now use by default; amalgam is the older silver-colored metal alloy.

  • Composite resin: matches tooth color, bonds to the tooth and usually needs less drilling of healthy tooth. Placement is technique-sensitive and takes longer, which helps explain the higher fee.
  • Amalgam: durable and lower cost, well suited to large fillings on back teeth. Its silver color makes it a poor choice for visible teeth, and fewer offices place it today.
  • Glass ionomer: releases fluoride and is sometimes used near the gum line or in baby teeth. It wears faster than composite.
  • Inlays and onlays: lab-made gold, porcelain or resin restorations for larger cavities. They cost considerably more than direct fillings and are quoted separately.

Insurance can affect which material makes financial sense. Some plans pay for composite on back teeth only up to the amalgam fee, leaving you to pay the difference, so ask before treatment.

Existing amalgam fillings that are intact do not need to be replaced just because of their material. Replacing sound fillings removes more tooth and adds cost; your dentist will recommend replacement when a filling is cracked, leaking or has decay around it.

What changes the cost to fill a cavity?

The cost to fill a cavity depends mainly on the filling material, the number of tooth surfaces involved, the location of the tooth and the fee level in your area.

  • Surfaces: dentists bill fillings by surface count, from one to four or more. A cavity between two teeth often involves two or three surfaces.
  • Front vs back tooth: back teeth are harder to reach and keep dry, so composite fillings on molars are priced higher.
  • Depth: a deep cavity may need a protective liner or base under the filling.
  • Region: fees in large cities usually run higher than in rural areas.
  • Exam and X-rays: a new-patient exam and bitewing X-rays may be billed on top of the filling.
  • Comfort options: local anesthetic is normally included, but nitrous oxide or other sedation is often an extra charge.

The practice itself matters too. A general dentist, a pediatric dentist and a high-end cosmetic practice can charge noticeably different fees for the same filling code, and out-of-network offices bill their full list price. Two quotes for the same tooth are worth comparing when the work is not urgent, as long as both list the same surfaces and material.

Several small cavities found at one visit can usually be filled in a single appointment. That does not lower the per-filling fee, but it saves time off work and repeat travel.

How much is a filling with insurance?

A filling with insurance usually costs a fraction of the full fee, because dental plans typically cover 50%–80% of basic procedures including fillings, according to Delta Dental. You pay the remaining share, plus any deductible you have not yet met.

Fillings usually count as basic services, which sit between preventive care and major work such as crowns, as Delta Dental explains in its overview of what dental plans cover. Several plan rules shape your final bill:

  • Deductible: you pay this first, then the plan's percentage applies.
  • Annual maximum: caps the total the plan pays in a year.
  • Waiting periods: new plans may delay coverage for basic work.
  • Material clauses: some plans pay the amalgam rate for back-tooth composites.
  • Replacement limits: plans may not pay to redo the same filling within a set period.

How much does it cost to get a cavity filled without insurance?

Getting a cavity filled without insurance costs about $145 to $480 for a composite on a front tooth, $165 to $530 for a composite on a back tooth, and $110 to $455 for an amalgam filling, based on Delta Dental data. Those figures cover the filling itself; an exam and X-rays may be added for new patients.

Self-pay patients often have more room to ask about price than they expect. Ask for the office's cash price, whether it offers an in-house membership plan, and whether a payment plan is available if you need several fillings at once.

Before you agree to treatment, a few questions make quotes easier to compare:

  • Which tooth, how many surfaces and which material does each filling involve?
  • Is the exam and X-ray fee included or separate?
  • Is there a discount for paying in full at the visit?
  • Which cavities are urgent, and which can safely be scheduled over the next few months?

Spacing out treatment can help a tight budget, but only for cavities your dentist considers small and slow-moving. Deep cavities close to the nerve should go first, because they are the ones most likely to turn into a root canal if left.

How can you pay less for a cavity filling?

The best ways to pay less for a cavity filling are choosing an in-network dentist, using a dental school or community clinic, paying with HSA or FSA funds, and treating cavities while they are small.

  1. In-network dentists charge negotiated fees, so both the plan's share and yours are calculated on a lower base.
  2. Dental schools offer fillings by supervised students at reduced prices, with longer appointments.
  3. Community health centers often use sliding-scale fees based on income.
  4. HSA and FSA accounts let you pay with pre-tax money.
  5. Payment plans from the office spread larger bills over several months. Check the terms on any medical credit card for deferred interest.
  6. Early treatment keeps a filling small. Fewer surfaces means a lower fee.

Prevention costs least of all. Sealants on the chewing surfaces of back teeth help block decay in deep grooves; see our page on dental sealants cost for prices and who benefits most. Fluoride toothpaste, cleaning between teeth and cutting back on frequent sugary drinks do the rest.

What happens when you get a cavity filled?

Getting a cavity filled usually takes one visit: the dentist numbs the area, removes the decayed part of the tooth, cleans the space and fills it with the chosen material. A small filling is often finished within an hour.

  1. An exam and X-rays confirm the size and location of the cavity.
  2. Local anesthetic numbs the tooth and gum.
  3. The decay is removed with a drill or other instruments.
  4. For composite, the tooth is etched, a bonding agent is applied, and resin is placed in layers hardened with a curing light. For amalgam, the material is packed into the space and shaped.
  5. The dentist checks your bite and polishes the filling.

Numbness wears off over a few hours. Mild sensitivity to cold or biting for a few days is common; follow your dentist's instructions, and call if the bite feels high or the sensitivity keeps getting worse. Our guide to cavity filling covers the procedure, aftercare and what to expect in more detail.

Cleveland Clinic explains how cavities form and why early treatment matters: decay that reaches the inner layers of the tooth can no longer be fixed with a simple filling.

When is a filling not enough?

A filling is not enough when decay or damage has reached the pulp or left too little tooth to hold a filling, in which case a crown, root canal or extraction may be needed. Those treatments cost considerably more than a filling, which is the strongest financial reason to treat cavities early.

Large cavities that undermine the walls of a tooth often need an onlay or crown for strength. Decay that reaches the nerve can require a root canal first. A chipped or cracked tooth is a different problem from a cavity, and repair options range from bonding to a crown; see our guide to chipped tooth repair cost.

Call a dentist promptly if a tooth throbs, wakes you at night, or aches for a long time after hot or cold. Seek same-day or emergency care for facial swelling, fever, swelling spreading toward the eye or neck, or trouble swallowing or breathing.

Do children's baby teeth need fillings, and what do they cost?

Children's baby teeth often do need fillings when cavities form, and they are generally billed with the same filling codes as adult teeth, so the same material and surface rules drive the price. Baby teeth hold space for adult teeth and help with chewing and speech, and some back baby teeth stay in place until the pre-teen years.

Cavities spread faster in baby teeth because the enamel is thinner. An untreated cavity can reach the nerve and cause pain or an infection, which may then need a pulp treatment, a stainless steel crown or an extraction with a space maintainer. Each of those costs more than an early filling.

Pediatric dentists may suggest nitrous oxide or other sedation for young or anxious children, and that is usually billed separately. Most dental plans that cover children treat fillings as a basic service, and Medicaid and CHIP include dental care for eligible children. Sealants and fluoride varnish at checkups are inexpensive ways to prevent many cavities in the first place.

Take a child to a dentist the same day for facial swelling, fever with a toothache, or a tooth injury that leaves a tooth loose, pushed in or knocked out.

How long do fillings last before they need replacing?

Fillings typically last many years, though none are permanent, and amalgam fillings tend to outlast composite fillings on heavily used back teeth. How long a filling lasts depends on its size, your bite, grinding habits and how well decay is kept away from its edges.

Replacing a filling usually costs about the same as placing the original, and sometimes more if decay has spread underneath. Regular checkups let your dentist spot a leaking or cracked filling before it becomes a larger, more expensive problem.

  • Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and clean between teeth.
  • Limit frequent sugary snacks and drinks.
  • Wear a night guard if you grind or clench your teeth.
  • Keep up with checkups so worn fillings are caught early.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a cavity filling cost without insurance?

A cavity filling without insurance costs about $145 to $480 for a tooth-colored composite on a front tooth, $165 to $530 on a back tooth, and $110 to $455 for a silver amalgam, according to Delta Dental's 2024 data. Back teeth and fillings covering more surfaces sit at the top of those ranges. These are national figures, so ask your dentist for a written quote.

How much is a filling with insurance?

With insurance, you usually pay only part of a filling's cost, because plans typically cover basic procedures such as fillings at 50%–80%, according to Delta Dental. Your deductible, annual maximum, waiting periods and whether the dentist is in network all affect your final share. A pre-treatment estimate from your insurer gives the clearest number.

Why are tooth-colored fillings more expensive?

Tooth-colored composite fillings cost more because placement takes longer and needs a dry tooth, careful layering and light curing. CareCredit's cost study puts the average composite filling at $226, compared with $139 for silver amalgam. Some insurance plans pay only the amalgam rate for back teeth, leaving you to cover the difference.

How many fillings can you get at once?

Several fillings can usually be done in one visit, often all the teeth on one side of the mouth so only that side is numbed. Your dentist decides based on how deep the cavities are and how long you can comfortably sit. Each filling is still billed separately according to its material and surface count.

Does getting a filling hurt?

Getting a filling usually does not hurt because the tooth and gum are numbed with local anesthetic first. You may feel pressure and vibration. Afterward, mild sensitivity to cold or biting for a few days is common. Pain that worsens, a throbbing tooth or swelling are reasons to call your dentist promptly.

Can a cavity go away without a filling?

A cavity that has broken through the enamel cannot heal on its own and needs a filling or other treatment. Very early enamel weakening, before a hole forms, can sometimes be reversed with fluoride and better hygiene. Your dentist can tell which stage you are at, and early treatment keeps the repair smaller and cheaper.

Is it cheaper to get a filling at a dental school?

Dental schools usually charge less for fillings than private offices because students do the work under faculty supervision. Appointments take longer, and you may need a screening visit before treatment starts. Community health centers with sliding-scale fees are another lower-cost option for people without dental insurance.
