Tooth Extraction Cost in 2026: Simple and Surgical Prices, Insurance and Aftercare

How much is a tooth extraction?

A tooth extraction costs $150 to $310 per tooth for a simple, non-surgical extraction and $260 to $460 per tooth for a surgical extraction when billed out-of-network, based on Delta Dental 2024 internal data. Those fees are for the extraction itself, per tooth.

A second national source gives slightly different numbers. CareCredit reports an average of $177 for a simple tooth extraction and $363 for a surgical tooth extraction in its 2023–2024 cost study. Both sets of figures land in the same general zone, and the next section explains why they are not identical.

The total bill for getting a tooth pulled is often higher than the extraction fee alone. An exam and X-ray are usually needed first, and some patients add sedation, a bone graft or a follow-up visit. Ask the office for an itemized estimate so you can see every line before the appointment.

All prices on this page are national averages or ranges from the named publishers. Local fees depend on your region, the type of provider and how difficult the tooth is to remove.

What is the difference between a simple and a surgical extraction?

A simple extraction removes a tooth that is fully visible above the gum, while a surgical extraction is needed when the tooth is broken, impacted or has roots that cannot be lifted out in one piece. The difference drives most of the gap in price.

A simple extraction is done under local anesthetic. The dentist loosens the tooth with an instrument called an elevator and removes it with forceps. Many general dentists do simple extractions in their own offices.

A surgical extraction usually involves a small cut in the gum, removing some bone around the tooth, or sectioning the tooth into pieces. Stitches are often placed. Oral surgeons handle many surgical extractions, especially impacted wisdom teeth and teeth with curved or fused roots. Delta Dental lists surgical extractions at $260 to $460 per tooth out-of-network, compared with $150 to $310 for simple ones.

A badly decayed or broken tooth can turn a planned simple extraction into a surgical one partway through, which is why some offices quote a range. Back teeth often fall into the higher bracket; our page on rotten tooth extraction cost covers molars in detail.

Why do tooth extraction cost figures differ between sources?

Tooth extraction cost figures differ between sources because they measure different things. Delta Dental's ranges come from out-of-network fees in its claims data, which show what dentists billed for extractions. CareCredit's figures are averages from a procedural cost study across many practices.

A range and an average answer slightly different questions. The Delta Dental range of $150 to $310 tells you where most simple extraction fees fall, while the CareCredit average of $177 tells you the middle of its sample. Neither includes every extra charge a patient might see on the day.

Comparing quotes is easier when you ask each office the same questions: is the fee per tooth, does it include the exam and X-ray, is sedation extra, and is the follow-up visit included?

What changes the price of a tooth extraction?

The price of a tooth extraction changes with the type of extraction, the tooth's position, the provider, the type of anesthesia and any added procedures. Knowing these factors helps you read a quote.

  • Tooth location and roots. Molars have more roots than front teeth and are often harder to remove.
  • Impaction or breakage. Teeth below the gum line or broken at the gum need surgical removal.
  • Provider. Oral surgeons typically charge more than general dentists, reflecting their training and equipment.
  • Anesthesia. Local anesthetic is usually included. Nitrous oxide, IV sedation or general anesthesia is billed separately.
  • Imaging. A single X-ray or a 3D scan may be needed before surgery.
  • Bone grafting. If you plan an implant later, the dentist may place a graft in the socket. CareCredit reports an average of $811 for an allograft bone graft after extraction. Cleveland Clinic explains how dental bone grafts work.
  • Region. Fees in large metro areas are often higher than in smaller towns.

A tooth extraction quote should list each of these items separately so you can see where the money goes.

How much does it cost to get a tooth pulled with insurance?

The cost to get a tooth pulled with insurance is usually your plan's share of the in-network fee after the deductible. On a common 100-80-50 plan, basic procedures such as extractions are covered at 80%, according to Delta Dental, which leaves the patient responsible for the remainder.

Insurance coverage for extractions varies by plan, so check these details first:

  • Plan category. Some plans list surgical extractions as basic and others as major, which can change the coverage share.
  • Deductible. You may pay the full in-network fee until the yearly deductible is met.
  • Annual maximum. Extractions, grafts and later implants can add up quickly against the yearly cap.
  • Waiting periods. New plans sometimes delay coverage for basic or major services.
  • Medical insurance. Medical plans occasionally pay for surgical extractions related to trauma or certain medical conditions; ask the oral surgeon's office to check.

How can I pay less for a tooth extraction without insurance?

The best ways to pay less for a tooth extraction without insurance are dental schools, community health centers, dental savings plans and payment plans. Asking for the self-pay price up front also helps, because some offices offer a discount for paying at the visit.

  1. Dental schools. Supervised students and residents often charge reduced fees for extractions.
  2. Community clinics. Federally funded health centers offer dental care on a sliding fee scale based on income.
  3. Dental savings plans. These membership plans give discounted fees at participating dentists; they are not insurance, so compare the yearly fee against the savings.
  4. HSA or FSA funds. Tooth extractions are generally an eligible expense, so you can pay with pre-tax dollars.
  5. Payment plans. In-house plans or third-party financing spread the cost; read the interest terms carefully.
  6. General dentist first. A simple extraction done by a general dentist usually costs less than a referral to a specialist.

Our page on tooth extraction cost without insurance covers self-pay pricing in more detail.

Do not delay care for a painful, infected tooth to save money. Facial swelling, fever or trouble swallowing or breathing needs same-day dental or emergency care.

How long does a tooth extraction take?

A simple tooth extraction usually takes only a few minutes once the area is numb, and the whole appointment typically takes under an hour. A surgical extraction takes longer, especially when bone removal, sectioning or stitches are needed, or when several teeth come out at once.

A tooth extraction appointment generally follows the same steps. The dentist reviews your health history and X-rays, numbs the area, removes the tooth, and places gauze over the socket so a blood clot can form. You then receive written aftercare instructions. Cleveland Clinic describes the procedure in more detail.

Tooth extraction pain during the procedure should be limited to pressure, because the local anesthetic blocks sharp pain. Tell the dentist if you feel sharp pain so more anesthetic can be given. Soreness after the numbness wears off is normal and typically eases over the next few days.

Tooth extraction with sedation means you need someone to drive you home and stay with you for a while afterward. Follow the fasting and medication instructions your dentist gives you before sedation.

What should I avoid after a tooth extraction?

After a tooth extraction, avoid anything that can dislodge the blood clot in the socket: drinking through a straw, spitting forcefully, smoking, vigorous rinsing and hard or crunchy foods near the site. The clot protects the bone and nerve while the socket heals. Your dentist's instructions take priority over the general guidance below.

  • How to stop bleeding after tooth extraction. Bite firmly on a folded gauze pad for 30 to 45 minutes without checking it. If oozing continues, replace the gauze or bite on a moistened black tea bag, and keep your head raised. Light pink saliva for a day is normal.
  • When you can drink soda. Most dentists advise skipping carbonated and very hot drinks for at least the first day or two, and never using a straw during early healing. Sip from a glass.
  • When you can sleep on your side. Keep your head elevated on extra pillows for the first night or two. Sleeping on the opposite side is usually fine; avoid pressing on the extraction side until tenderness eases.
  • When you can brush your teeth. Brush the rest of your mouth gently from the first night or next morning, avoiding the socket. Gentle warm saltwater rinses usually start after the first day, if your dentist agrees.
  • Eating. Choose soft, cool foods at first and chew on the other side. See our guide on what to eat after tooth extraction.
  • Smoking. Smoking raises the risk of dry socket and slows healing. Read our page on smoking after tooth extraction before you light up.

Should I plan to replace a tooth after it is pulled?

Planning to replace a pulled tooth is worth discussing before the extraction, because the choice can change what happens on the day and what you pay. Front teeth and most back teeth that help you chew are usually replaced, while wisdom teeth are not.

A missing tooth can let neighboring teeth drift or tip into the gap and the opposing tooth grow further out of its socket over time. The jawbone where the tooth stood also slowly shrinks once the root is gone. These changes do not happen overnight, but they can make a later implant or bridge harder to place.

Tooth replacement after an extraction usually means an implant, a bridge or a removable partial denture. If an implant is likely, the dentist may recommend a bone graft in the socket at the time of extraction to preserve bone; CareCredit reports an average of $811 for an allograft bone graft after extraction. Ask whether the graft is truly needed for your plan, and whether the extraction, graft and later implant can be timed to make the most of your insurance benefits.

A tooth extraction treatment plan that includes the replacement step gives you a clearer picture of the full cost than the extraction fee alone.

When is pain after a tooth extraction a problem?

Pain after a tooth extraction is a problem when it gets worse instead of better after the first few days, or when it comes with swelling, fever, pus or a bad taste. Normal soreness usually peaks early and fades steadily.

Dry socket is the most common painful complication. The blood clot is lost or breaks down, leaving bone exposed, and throbbing pain often spreads toward the ear a few days after the extraction. Cleveland Clinic explains the symptoms and treatment. Dentists treat dry socket by cleaning the socket and placing a soothing dressing, which means an extra visit.

Infection is less common but needs prompt care. Signs include swelling that increases after the third day, warmth, pus, fever or a persistent foul taste. Our page on the infected tooth extraction site covers what to look for.

Call your dentist or seek urgent care for bleeding that will not stop with firm pressure, swelling that spreads toward the eye or neck, fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or severe pain that pain relievers do not control.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to have a tooth pulled?

Having a tooth pulled costs $150 to $310 per tooth for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363. Exams, X-rays and sedation are often billed separately, and local prices vary.

How much is a tooth extraction with insurance?

A tooth extraction with insurance usually costs your share of the in-network fee after the deductible. On a common 100-80-50 plan, basic procedures such as extractions are covered at 80%, per Delta Dental. Your plan's category for surgical extractions, waiting periods and annual maximum also affect what you pay.

How long does a tooth extraction take?

A simple tooth extraction usually takes a few minutes once the area is numb, and the full appointment typically takes under an hour. Surgical extractions take longer, especially for impacted or broken teeth, multiple teeth or when stitches are needed. Your dentist can give you a time estimate in advance.

How do you stop bleeding after a tooth extraction?

To stop bleeding after a tooth extraction, bite firmly on folded gauze for 30 to 45 minutes without checking it. If oozing continues, use fresh gauze or a moistened black tea bag and keep your head raised. Call your dentist or seek urgent care if heavy bleeding does not slow with steady pressure.

When can I drink soda after a tooth extraction?

Most dentists suggest avoiding soda and other carbonated drinks for at least the first day or two after a tooth extraction, and never drinking through a straw during early healing. Sip from a glass once your dentist says it is fine. Your own dentist's instructions take priority.

When can I brush my teeth after a tooth extraction?

You can usually brush the rest of your teeth gently the night of the extraction or the next morning, keeping the brush away from the socket. Avoid vigorous rinsing and spitting for the first day. Gentle saltwater rinses typically begin after the first day if your dentist approves.

Is a bone graft needed after a tooth extraction?

A bone graft is not needed after every tooth extraction. Dentists usually suggest one when an implant is planned or the socket has lost bone. CareCredit reports an average of $811 for an allograft bone graft after extraction. Ask whether the graft is necessary for your treatment plan.
