Types of Dentures and Cost: Full, Partial, Immediate, Flexible and Implant-Supported

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Types of dentures and cost range widely: an upper and lower set averages $452 for basic, $1,968 for traditional and $6,514 for premium dentures, according to CareCredit. Partials, immediate dentures and implant-supported dentures are priced differently. These are national averages, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • CareCredit's survey averages $452 for basic, $1,968 for traditional and $6,514 for premium upper and lower dentures.
  • Delta Dental lists single full dentures at $1,115 to $2,425 (lower) and $1,220 to $2,540 (upper) out-of-network.
  • Implant-supported full dentures cost the most, at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance per GoodRx.
  • Most dentures are made of acrylic resin teeth and bases, sometimes with a metal framework for partials.
  • Daily cleaning, overnight removal and periodic relines help dentures fit well and last longer.

How much are false teeth?

False teeth cost an average of $452 for a basic upper and lower set, $1,968 for traditional dentures and $6,514 for premium dentures, according to the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study published by CareCredit. Implant-supported full dentures cost far more, at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance according to GoodRx.

Delta Dental 2024 internal data lists a single full lower denture at $1,115 to $2,425 and a full upper denture at $1,220 to $2,540, out-of-network. Those ranges are for one arch, while CareCredit's averages cover an upper and lower set, so the numbers are not directly comparable. Delta Dental reports out-of-network fees; CareCredit reports survey averages by quality tier.

The gap between basic and premium dentures comes mainly from materials and lab work. Basic dentures use stock teeth and simpler processing, while premium dentures use more lifelike teeth, custom shading and more fitting appointments. Our main guide to dentures cost explains each tier in more detail.

What are dentures and what are they made of?

Dentures are removable replacements for missing teeth and the surrounding gum tissue, custom made from impressions of your mouth. Full dentures replace all teeth on an arch, while partial dentures fill gaps and clip onto remaining natural teeth.

Most dentures are made of these materials:

  • Acrylic resin. The pink, gum-colored base of most full dentures and many partials. Acrylic is easy to adjust, reline and repair.
  • Acrylic or porcelain teeth. Acrylic teeth are the most common today because they bond well to the base and are less likely to chip opposing teeth. Porcelain is harder but more brittle.
  • Metal frameworks. Cast cobalt-chromium frames make partials thinner and stronger, with metal clasps that grip natural teeth.
  • Flexible nylon-type resin. Used for metal-free flexible partials.

Upper dentures cover the roof of the mouth, which helps suction and stability. Lower dentures are horseshoe-shaped to leave room for the tongue, which is one reason lower dentures often feel less secure. The Cleveland Clinic describes how prosthodontists design each type.

What are the main types of dentures?

The main types of dentures are conventional full dentures, partial dentures, immediate (temporary) dentures, flexible partials and implant-supported dentures. Each solves a different problem and carries a different cost.

  • Conventional full dentures. Made after extractions have healed, usually for a better long-term fit. Delta Dental lists $1,115 to $2,425 for a lower and $1,220 to $2,540 for an upper, out-of-network.
  • Partial dentures. Replace some teeth on an arch. Delta Dental lists $1,425 to $2,490 for a lower partial and $1,435 to $2,785 for an upper partial. Our page on partial dentures cost, with before and after examples compares designs.
  • Immediate or temporary dentures. Placed on the day teeth are removed, so you are never without teeth. GoodRx lists an immediate full denture at $1,900. Gums shrink as they heal, so immediate dentures usually need relines or replacement.
  • Flexible partials. Metal-free and comfortable, at about $1,700 per GoodRx. See our guide to flexible dentures.
  • Implant-supported dentures. Attach to implants for much better stability, at $10,500 to $21,500 for full dentures per GoodRx. Our page on snap in dentures covers removable implant options.

Healthline compares how dentures and implants differ for comfort, chewing and upkeep.

How can you get cheap dentures or use insurance to pay less?

You can get cheaper dentures by using dental insurance, choosing an in-network dentist, visiting a dental school clinic, picking a basic or traditional tier, and paying with HSA or FSA funds. Very cheap dentures may cost more over time if they fit poorly and need frequent adjustments.

  • Dental insurance. Dentures are usually a major service with partial coverage, subject to the annual maximum and often a waiting period. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate.
  • Medicare and Medicaid. Original Medicare generally does not cover dentures; some Medicare Advantage plans do. Medicaid adult dental benefits vary by state.
  • Dental schools. Supervised students make dentures at reduced fees, with more appointments.
  • HSA and FSA. Dentures are an eligible expense, so pre-tax funds lower the real cost.
  • Itemized quotes. Confirm whether extractions, adjustments and relines are included.

The price tier matters. CareCredit's $452 basic average is the lowest-cost set, but traditional and premium dentures usually offer better fit and appearance. Discuss what each tier includes before deciding.

When do you need to reline dentures?

You need to reline dentures when they become loose, rub sore spots or trap food, which happens as the gums and jawbone slowly shrink after tooth loss. GoodRx lists a chairside reline at about $350 or more, and lab relines, which take longer, may cost differently.

Immediate dentures usually need a reline within the first months as extraction sites heal. Conventional dentures are often relined every few years. A dentist checks fit at regular visits and can tell you when a reline, rebase or new denture is the better choice. Avoid home reline kits unless your dentist approves, because a poor fit can damage gums.

A reline is different from a rebase and from a new denture. A reline adds new material to the fitting surface so the denture matches your current gums. A rebase replaces the whole pink base while keeping the existing teeth. A new denture is usually the better choice when the teeth are badly worn, the bite has collapsed, or the base is cracked. Ask your dentist to explain which option fits your situation and what each costs locally before you decide.

How do you care for and clean dentures?

You care for dentures by removing and rinsing them after meals, brushing them daily with a soft brush and non-abrasive denture cleaner, soaking them overnight, and cleaning your gums and any natural teeth. Good care keeps dentures fresh and reduces gum irritation.

  1. Rinse dentures after eating to remove food particles.
  2. Brush daily with a denture brush and denture cleanser; regular toothpaste can scratch acrylic.
  3. Soak overnight in water or a denture solution so the material does not dry out and warp.
  4. Never use hot water, which can distort the base.
  5. Brush your gums, tongue and palate, and floss remaining teeth.
  6. Handle dentures over a towel or basin of water to prevent breakage.

See your dentist if dentures cause sores that do not heal within a few days, feel loose or cause clicking. Get prompt care for a sore that lasts more than two weeks, swelling, fever, or a white or red patch that does not go away. Follow your dentist's instructions on wearing time and cleaning.

Frequently asked questions

What are the different types of dentures?

The different types of dentures are full (complete) dentures, partial dentures, immediate dentures placed on the day of extraction, flexible partials, and implant-supported dentures. Each type suits a different number of missing teeth and budget. Your dentist recommends a type based on your remaining teeth, gums and jawbone.

How much does a full set of dentures cost?

A full set of upper and lower dentures averages $452 for basic, $1,968 for traditional and $6,514 for premium dentures, according to CareCredit. Delta Dental lists single full dentures at $1,115 to $2,425 for a lower and $1,220 to $2,540 for an upper, out-of-network. Local prices vary.

Are temporary dentures cheaper?

Temporary dentures are not always cheaper. An immediate full denture, often used as a temporary, costs about $1,900 without insurance according to GoodRx. Immediate dentures also usually need relines as gums heal, adding cost, and some people later replace them with conventional dentures.

How long do dentures last?

Dentures often last several years before needing replacement, though relines may be needed sooner as gums change. Wear, staining and changes in fit all affect lifespan. Regular dental checkups help catch fit problems early, and your dentist can tell you when a reline or new denture is needed.

Can you sleep in your dentures?

Most dentists advise removing dentures at night to let gums rest and to lower the risk of irritation and fungal infections. Soak dentures overnight in water or a cleaning solution so they do not dry out. Follow your own dentist's advice, especially in the first days after receiving new dentures.
