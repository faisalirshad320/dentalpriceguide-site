Tooth Abscess Stages: From Cavity to Swelling, and When to Get Help

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 26, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

A tooth abscess usually develops in stages: enamel decay, dentin decay, pulp inflammation, pulp infection, then a pocket of pus at the root tip that can spread into the jaw and face. Pain often peaks when the pulp is inflamed and may fade as the nerve dies, even though infection continues. Any facial swelling needs prompt care (Cleveland Clinic).

Key takeaways
  • A tooth abscess is a pocket of pus caused by a bacterial infection.
  • Most abscesses start as an untreated cavity or crack that reaches the pulp.
  • Pain can fade at a later stage, which does not mean the infection has cleared.
  • Facial swelling, fever or trouble swallowing or breathing are emergency signs.
  • Treatment is a root canal or extraction, plus drainage when needed.

What is an abscessed tooth?

An abscessed tooth is a tooth with a pocket of pus caused by a bacterial infection. The pus forms because the body is fighting bacteria that have reached places they should not be, such as the inside of the tooth or the gum around it.

There are two main types. A periapical abscess forms at the tip of the root after bacteria invade the pulp, usually through a deep cavity or crack. A periodontal abscess forms in the gum alongside a root, usually linked to gum disease; the Cleveland Clinic describes this type in detail. The stages on this page follow the more common periapical path.

An abscess does not go away on its own. It may drain and feel better for a while, but the source of the infection remains until a dentist treats it. Our main tooth abscess guide covers the condition overall.

What are the early stages of a tooth abscess?

The early stages of a tooth abscess are really the stages of tooth decay. Stage one is enamel decay, where acids from plaque bacteria weaken the hard outer layer. You may see a chalky white spot or a brown mark, and you usually feel nothing.

Stage two is dentin decay. Once decay breaks through the enamel into the softer dentin underneath, it spreads faster. Teeth may become sensitive to sweets or cold, and a visible hole or dark spot may appear. The Cleveland Clinic's guide to cavities explains how decay progresses.

At these stages a simple filling, and sometimes fluoride alone for very early enamel lesions, stops the process. This is the cheapest and easiest point to intervene, which is why regular check-ups that catch decay early are so valuable.

What happens when decay reaches the pulp?

When decay reaches the pulp, the nerve and blood vessels inside the tooth become inflamed. This stage is often the most painful. Hot or cold may cause pain that lingers long after the food or drink is gone, and the tooth may throb on its own, especially at night.

Dentists distinguish between reversible inflammation, where removing the decay lets the pulp recover, and irreversible inflammation, where the pulp will not heal. Lingering pain is a typical sign of the irreversible kind. At that point the tooth usually needs a root canal or an extraction.

Why does abscess pain sometimes stop?

Tooth abscess pain sometimes stops because the nerve inside the tooth has died. A dead nerve can no longer send pain signals, so a tooth that throbbed for days may suddenly feel quiet.

That quiet is misleading. Bacteria continue to multiply inside the dead pulp and spread out through the root tip into the bone. The tooth may darken in colour over time. Many people delay care at this stage because the pain has gone, only to develop an abscess weeks or months later.

What are the symptoms of a tooth abscess?

The symptoms of a tooth abscess usually include pain when biting, a tooth that feels raised or loose, and swelling of the gum near the root. A small pimple-like bump (a fistula) may appear on the gum and leak salty or foul-tasting fluid.

  • Throbbing pain that can spread to the ear, jaw or neck.
  • Tenderness when chewing or touching the tooth.
  • Gum swelling or a bump near the root.
  • Bad taste or bad breath from draining pus.
  • Swollen lymph nodes under the jaw.
  • Fever or feeling unwell, which suggests the infection is spreading.

When an abscess drains through the gum, pain may ease, but drainage is not a cure.

When does tooth abscess swelling become an emergency?

Tooth abscess swelling becomes an emergency when it spreads beyond the gum into the face, under the jaw or toward the eye, or when it comes with fever, trouble swallowing, trouble opening the mouth or trouble breathing. These signs mean the infection may be moving into deeper tissues.

Go to an emergency department or call emergency services for swelling of the face or neck with fever, difficulty swallowing or breathing, swelling under the tongue, or a fast-spreading swelling. Do not wait for a routine dental appointment.

For swelling limited to the gum without fever, contact a dentist the same day if possible. Do not try to pop or cut an abscess yourself, and do not apply heat to facial swelling.

How can you stop a tooth abscess from developing?

You can stop most tooth abscesses from developing by dealing with decay and cracks while they are small. Because an abscess is usually the last stage of a long process, every earlier stage is a chance to intervene cheaply.

  • Keep regular check-ups with X-rays, which find decay between teeth and under old fillings before it hurts.
  • Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and clean between your teeth every day.
  • Limit frequent sugary snacks and drinks, which feed the bacteria that cause decay.
  • Have sensitivity checked rather than waiting for it to become pain.
  • Repair chipped teeth and broken fillings quickly so bacteria cannot leak inside.
  • Treat gum disease to lower the risk of a periodontal abscess.
  • Get a tooth checked after any knock or injury, even if it looks fine.

People with diabetes, a weakened immune system or a dry mouth from medications may be at higher risk and benefit from more frequent check-ups.

How is a tooth abscess treated, and what does it cost?

A tooth abscess is treated by removing the source of infection and letting the pus drain. The two main options are a root canal to save the tooth or an extraction. Antibiotics may be added when infection has spread, but they do not replace treating the tooth.

Delta Dental's out-of-network data puts a root canal to save an abscessed tooth at about $1,200 for a front tooth and about $1,500 for a molar, according to its root canal cost guide. Extracting an abscessed tooth runs $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical one, per Delta Dental's extraction guide. Extraction is cheaper on the day, but replacing the tooth later adds cost. See tooth abscess treatment for the full options.

Frequently asked questions

What are the stages of a tooth abscess?

A tooth abscess usually progresses from enamel decay to dentin decay, then inflammation of the pulp, infection or death of the pulp, a pus-filled abscess at the root tip, and finally possible spread into the jaw and face. Treating decay early stops the process before an abscess forms.

How long does it take for a tooth abscess to form?

The time it takes for a tooth abscess to form varies widely, from weeks to many months or even years. Decay may progress slowly for a long time, while an injured or cracked tooth can become infected faster. Regular check-ups help catch problems before an abscess develops.

Can a tooth abscess go away on its own?

A tooth abscess does not go away on its own. It may drain and the pain may ease, but bacteria remain inside the tooth or gum. Without a root canal, extraction or gum treatment, the infection usually returns and can spread.

Is tooth abscess swelling dangerous?

Tooth abscess swelling can be dangerous when it spreads into the face, neck or under the tongue, or comes with fever, trouble swallowing or trouble breathing. These are emergency signs that need immediate medical care. Swelling limited to the gum still needs a dentist promptly.

Why did my abscessed tooth stop hurting?

An abscessed tooth may stop hurting because the nerve inside it has died or because the pus has found a way to drain through the gum. The infection is still present in both cases and needs dental treatment to prevent it spreading.
