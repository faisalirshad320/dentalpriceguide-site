Failed Gum Graft: How to Tell, Why It Happens and Your Options

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS, M.Phil Chemical PathologyUpdated November 4, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A gum graft may have failed if the graft tissue turns dark grey or black, comes loose, falls away, or leaves the root exposed again once healing should be complete. Some whiteness and swelling in the first week is normal. Contact your periodontist promptly; partial failures can often be improved with a second procedure. A graft averages $2,742 according to CareCredit.

Key takeaways
  • True gum graft failure is uncommon but more likely in smokers.
  • White or puffy tissue in the first week is usually normal healing.
  • Warning signs include dark tissue, detachment, pus and exposed roots after healing.
  • Partial coverage is not always failure; ask your periodontist to assess.
  • A repeat graft costs about the same as the first, averaging $2,742 (CareCredit).

What does normal gum graft healing look like?

Normal gum graft healing often looks alarming to patients. In the first week, the graft may appear white, grey-white or slightly puffy because the surface layer is sloughing while the graft develops a new blood supply. Mild swelling, tenderness and minor bleeding are common, and the palate donor site may be more sore than the graft itself.

Over the next few weeks, the tissue turns pinker and firmer and gradually blends with the surrounding gum. Full maturation takes a few months. Our guide to gum graft before and after shows the expected stages.

What are the signs of a failed gum graft?

The signs of a failed gum graft usually become clear after the first week or two, when healthy grafts should be improving.

  • Dark grey, black or brown tissue, suggesting the graft has lost its blood supply.
  • Tissue detaching or falling away from the site.
  • Root re-exposed after the expected healing period.
  • Pus, increasing swelling or a bad smell, which can signal infection.
  • Pain that worsens after the first few days instead of improving.
  • Stitches loosening early with the graft shifting position.

Contact your periodontist promptly for any of these signs, or for fever, bleeding that will not stop with gentle pressure, or rapidly increasing swelling.

Is partial root coverage a failure?

Partial root coverage is not necessarily a failure. The amount of coverage a graft can achieve depends on the bone and gum levels between the teeth, the thickness of the tissue and the shape of the recession. Your periodontist may have predicted partial coverage from the start.

Even when not fully covering the root, a graft can thicken the gum, reduce sensitivity and stop recession from progressing, which are important health goals. Ask your periodontist to compare the result with what was expected for your case. The Cleveland Clinic's gum recession guide explains why outcomes vary.

Why do gum grafts fail?

Gum grafts fail mainly when the graft cannot establish a blood supply or is disturbed while healing. Common causes include:

  • Smoking or vaping, which reduces blood flow to the tissue.
  • Disturbing the graft by brushing, pulling the lip to look, or eating on that side too early.
  • Infection at the surgical site.
  • Movement of the graft if stitches loosen or the area is stretched.
  • Poor blood supply in the recipient area or very thin tissue.
  • Uncontrolled health conditions such as diabetes.
  • Ongoing causes of recession, such as aggressive brushing or grinding, which were not addressed.

What should you do if your gum graft fails?

If your gum graft fails, see your periodontist to assess what happened and plan next steps. They will examine the site, check for infection and decide whether to wait for further healing or plan another procedure.

A failed or partially successful graft can often be repeated after the area has healed, sometimes with a different technique or graft material. If smoking contributed, stopping before a second attempt greatly improves the chances. Treating gum disease and correcting brushing habits also matter.

Do some graft types fail more often?

Some graft types may be more sensitive to healing conditions than others. Connective tissue grafts, which take tissue from under the palate and place it under a flap of existing gum, tend to have a good blood supply from both sides and are widely used for root coverage. Free gingival grafts are excellent for thickening thin gum tissue but may give less colour match.

Donor tissue and collagen substitutes avoid a palate wound and can work well in suitable cases. Healthline's gum graft guide describes each type. Your periodontist chooses the technique based on your tissue and goals.

How much does a repeat gum graft cost?

A repeat gum graft usually costs about the same as the original procedure. CareCredit reports a national average of $2,742 for gum graft surgery, with a range of $2,120 to $4,982, and state averages for connective tissue grafts from $2,341 in Oklahoma to $4,013 in Hawaii.

Ask your periodontist whether a repeat procedure after a failed graft is offered at a reduced fee, and whether insurance will cover a second attempt. See our gum graft cost guide and CareCredit's procedure cost list.

How can you help a gum graft succeed?

You can help a gum graft succeed by following aftercare instructions carefully during the first two weeks, when the graft is most vulnerable.

  1. Do not brush or floss the graft until told to.
  2. Do not pull your lip or cheek to look at the graft.
  3. Eat soft, cool foods and chew on the other side.
  4. Avoid smoking and vaping entirely.
  5. Use prescribed rinses and medicines as directed.
  6. Wear a palatal stent if provided.
  7. Keep every follow-up appointment.

How do you protect your gums after a graft?

Protecting your gums after a graft means addressing whatever caused recession in the first place. Use a soft toothbrush with gentle pressure, wear a night guard if you grind, keep up regular cleanings to control gum disease, and avoid tobacco.

Regular check-ups with gum measurements let your dentist spot new recession early on the grafted tooth or others.

Will insurance pay for a second gum graft?

Insurance may pay for a second gum graft if the procedure is still needed for health reasons and your plan's frequency limits allow it. Many plans limit how often the same area can be grafted within a set number of years, so a repeat soon after the first may be denied.

Ask your periodontist to submit a pre-treatment estimate with photos, measurements and notes explaining why the first graft did not succeed and why another is needed. If coverage is denied, ask about payment plans or a reduced fee for the repeat. Some practices adjust their charge when they redo their own work within a set period.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my gum graft failed?

A gum graft may have failed if, after the first week or two, the tissue turns dark grey or black, comes loose, falls away or the root is exposed again. Pus, increasing swelling or worsening pain can signal infection. Contact your periodontist to assess it.

Is it normal for a gum graft to turn white?

Yes, it is normal for a gum graft to look white or grey-white in the first week as the surface layer sloughs while the graft develops a blood supply. It should gradually turn pink. Dark grey or black tissue is a warning sign.

What causes a gum graft to fail?

Gum grafts usually fail because the tissue cannot establish a blood supply or is disturbed during healing. Smoking, brushing or pulling at the site too early, infection, graft movement and uncontrolled diabetes are common causes.

Can a failed gum graft be redone?

A failed gum graft can often be redone once the area has healed, sometimes with a different technique or material. Stopping smoking and controlling gum disease improve the chances of success the second time.

How much does it cost to redo a gum graft?

Redoing a gum graft usually costs about the same as the first procedure. CareCredit reports a national average of $2,742, with a range of $2,120 to $4,982. Ask whether your periodontist offers a reduced fee for a repeat procedure.
