What are the main types of dental implants?

The main types of dental implants are endosteal, subperiosteal and zygomatic implants, sorted by where the implant anchors, plus mini implants and full-arch systems sorted by size and use. Your dentist or oral surgeon chooses the type based on how much healthy jawbone you have and how many teeth need replacing.

  • Endosteal implants: titanium or zirconia posts placed directly into the jawbone. Endosteal implants are the most common type for single teeth, bridges and implant-supported dentures.
  • Subperiosteal implants: a custom metal framework that sits on top of the jawbone, under the gum. Subperiosteal implants are now uncommon and reserved for people who cannot have enough bone for endosteal implants.
  • Zygomatic implants: extra-long implants anchored in the cheekbone (zygoma) instead of the upper jaw. Zygomatic implants are a specialist option for severe upper jaw bone loss.
  • Mini dental implants: narrow-diameter implants, often used to hold a lower denture in place.
  • All-on-4 and full-arch implants: a fixed set of teeth attached to four or more implants in one jaw.

Implants are a major step in prosthodontics, the branch of dentistry that replaces teeth. The Cleveland Clinic guide to prosthodontics explains how implants compare with dentures and bridges.

When are mini dental implants or zygomatic implants used?

Mini dental implants are used mainly to stabilize dentures or replace small teeth where bone is thin, while zygomatic implants are used when the upper jaw has too little bone for standard implants and grafting is not a good option.

Mini dental implants are thinner than standard implants and can often be placed with a less invasive technique. Mini implants usually cost less per implant than full-size ones, and several can lock a loose lower denture in place. Because they are narrow, mini implants are not usually the first choice for back teeth that take heavy chewing forces.

Zygomatic dental implants are long implants that pass through the upper jaw and anchor in the dense cheekbone. Zygomatic implants can let some people with major upper bone loss avoid extensive grafting and get fixed teeth sooner. The surgery is more complex and is done by experienced oral surgeons or specialist teams.

For people with moderate bone loss, bone grafting is often the alternative. CareCredit reports an average of $811 for an allograft (donor bone) and $2,779 for an autograft (your own bone). The Cleveland Clinic guide to dental bone grafts explains the procedure.

What are screwless dental implants?

Screwless dental implants are implants whose crown attaches to the implant without a retaining screw, usually by cement or a friction-fit (locking taper) connection. The implant post itself is still anchored in the jawbone; screwless describes how the visible tooth is held on, not how the implant is placed.

Supporters of screwless designs point to no screw-access hole in the crown and no screw that can loosen. The trade-off is that a cemented or locked crown can be harder to remove for repair, and excess cement left under the gum can irritate the tissue if not cleaned away carefully.

Screw-retained crowns, by contrast, are easy for a dentist to remove and reattach, which helps with maintenance. Neither approach is right for everyone, and many dentists use both depending on the tooth and bite. Ask your dentist which connection they plan to use and why.

Screwless dental implants cost is usually in the same general range as other single implants, since the surgery and implant are similar. Be wary of marketing that presents screwless implants as a new type of implant or a way to avoid surgery; implants of any kind require surgical placement.

What is the tooth implant process?

The tooth implant process usually takes several months and includes a consultation and scan, implant placement, a healing period while the bone bonds to the implant, and then fitting of the abutment and crown.

  1. Consultation and imaging. Your dentist examines your mouth and often orders a cone-beam CT scan to measure bone; CareCredit reports an average of $466 for a cone-beam CT.
  2. Preparatory work. Extractions, gum treatment or a bone graft may come first.
  3. Implant placement. The implant is placed in the jawbone under local anesthesia, with sedation if needed.
  4. Healing. The bone fuses to the implant over a period of months, a process called osseointegration. You may wear a temporary tooth.
  5. Abutment and crown. A connector (abutment) and custom crown, bridge or denture are attached.

Some practices offer same-day or immediate-load implants, where a temporary tooth goes on at placement. Whether that is safe depends on bone quality and bite, so follow your surgeon's advice.

After surgery, mild swelling and soreness are common for a few days. Call your dentist promptly for fever, swelling that keeps increasing, bleeding that does not stop with pressure, severe pain, or an implant that feels loose.

What do the different types of dental implants cost?

A single dental implant costs $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental, while full-arch All-on-4 implants average $15,176, according to CareCredit.

CareCredit also lists $2,143 for a single full-size implant and $4,344 for a standard endosteal implant per tooth. These figures differ because the sources measure different things: Delta Dental reports its internal data for one tooth without benefits, and CareCredit reports a separate cost survey whose line items may cover different parts of the treatment. Always check whether a quote includes the implant post only or the abutment and crown too. All figures are national averages; local prices vary. Our guide to dental implant cost breaks down each part.

Insurance can help with major work like implants. Delta Dental describes typical coverage of 50% for major services under a 100-80-50 plan, up to the annual maximum (for example $1,500). See what insurance covers dental implants and the cost of full mouth dental implants. Be cautious with very low advertised prices such as 399 dental implant deals, which often exclude the abutment, crown and scans.

Which type of dental implant is best for me?

The best type of dental implant for you depends on your bone volume, the number of missing teeth, your bite and your budget, and the decision should be made with your dentist or oral surgeon after an exam and imaging.

  • One or a few missing teeth with good bone: standard endosteal implants with crowns or a bridge.
  • Loose lower denture: mini implants or two to four standard implants to hold the denture.
  • All teeth missing in one jaw: All-on-4 or another full-arch design.
  • Severe upper bone loss: bone grafting, or zygomatic implants in specialist hands.

Implants are not the only option. Bridges and dentures cost less up front and may suit some people better. Healthline compares implants vs bridges and dentures vs implants if you are weighing your choices.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common type of dental implant?

The most common type of dental implant is the endosteal implant, a titanium or zirconia post placed directly into the jawbone. Endosteal implants support single crowns, bridges and implant-retained dentures. Other types, such as subperiosteal and zygomatic implants, are used mainly when bone is too limited for standard implants.

What are screwless dental implants?

Screwless dental implants are implants whose crown is held on without a retaining screw, usually with cement or a friction-fit connection. The implant still sits in the jawbone and still requires surgery. The design avoids a screw-access hole but can make the crown harder to remove for repairs.

Are mini dental implants cheaper?

Mini dental implants usually cost less per implant than full-size implants, because they are smaller and often placed with a simpler technique. They are commonly used to stabilize lower dentures. Total cost depends on how many are needed and the denture work involved, so ask for an itemized quote.

What are zygomatic dental implants?

Zygomatic dental implants are long implants anchored in the cheekbone instead of the upper jaw. They are used for people with severe upper jaw bone loss who cannot have standard implants or prefer to avoid extensive bone grafting. Placement is complex and done by experienced surgeons.

How much does All-on-4 cost?

All-on-4 implants for one full arch average $15,176, according to CareCredit's Synchrony cost study for 2023–2024. Your price depends on your location, the materials used for the fixed teeth and whether you need extractions or grafting first. This is a national average, and local prices vary.

Does insurance cover dental implants?

Many dental plans cover implants as major work, often at 50% under a 100-80-50 structure, up to the plan's annual maximum, according to Delta Dental. Annual maximums such as $1,500 can be used up quickly by implant treatment. Check waiting periods and ask for a pre-treatment estimate.
