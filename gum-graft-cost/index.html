How much does a gum graft cost?

A gum graft costs $2,742 on average in the United States, with most quotes falling between $2,120 and $4,982, according to CareCredit's 2024 Synchrony cost study. That figure is a national average for gum graft surgery, and your local price can sit above or below it.

The wide range reflects how different gum graft cases can be. A graft over one tooth with mild recession is a smaller procedure than grafting several neighboring teeth, and a periodontist in an expensive city usually charges more than a general dentist in a lower-cost area.

Most gum graft quotes are written per tooth or per site, meaning per area of gum being treated. Grafting adjacent teeth in one session can cost less per site than treating each tooth separately, because the surgery, anesthesia and follow-up visits are shared.

A gum graft fee may or may not include the consultation, periodontal charting, X-rays, sedation, donor tissue and post-operative checks. Ask for an itemized written estimate so you can compare quotes on equal terms. CareCredit also publishes a broader dental procedure cost list if you need to budget for related treatment such as deep cleaning.

How much does a gum graft cost in my state?

Gum graft cost changes noticeably by state, with connective tissue graft averages running from $2,341 in Oklahoma to $4,013 in Hawaii, according to CareCredit's 2024 cost study. The state figures below are averages for connective tissue grafts, the most common type of gum graft.

  • Oklahoma: $2,341 (lowest state average reported)
  • Texas: $2,536
  • Florida: $2,726
  • New York: $3,021
  • California: $3,478
  • Hawaii: $4,013 (highest state average reported)

State averages are a starting point, not a quote. Within one state, a periodontist in a major metro area and a dentist in a small town can charge very different fees for the same gum graft. Office rent, staff wages and local demand for specialist care all feed into the price.

If you live near a state line or a lower-cost city, comparing quotes across a wider area can be worthwhile. Remember to count travel time and the follow-up visits a gum graft needs, which usually include a stitch check about one to two weeks after surgery.

What affects the cost of gum graft surgery?

The cost of gum graft surgery depends mostly on how many teeth are treated, the type of graft, where the tissue comes from and who performs the surgery. Several other factors also shape the final number:

  • Number of sites: each additional tooth or gum area adds to the fee, although adjacent sites are often discounted.
  • Graft type: connective tissue, free gingival and pedicle grafts take different amounts of time and skill.
  • Tissue source: using donor tissue or a collagen matrix adds a material cost but avoids a second wound on your palate.
  • Provider: periodontists, who specialize in gums, usually charge more than general dentists.
  • Location: state and city averages vary widely, as the CareCredit figures show.
  • Sedation: local anesthesia is standard; oral or IV sedation is often billed separately.
  • Related treatment: scaling and root planing, gum disease treatment or fillings on exposed roots may be needed first.

The severity of your gum recession also matters. Deeper recession or thin gum tissue can call for a larger graft or a more complex technique, which takes more chair time. The Cleveland Clinic guide to gum recession explains how recession develops and why grafting is recommended.

What are the types of gum grafts and which costs more?

The three main types of gum grafts are connective tissue grafts, free gingival grafts and pedicle grafts, and some dentists also use donor tissue or collagen materials instead of tissue from your mouth. The type your dentist recommends depends on how much gum you have, how thick it is and what the graft needs to achieve.

Connective tissue graft. A connective tissue graft is the most common technique for covering exposed roots. The surgeon opens a small flap on the roof of your mouth, takes tissue from beneath the surface and stitches it over the receded area. CareCredit's state averages for this graft range from $2,341 to $4,013.

Free gingival graft. A free gingival graft takes a thin strip of tissue directly from the surface of the palate. Dentists often choose a free gingival graft to thicken thin gums and add a firm band of protective tissue, rather than mainly to cover roots.

Pedicle graft. A pedicle graft, also called a lateral graft, slides gum tissue over from a neighboring tooth while keeping it attached at one edge. A pedicle graft only works when there is plenty of healthy gum next to the receded area.

Donor tissue and collagen grafts. Processed donor tissue or collagen matrix can replace tissue from your palate. Donor material adds a material cost but means one surgical site instead of two, which many people find more comfortable. The Healthline overview of gum grafts compares these options in more detail.

Does dental insurance cover a gum graft?

Dental insurance often covers part of a gum graft when the surgery treats gum recession for health reasons, such as exposed roots, sensitivity or gum loss that threatens the tooth. Plans are far less likely to pay when a graft is done mainly to improve the look of the gumline.

Most dental plans place gum grafts under major or periodontal surgery benefits. Coverage depends on your plan's percentage for major work, your deductible, any waiting period for major services and your annual maximum. A single gum graft can use a large share of a typical annual maximum, so check your remaining benefits before scheduling.

Insurers usually want documentation that the graft is necessary. That often means periodontal charting showing pocket depths and recession measurements, X-rays and photos. Ask your dentist or periodontist to submit a pre-treatment estimate so you see in writing what the plan expects to pay.

If several teeth need grafting, splitting the work between two plan years can let you use two annual maximums. Talk to your periodontist about whether delaying part of the treatment is clinically safe for your situation before you plan around benefits.

Medical insurance rarely pays for a gum graft unless the treatment is linked to an injury or a covered medical condition. Your dental plan is usually the main source of coverage.

How can I pay less for a gum graft?

The most effective ways to pay less for a gum graft are using in-network providers, timing treatment around your insurance year and spreading payments over time. Several options work well together:

  1. Choose an in-network periodontist. In-network offices accept discounted fees agreed with your insurer, which lowers your share even before benefits apply.
  2. Treat adjacent teeth together. Grafting neighboring sites in one session often costs less per site than separate surgeries.
  3. Look at dental schools. University periodontics programs often offer gum grafts at reduced fees, with residents supervised by experienced faculty.
  4. Use HSA or FSA funds. A gum graft that treats recession is usually a qualified dental expense; confirm with your plan administrator.
  5. Ask about payment plans. Many offices offer in-house payment plans or third-party dental financing; read the interest terms closely, especially deferred-interest offers.
  6. Compare two or three quotes. Make sure each quote covers the same number of sites, the same graft type and the same follow-up visits.

Prevention also saves money. Gentle brushing with a soft brush, treating gum disease early and wearing a night guard if you grind can slow recession and may reduce how many sites eventually need grafting.

What happens during gum graft surgery?

Gum graft surgery is an outpatient procedure that usually takes about one to two hours, done under local anesthesia with optional sedation. You go home the same day.

  1. Preparation. The periodontist numbs the area and cleans the exposed root surface.
  2. Harvesting tissue. For a connective tissue or free gingival graft, a small piece of tissue is taken from the roof of your mouth. With donor tissue, this step is skipped.
  3. Placing the graft. The graft is positioned over the receded area and secured with fine stitches.
  4. Protection. Some surgeons place a protective dressing over the palate or graft site.

Recovery from a gum graft usually involves soreness and some swelling for the first several days, a soft-food diet and avoiding brushing the graft area until your periodontist says it is safe. Most people return to work within a day or two. The gum continues to mature over the following weeks.

Follow your periodontist's instructions on rinsing, pain relief and diet closely, because disturbing the graft early is a common reason grafts fail to take. Contact your periodontist promptly if you notice bleeding that will not stop with gentle pressure, swelling that keeps increasing after the first few days, fever, pus or severe pain.

What if a gum graft fails, and what does that cost?

A failed gum graft usually means the graft tissue did not attach to its new blood supply and pulled away, shrank significantly or died off, and repeating the surgery means paying for a second procedure. Gum grafts have a good track record, but failure can happen, especially with smoking, poor healing or early disturbance of the site.

Signs that a gum graft may be failing include the graft turning white or gray and not recovering, tissue pulling away from the tooth, or the root becoming exposed again. Some color change is normal in the first days, so ask your periodontist rather than guessing. Our guide to a failed gum graft covers warning signs and next steps.

Before agreeing to surgery, ask your periodontist what happens if the graft does not take. Some practices reduce the fee for a repeat graft, while others charge the full price. Getting that policy in writing protects your budget.

A successful gum graft should cover or protect the exposed root and leave a firmer, healthier gumline. Results take a few months to settle. To see what realistic healing looks like, browse gum graft before and after examples.

Is a gum graft worth the cost?

A gum graft is often worth the cost when gum recession is exposing roots, causing sensitivity or putting a tooth at risk, because untreated recession can lead to root decay and tooth loss. Replacing a lost tooth usually costs more than protecting it with a graft.

Exposed roots are softer than enamel and more prone to decay and wear. Grafting adds a protective layer of gum, can reduce sensitivity to cold and sweet foods, and can stop recession from worsening. Many people also like the more even gumline that results.

A gum graft is less essential when recession is mild, stable and not causing symptoms. In that case, your dentist may suggest monitoring the area, changing your brushing technique and treating any gum disease first. Ask your dentist or periodontist to measure your recession over time so you can decide based on real change rather than appearance alone.

Timing affects value too. Gum graft surgery is usually simpler and more predictable when recession is caught early, before a large area of root is exposed or bone support around the tooth has been lost. Waiting until a tooth is loose or decayed at the root can mean extra treatment on top of the graft, and sometimes the tooth cannot be saved at all.

Gum grafting is a decision to make with your periodontist, using your own measurements and X-rays. National averages such as $2,742 help you budget, but your treatment plan and written estimate are what you should rely on.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a gum graft cost per tooth?

A gum graft costs $2,742 on average, with a range of $2,120 to $4,982, according to CareCredit's 2024 Synchrony cost study. Many offices price grafts per tooth or per site, and treating neighboring teeth in one session can lower the cost per site. Your local price depends on your state, provider and graft type.

Is gum graft surgery painful?

Gum graft surgery itself should not hurt because the area is numbed with local anesthesia. Afterward, soreness and swelling are common for several days, especially at the palate if tissue was taken from there. Your periodontist will recommend pain relief. Severe or worsening pain, fever or heavy bleeding needs a prompt call.

Does insurance cover gum graft surgery?

Dental insurance often covers part of gum graft surgery when it treats recession for health reasons, usually under major or periodontal benefits. Coverage depends on your deductible, annual maximum and waiting periods. Ask your periodontist to file a pre-treatment estimate so you know your share before surgery.

How long does gum graft recovery take?

Initial gum graft recovery usually takes one to two weeks, with soreness easing over the first several days and stitches checked or removed at a follow-up visit. Full healing and maturing of the gum tissue continues for several weeks after that. Follow your periodontist's specific instructions on eating, brushing and rinsing.

Why does gum graft cost vary so much by state?

Gum graft cost varies by state because office rent, staff wages and demand for specialists differ. CareCredit's 2024 study shows connective tissue graft averages from $2,341 in Oklahoma to $4,013 in Hawaii, with California at $3,478 and Texas at $2,536. Prices also vary within a state between cities and towns.

Can I get a gum graft at a dental school?

Many university dental schools with periodontics programs offer gum grafts at reduced fees. Residents perform the surgery under supervision from experienced periodontists. Appointments tend to take longer and you may wait for a slot, but the savings can be meaningful if you are paying without insurance.
