How Much Does Dental Insurance Cost? Premiums, Coverage and What You Really Pay

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

How much dental insurance costs depends on the monthly premium plus what you pay at the dentist. A typical plan covers 100% of preventive care, 80% of basic care and 50% of major care, up to an annual maximum such as $1,500, according to Delta Dental. Premiums vary by plan, age and state.

Key takeaways
  • The true cost of dental insurance is the premium plus your deductible, coinsurance and anything above the annual maximum.
  • Delta Dental describes a typical plan as 100% preventive, 80% basic and 50% major coverage.
  • Annual maximums cap what the plan pays each year; Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example.
  • Employer plans usually cost the employee less than buying an individual plan, because the employer pays part of the premium.
  • Plans with no waiting period exist, but they often carry higher premiums or lower early coverage.

How much does dental insurance cost per month?

Dental insurance costs a monthly premium that varies with the type of plan, whether it comes through an employer, your age, where you live and how many people are covered. There is no single national price, so the most useful way to judge a plan is to add up the premium and the out-of-pocket costs it leaves you with.

Premiums are only the first layer. On top of them you pay a yearly deductible before most basic and major benefits start, a coinsurance share of each covered procedure, and the full cost of anything after the plan reaches its annual maximum. Preventive visits are often exempt from the deductible.

Employer-sponsored plans are usually the least expensive way to get dental coverage, because the employer pays part of the premium and negotiates group rates. Individual plans bought directly from an insurer or through a marketplace cost more for the same coverage, and plans for families cost more than single coverage.

When comparing quotes, write down four numbers for each plan: monthly premium, deductible, coinsurance by category, and annual maximum. Those four numbers tell you more than the plan name or marketing.

What does dental insurance cover?

Dental insurance usually covers preventive care at 100%, basic care at 80% and major care at 50%, a structure often called a 100-80-50 plan. Delta Dental lists fillings, root canals and extractions as typical basic services, and crowns, bridges and implants as typical major services.

Preventive care means exams, cleanings and routine X-rays, often twice a year. Because a 100-80-50 plan pays preventive care in full, regular checkups usually cost you nothing beyond the premium when you see an in-network dentist.

Basic care at 80% means the plan pays most of a filling or extraction and you pay the rest. Some plans place root canals or gum treatment in the major category instead, so check the plan's own list rather than assuming.

Major care at 50% means you split crowns, bridges, implants and dentures roughly evenly with the plan, subject to the annual maximum. Many plans exclude implants entirely or limit how often a crown can be replaced.

Cosmetic work such as whitening and veneers is usually not covered at all. Our guide to cosmetic dentistry cost explains what those treatments typically run and how people pay for them.

What is a dental insurance annual maximum?

A dental insurance annual maximum is the most the plan will pay for your care in one benefit year; after you reach it, you pay the full cost until the plan resets. Delta Dental explains how the maximum works, and its coverage guide uses $1,500 as an example annual maximum.

The annual maximum is the single biggest limit on what dental insurance is worth. A plan with a $1,500 maximum can comfortably cover cleanings, X-rays and a few fillings in a year. A crown plus a root canal plus an implant in the same year can push total costs well past that cap, leaving you to pay the difference.

Unused benefits generally do not roll over, although a few plans offer limited rollover for people who keep up with preventive visits. Many plans reset on January 1, others on the policy anniversary.

If you need several major procedures, ask your dentist whether some can safely be spread across two benefit years. Splitting treatment can let you use two annual maximums instead of one.

Dental insurance cost by plan type

Dental insurance cost differs by plan type, mainly because each type trades monthly price against your choice of dentist. The three common structures are DHMO, PPO and indemnity plans, with dental discount plans as a separate, non-insurance option.

  • DHMO (dental HMO). Usually the lowest premiums. You choose a primary dentist from a network, need referrals for specialists, and pay set copays. Out-of-network care is typically not covered.
  • PPO (preferred provider organization). The most common employer plan. Premiums are moderate, you can see any dentist, and in-network dentists charge discounted fees. Out-of-network care is covered at a lower level.
  • Indemnity (fee-for-service). Usually the highest premiums but the most freedom. The plan pays a share of a set allowance, and you may be billed the difference if your dentist charges more.
  • Dental discount plan. Not insurance. You pay a membership fee and get reduced prices at participating dentists, with no annual maximum and usually no waiting period.

A cheaper premium is not always the cheaper plan. A low-premium DHMO can work out well if you like the network dentists, while a PPO can save money for someone who needs a specialist or wants to keep a current dentist.

What affects the cost of dental insurance?

The cost of dental insurance is shaped by who pays the premium, how much the plan covers and how large the network is. The same insurer can sell plans at very different prices depending on those choices.

  • Employer contribution. Group plans with an employer share cost employees much less than individual plans.
  • Coverage level. Plans that pay more for basic and major care, or have no waiting period, charge higher premiums.
  • Annual maximum. A higher maximum usually means a higher premium.
  • Deductible. A higher deductible lowers the premium but raises what you pay before benefits start.
  • Network type. DHMO plans cost less than PPO plans, which cost less than indemnity plans.
  • Who is covered. Family and couple coverage costs more than individual coverage.
  • Age and location. Individual plan pricing can vary by age bracket and by state or ZIP code.

Bundled coverage also matters. Some medical plans include pediatric dental benefits, and some Medicare Advantage plans include limited dental coverage, which can reduce or remove the need for a separate plan.

Dental insurance without a waiting period

Dental insurance without a waiting period lets you use basic or major benefits soon after the plan starts, but those plans usually charge higher premiums or pay less in the first year. Most individual plans cover preventive care right away and apply waiting periods to basic and major services, often several months for basic work and longer for major work.

Waiting periods exist so people do not buy a plan only for a single expensive procedure and then cancel. Employer group plans often have short or no waiting periods, because enrollment is not tied to a sudden need.

If you need a crown or root canal soon, compare the total first-year cost. Add a year of premiums, the deductible and your coinsurance share, and set that against the dentist's cash price. Sometimes a dental discount plan or a cash-pay discount costs less than a no-waiting-period insurance plan for one urgent procedure.

If you are switching from employer coverage, ask the new insurer whether it will waive waiting periods with proof of prior continuous dental coverage. Many insurers will.

Dental care cost with vs without insurance

Dental care costs less with insurance mainly because of negotiated in-network fees and full coverage for preventive visits. Without insurance, you pay each dentist's full fee, though many offices offer a cash discount or in-house membership plan.

Here is how a 100-80-50 plan changes the bill in practice. A cleaning and exam is typically covered in full. A filling or extraction is covered at 80% after the deductible, so you pay the remaining share of the in-network fee. A crown is covered at 50%, so you pay half of the in-network fee, and anything beyond the annual maximum is yours alone.

To see typical cash prices for common procedures, CareCredit publishes a dental procedure cost list based on the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study. Those survey averages are what an uninsured patient might expect to pay, while insurer figures reflect claims data, so the two sources measure different things and do not always match.

Whether a plan pays off depends on the care you expect to need. Our guide to whether dental insurance is worth it walks through the math for people who only need checkups and for people facing major work.

How to pay less for dental insurance and dental care

The easiest way to pay less for dental insurance is to take an employer plan if one is offered, and then use in-network dentists so every claim is priced at negotiated fees. Beyond that, a few habits stretch your benefits further.

  • Use every preventive visit. Cleanings and exams covered at 100% catch problems while they are cheap to fix.
  • Stay in-network. In-network fees lower both the plan's payment and your share.
  • Get pre-treatment estimates. Ask the dental office to submit major work to the insurer before treatment so you know your share in advance.
  • Time major work. Spreading treatment across benefit years can use two annual maximums.
  • Use HSA or FSA funds. Deductibles, coinsurance and most dental treatment are qualified medical expenses for pre-tax accounts. Dental premiums paid with after-tax money are not usually HSA-eligible, so check the rules for your account.
  • Consider dental schools. Dental school clinics offer supervised care at lower fees, which helps when you have no coverage or have used your maximum.
  • Ask about payment plans. Many dentists offer in-house payment plans for the portion insurance does not cover.

Check whether your state's Medicaid program or a community health center offers dental care if your income is limited. Coverage for adults varies widely from state to state.

Dental insurance cost for families, children and seniors

Dental insurance for families, children and seniors is priced and structured differently from single adult coverage, so the best option depends on who needs care. Family plans cost more per month than individual plans, but they usually cover each person's preventive visits in full, which adds up quickly with children who need cleanings, sealants and fluoride.

Children often have other routes to coverage. Many marketplace medical plans include pediatric dental benefits, and children in lower-income households may qualify for dental care through Medicaid or the Children's Health Insurance Program. Orthodontic coverage, when a plan offers it, usually has its own lifetime maximum separate from the annual maximum.

Seniors face a different gap. Original Medicare does not cover routine dental care such as cleanings, fillings, extractions or dentures. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits, often with lower annual maximums than employer plans, and stand-alone senior dental plans are sold separately.

Retirees losing employer coverage should compare a stand-alone dental plan, a Medicare Advantage plan's dental benefit and a dental discount plan side by side, using the care they expect to need in the first year.

How to choose a dental insurance plan

Choosing a dental insurance plan comes down to matching the plan's limits to the care you expect over the next year or two. Start by listing what you already know you need, from routine cleanings to a planned crown or orthodontic treatment for a child.

  1. Check whether your current dentist is in the plan's network.
  2. Compare the annual maximum with the care you expect; a $1,500 maximum, the example Delta Dental uses, goes quickly with major work.
  3. Read the waiting periods for basic and major services.
  4. Look for exclusions such as implants, missing-tooth clauses for teeth lost before coverage began, and frequency limits on crowns.
  5. Check whether orthodontics is included and whether it covers adults or only children.
  6. Add a full year of premiums to the deductible and your coinsurance on planned work, then compare that total across plans.

A plan with a slightly higher premium can still cost less overall if it covers the work you actually need. Ask the insurer for the full benefits summary, not just the brochure, before you enroll.

Frequently asked questions

How much does dental insurance cost for one person?

Dental insurance for one person costs a monthly premium that varies by plan type, coverage level, age and state, with employer plans usually costing the employee much less than individual plans. Compare the premium together with the deductible, coinsurance and annual maximum, because those determine what you actually pay at the dentist each year.

What does a 100-80-50 dental plan mean?

A 100-80-50 dental plan pays 100% of preventive care, 80% of basic care such as fillings, root canals and extractions, and 50% of major care such as crowns, bridges and implants, according to Delta Dental. Coverage usually applies after the deductible and up to the plan's annual maximum.

Is there dental insurance with no waiting period?

Dental insurance with no waiting period is available from some insurers, especially for preventive care, and some plans also cover basic or major work right away. Those plans tend to cost more or pay less in the first year. Employer plans and insurers that credit prior coverage often waive waiting periods.

What happens when I reach my dental insurance annual maximum?

Reaching your dental insurance annual maximum means the plan stops paying for the rest of the benefit year, so you pay the full fee for further treatment. Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example annual maximum. The amount resets at the start of the next benefit year, and unused benefits usually do not roll over.

Does dental insurance cover cosmetic dentistry?

Dental insurance usually does not cover cosmetic dentistry such as teeth whitening or veneers placed only for appearance. Procedures that restore function, like a crown on a broken tooth, may be covered as major care even if they also improve appearance. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate to confirm what your plan will pay.

Can I use my HSA for dental insurance premiums?

HSA funds generally cannot be used for dental insurance premiums, though there are limited exceptions in IRS rules. HSA and FSA funds can be used for deductibles, coinsurance and most dental treatment, including fillings, crowns, root canals and extractions. Check your account administrator's rules before paying.
