Dentures vs Implants: Costs, Pros and Cons, and How to Decide

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated November 1, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

Dentures are removable replacement teeth that rest on the gums, while implants are posts fixed in the jawbone that support crowns, bridges or full-arch teeth. Dentures cost much less upfront; implants feel more like natural teeth, chew better and help preserve bone. A full-arch All-on-4 averages $15,176 according to CareCredit.

Key takeaways
  • Dentures are removable and sit on the gums; implants are fixed in the jawbone.
  • Dentures cost far less upfront; implants cost more but often last longer.
  • Implants restore stronger chewing and help prevent bone loss.
  • Implant-supported or snap-in dentures are a middle-ground option.
  • Bone loss, health conditions and budget all shape the right choice.

What is the difference between dentures and implants?

The main difference between dentures and implants is how they are held in the mouth. Conventional dentures are removable plates of false teeth that rest on the gums. Upper dentures are held mainly by suction against the palate; lower dentures rely on the shape of the ridge, the tongue and cheeks, and sometimes adhesive.

Implants are titanium or zirconia posts placed in the jawbone. Once the bone bonds to them, they act like artificial roots and can support a single crown, a bridge or a full set of teeth. Implant-supported teeth do not move when you eat or speak. Healthline's dentures versus implants guide compares the two in detail.

Are dentures cheaper than implants?

Dentures are much cheaper than implants upfront. A full set of conventional dentures generally costs a fraction of a full-arch implant solution, which CareCredit averages at $15,176 per arch for All-on-4.

For individual teeth, Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits, according to its implant cost guide. Over the long term, dentures need relines, repairs and replacement every several years, and implants may need crown replacements, so the lifetime cost difference is smaller than the upfront gap suggests. Which is more comfortable, dentures or implants?

Implants are usually more comfortable for daily life once healed, because they are fixed and do not rub or slip. Many denture wearers deal with sore spots, especially in the first months and as the gums change shape.

Upper dentures cover the palate, which can affect taste and the sensation of hot food. Lower dentures are often the hardest to keep stable because there is less surface for suction. Implant-supported teeth leave the palate uncovered and stay in place when you speak, laugh and eat.

Can you eat normally with dentures or implants?

You can eat more normally with implants than with conventional dentures. Implants anchor teeth in the bone, so chewing force is close to that of natural teeth. People with implant-supported teeth can usually eat crunchy and chewy foods with confidence.

Conventional dentures provide much less chewing power. Many people learn to cut food into small pieces, avoid sticky or hard foods, and chew on both sides at once to keep dentures stable. Snap-in implant dentures improve this considerably while costing less than fully fixed implant teeth.

How do dentures and implants affect jawbone?

Implants help preserve the jawbone, while conventional dentures do not. Without tooth roots, the jawbone gradually shrinks, and pressure from dentures resting on the gums can speed this up in some people.

Over years, bone loss changes how dentures fit, requiring relines or new dentures, and can change facial appearance, with lips and cheeks appearing sunken. Implants transmit chewing force into the bone, helping maintain its volume. If bone has already been lost, a graft may be needed before implants; the Cleveland Clinic explains bone grafts, and CareCredit averages them at $811 for an allograft and $2,779 for an autograft.

What about implant-supported or snap-in dentures?

Implant-supported or snap-in dentures are a middle ground between conventional dentures and fully fixed implants. A denture clips onto two to four implants, making it far more stable while still being removable for cleaning.

They cost more than conventional dentures but less than fixed full-arch implant teeth, and they offer a big improvement in stability, especially for lower dentures. Ask your dentist whether this option suits your bone and budget. See types of dental implants for more.

Who is a good candidate for dentures or implants?

Good candidates for implants have enough jawbone or are willing to have grafting, healthy gums, and are healthy enough for minor oral surgery. Smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, some medications and previous radiation to the jaw can raise the risk of implant failure.

Dentures may be preferable for people who cannot have surgery, have significant bone loss and do not want grafting, or need an affordable solution quickly. Many people start with dentures and add implants later. The Cleveland Clinic's prosthodontics guide explains how prosthodontists plan these options.

Does insurance cover dentures and implants?

Insurance more commonly covers dentures than implants. Many plans include dentures as a major service, which Delta Dental describes as typically covered at 50%, up to the annual maximum, with $1,500 as an example, in its coverage guide.

Implant coverage varies; some plans exclude implants or limit payment to what dentures would cost. Always request a pre-treatment estimate. See what insurance covers for dental implants and our main dental implant cost guide.

What should you ask your dentist when choosing?

When choosing between dentures and implants, ask questions that make costs and expectations clear. How much bone do I have, and would I need a graft? What will each option cost in total, including scans, extractions and temporary teeth? How long will each last, and what maintenance is needed? How will each affect eating and speech?

A cone-beam CT scan, averaging $466 per CareCredit, is often used to plan implants. Get written quotes for each option and allow time to decide. Many dentists offer a staged plan that begins with affordable dentures and adds implants later.

What is daily life like with dentures versus implants?

Daily life with conventional dentures involves a routine of removing, cleaning and soaking them, and many people use adhesive to improve stability. Some people feel self-conscious about dentures slipping when they laugh, sneeze or eat in public, especially with lower dentures.

Implant-supported fixed teeth stay in place all the time and are brushed in the mouth, much like natural teeth, with special brushes or a water flosser to clean underneath. Snap-in implant dentures still come out for cleaning, but they lock into place firmly during the day. For many people, the confidence of teeth that do not move is the biggest difference between the two.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better, dentures or implants?

Implants are usually better for comfort, chewing and bone preservation, while dentures are better for lower upfront cost and avoiding surgery. Implant-supported snap-in dentures offer a middle ground. The best choice depends on your bone, health and budget.

Are implants more expensive than dentures?

Implants are much more expensive than conventional dentures upfront. CareCredit averages All-on-4 at $15,176 per arch, and Delta Dental puts a single implant at $2,800 to $5,600. Dentures need relines and replacements over time, which narrows the long-term difference.

Do dentures cause bone loss?

Dentures do not stop the bone loss that happens after teeth are removed, and pressure on the gums can contribute to it. Over years, this changes denture fit and facial appearance. Implants help preserve bone by transmitting chewing forces into the jaw.

Can I switch from dentures to implants later?

You can usually switch from dentures to implants later, though bone loss over time may require a bone graft first. Some people convert their existing dentures into snap-in dentures supported by a few implants.

What are snap-in dentures?

Snap-in dentures are removable dentures that clip onto two to four dental implants, making them much more stable than conventional dentures. They cost more than regular dentures but less than fully fixed implant teeth.
