Dental Cleaning Cost in 2026: Standard, Deep and Periodontal Cleanings Compared

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

The typical dental cleaning cost for a standard adult cleaning is $85 to $160 without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental. The exam and X-rays are usually billed separately, and deep cleanings for gum disease cost more. Most dental insurance plans treat routine cleanings as preventive care.

Key takeaways
  • Delta Dental puts a standard cleaning at $85 to $160 without dental benefits; the exam and X-rays are separate line items.
  • Deep cleaning (scaling and root planing) is priced per quadrant: $180 to $295 per Delta Dental, or a $242 average in CareCredit's survey.
  • A first visit with an exam, X-rays and a cleaning costs much more than a cleaning alone.
  • Most dental plans cover routine cleanings as preventive care, often twice a year with little or nothing to pay.
  • Dental hygiene schools, in-network dentists and in-office membership plans are the easiest ways to pay less without insurance.

How much is a dental cleaning?

A standard dental cleaning costs $85 to $160 without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental. That range covers a routine adult cleaning, called a prophylaxis, for someone with healthy gums. It is a national figure, so your local price may be higher or lower.

A dental cleaning is rarely the only item on the bill. Most visits also include an exam by the dentist, and X-rays are taken at regular intervals. When people ask how much a teeth cleaning costs, they often mean the whole visit, which can add up to several times the cleaning fee on its own.

Price also depends on the type of cleaning you need. A routine cleaning is the least expensive option. A full mouth debridement, scaling and root planing, or periodontal maintenance cleaning each costs more, because each takes longer and treats gum disease or heavy buildup rather than simply polishing healthy teeth.

Before booking, ask the office whether the quoted price is for the cleaning only or for the cleaning, exam and X-rays together. That one question avoids most billing surprises.

What is the average cost of a dental cleaning visit with exam and X-rays?

The average cost of a dental cleaning visit with an exam and X-rays is the cleaning fee plus several separate charges, so a full visit costs noticeably more than the cleaning alone. The Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study published by CareCredit lists a routine exam at a national average of $203, with a range of $50 to $350.

X-rays are the other common add-on. CareCredit lists bitewing X-rays, which check for decay between back teeth, at an average of $65, and full-mouth X-rays at an average of $226. Bitewings are commonly taken about once a year for adults, while a full-mouth series is usually taken at a first visit and then only every few years.

Here is how the separate items typically stack up at a visit:

  • A returning patient with healthy gums often pays for a cleaning and an exam, with bitewings at some visits.
  • A new patient usually pays for a cleaning, a comprehensive exam and a full set of X-rays, making the first visit the most expensive.
  • A patient with gum disease may be booked for a separate deep cleaning visit after the exam.

Why do dental cleaning prices differ between sources?

Dental cleaning prices differ between sources because each publisher measures something slightly different. Delta Dental reports ranges of what dentists charge for each cleaning type without dental benefits, while CareCredit reports survey averages for individual procedures.

The deep cleaning numbers show the difference clearly. Delta Dental gives scaling and root planing a range of $180 to $295 per quadrant, while CareCredit lists a national average of $242 per quadrant. The CareCredit average sits inside the Delta Dental range, which is a good sign that both describe similar real-world fees from different angles.

Local factors matter more than either national figure. Dental fees in large metro areas usually run higher than in smaller towns, and a practice with high overhead or specialist staff may charge more for the same procedure code. A periodontist, for example, may charge differently from a general dentist for gum treatment.

Use the national numbers to judge whether a quote is reasonable. If a quote sits far outside the ranges here, ask what is included and why.

How much do different types of dental cleaning cost?

Different types of dental cleaning cost different amounts, from $85 to $160 for a standard cleaning up to $180 to $295 per quadrant for scaling and root planing, based on Delta Dental figures. Your dentist chooses the type based on gum health and how much buildup is present.

  • Standard cleaning (prophylaxis): $85 to $160. Removes plaque and tartar above and slightly below the gumline, followed by polishing. Suits people with healthy gums.
  • Full mouth debridement: $150 to $260. Used when heavy tartar makes it impossible to examine the teeth properly. A debridement is often a first step, followed by an exam and a standard or deep cleaning at a later visit.
  • Scaling and root planing: $180 to $295 per quadrant. A deep cleaning for gum disease that cleans below the gumline and smooths root surfaces. CareCredit lists a $242 average per quadrant.
  • Periodontal maintenance: $140 to $220. An ongoing cleaning for people who have been treated for gum disease, usually scheduled more often than routine cleanings.

Because scaling and root planing is priced per quadrant (one quarter of the mouth), a full-mouth deep cleaning means up to four quadrant fees. Many patients need only some quadrants treated. What changes the price of a teeth cleaning?

The price of a teeth cleaning changes mainly with gum health, the amount of tartar, whether you are a new patient, where you live, and whether the dentist is in your insurance network. Small differences in each factor add up across a visit.

  • Gum health. Pockets around the teeth that are deeper than normal may mean a deep cleaning instead of a routine one.
  • Tartar buildup. A long gap since your last cleaning often means more tartar and sometimes a debridement first.
  • New patient status. First visits usually include a comprehensive exam and full-mouth X-rays.
  • Location. City practices generally charge more than rural ones.
  • Network status. In-network dentists charge insured patients a negotiated fee that is often lower than their standard fee.
  • Extras. Fluoride varnish, sealants, oral cancer screening devices and numbing for sensitive teeth may be billed separately.

The single biggest swing is gum health. A routine cleaning every six months is far cheaper than treating gum disease once it develops, which is why regular cleanings tend to save money over time.

How much does a dental cleaning cost with insurance?

A dental cleaning with insurance often costs little or nothing out of pocket, because most dental plans treat routine cleanings as preventive care. Delta Dental explains that preventive services such as cleanings and exams usually receive the highest level of coverage in a typical plan.

Coverage still has limits worth checking:

  • Frequency. Many plans cover two routine cleanings per year. A third cleaning may be your cost.
  • Deep cleanings. Scaling and root planing is often classed as a basic or periodontal service, which means the plan pays a smaller share and a deductible may apply.
  • Periodontal maintenance. Some plans count these visits toward the same twice-yearly limit as routine cleanings.
  • Network. Out-of-network dentists may bill you the difference between their fee and what the plan allows.
  • Annual maximum. Preventive care may or may not count toward the yearly cap, depending on the plan.

The Delta Dental dental cleaning guide describes what happens at preventive visits. If your dentist recommends a deep cleaning, ask the office to send a pre-treatment estimate so you know your share before treatment begins.

Billing codes explain many surprise charges. A routine cleaning, a periodontal maintenance cleaning and scaling and root planing each have a different code, and a plan can treat each code differently. If the office changes the planned cleaning type after the exam, the insurance share can change too, so ask which code will be billed whenever the plan for your visit changes. Keeping your explanation of benefits statements also helps you track how many covered cleanings remain in the plan year.

How much is a dental cleaning without insurance?

A dental cleaning without insurance costs $85 to $160 for the cleaning alone, according to Delta Dental, plus the exam and any X-rays. Self-pay patients see the full fee for each line item, which is why a first visit can feel expensive.

Many practices now offer ways to lower self-pay prices:

  • In-office membership plans. An annual fee that typically covers two cleanings, exams and routine X-rays, plus discounts on other work.
  • Cash discounts. Some offices reduce fees for payment at the time of service.
  • Dental savings plans. Discount plans that give reduced fees at participating dentists. These are not insurance, so check which offices accept them.
  • New patient specials. Introductory offers can be useful, but confirm exactly what is included and what happens if a deep cleaning is recommended.

Our detailed guide on how much a dental cleaning costs without insurance compares these options and lists questions to ask before your visit.

Self-pay patients benefit most from asking for a written estimate before the cleaning starts. A written estimate should list the cleaning, the exam and each type of X-ray, so you can decline anything that is optional or ask whether recent X-rays from another dentist can be transferred instead of retaken. Transferring X-rays you already paid for is a simple way to avoid paying for the same images twice.

How can you pay less for a teeth cleaning?

You can pay less for a teeth cleaning by visiting a dental hygiene or dental school clinic, using a community health center, choosing in-network dentists, paying with HSA or FSA funds, and keeping up with regular cleanings so deep cleanings are less likely.

  1. Dental hygiene and dental schools. Students clean teeth under supervision at reduced fees. Appointments often take longer, but the care is checked by licensed instructors.
  2. Community health centers. Federally funded health centers often provide dental services with fees based on income.
  3. In-network dentists. If you have insurance, an in-network office keeps fees at the negotiated rate.
  4. HSA and FSA accounts. Dental cleanings are an eligible expense, so paying with pre-tax money reduces the effective price.
  5. Stay on schedule. Regular cleanings keep tartar manageable and reduce the chance of needing a debridement or scaling and root planing.
  6. Ask before extras. Optional add-ons can be declined if they are not clinically needed; ask your dentist which items are recommended for your situation.

Medicaid dental coverage for adults varies by state. Some states include cleanings for adults, while others limit adult dental benefits, so check with your state program.

What happens during a dental cleaning?

A dental cleaning involves a quick check of your mouth, removal of plaque and tartar with hand or ultrasonic instruments, polishing, and often flossing and fluoride. A routine adult cleaning usually takes under an hour, and the dentist typically examines your teeth at the same visit.

  1. Review and check. The hygienist reviews your health history and looks at your teeth and gums, often measuring gum pocket depths.
  2. Scaling. Tartar is removed from tooth surfaces and just under the gumline.
  3. Polishing. A gritty paste smooths the teeth and removes surface stains.
  4. Flossing and fluoride. Teeth are flossed, and fluoride may be applied to strengthen enamel.
  5. Exam. The dentist checks for decay, gum disease and other concerns, reviewing any X-rays.

Mild gum tenderness or slight bleeding after a cleaning is common, especially if it has been a while. Deep cleanings may leave the gums sore for a few days, and your dentist may suggest a rinse or specific home care. Follow your dentist's instructions after any deep cleaning.

Call your dentist promptly if you notice facial swelling, fever, bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure, or severe pain after a cleaning or at any time. Gums that bleed easily, persistent bad breath, or teeth that feel loose are also reasons to book a visit, because they can be signs of gum disease.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a teeth cleaning cost without insurance?

A teeth cleaning without insurance costs $85 to $160 for a standard adult cleaning, according to Delta Dental. The exam and X-rays are usually billed separately; CareCredit lists a routine exam at a national average of $203 and bitewing X-rays at an average of $65. Local prices vary.

How much is a deep cleaning compared with a regular cleaning?

A deep cleaning costs more than a regular cleaning because it is billed per quadrant. Delta Dental lists scaling and root planing at $180 to $295 per quadrant, and CareCredit lists a $242 average, compared with $85 to $160 for a standard cleaning. Many patients need only some quadrants treated.

Does insurance cover dental cleanings?

Most dental insurance plans cover routine dental cleanings as preventive care, often twice a year with little or nothing to pay at an in-network dentist. Deep cleanings and periodontal maintenance may be covered at a lower level and can be subject to a deductible, so check your plan details.

How often should you get a dental cleaning?

Many adults get a dental cleaning about every six months, but the right interval depends on your gum health. People treated for gum disease often need periodontal maintenance more frequently. Follow your dentist's recommendation, since a schedule that suits your mouth helps prevent costlier treatment later.

Why is my first dental cleaning visit so expensive?

A first dental cleaning visit is often expensive because it usually includes a comprehensive exam and a full set of X-rays along with the cleaning. CareCredit lists full-mouth X-rays at an average of $226. Follow-up visits are usually cheaper because full-mouth X-rays are not repeated every time.

What is periodontal maintenance and how much does it cost?

Periodontal maintenance is an ongoing cleaning for people previously treated for gum disease. Delta Dental lists periodontal maintenance at $140 to $220 per visit without dental benefits. These cleanings focus on keeping gum pockets clean and are often scheduled more often than standard cleanings.
