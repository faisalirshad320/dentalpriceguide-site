How Much Is a Root Canal Without Insurance?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 26, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Without insurance, a root canal costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, $1,300 for a premolar and $1,500 for a molar, based on Delta Dental's out-of-network data. Those fees do not include the crown or filling that usually follows, which is a separate charge.

  • Dental schools, payment plans and membership plans can lower the cost.

How much does a root canal cost without insurance?

A root canal without insurance costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, about $1,300 for a premolar and about $1,500 for a molar, according to Delta Dental's 2025 out-of-network data. Price rises toward the back of the mouth because back teeth have more canals and are harder to reach.

Other sources tell a similar story. CareCredit's procedure cost list reports averages of $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar. Pearl's dental cost data gives a national range of $685 to $1,800 for root canal codes D3310 to D3330, with an average of $1,050.

The sources differ because they measure different things. Delta Dental reports out-of-network claims, CareCredit reports survey averages, and Pearl draws on ADA fee survey data. All three are national figures, and your local price may be higher or lower.

Why does a molar root canal cost more?

A molar root canal costs more than a front tooth root canal because molars usually have three or four canals instead of one. Each canal must be found, cleaned, shaped and sealed, and molar canals are often narrow or curved.

Molars also sit at the back of the mouth, where access is harder, and they are more likely to have hidden extra canals. Complex molars are often referred to an endodontist, a root canal specialist whose fees can be higher than a general dentist's. In return, specialists use microscopes and advanced techniques that can improve success in difficult teeth.

How much is a root canal and crown without insurance?

A root canal and crown without insurance costs noticeably more than the root canal alone, because the crown is a separate procedure with its own fee. Delta Dental notes that its root canal figures do not include the final crown or filling.

Back teeth usually need a crown after a root canal to protect the tooth from cracking under chewing forces, as the Cleveland Clinic explains. Front teeth with enough remaining structure can often be restored with a filling instead. When you ask for a quote, request the root canal fee, any core build-up fee and the crown fee separately so you can see the full total.

What other fees can be added to a root canal bill?

A root canal bill can include several charges beyond the root canal itself. Knowing them in advance helps you compare quotes fairly.

  • Exam and X-rays: diagnosis usually includes an exam and one or more X-rays.
  • Emergency visit: if you are seen urgently for pain, there may be a separate fee.
  • Core build-up: a filling material that rebuilds the tooth before a crown.
  • Crown or filling: the final restoration.
  • Specialist fee: endodontists may charge more for complex cases.
  • Retreatment: redoing a failed root canal usually costs more than the first treatment.

Is a root canal cheaper at a general dentist or an endodontist?

A root canal is often cheaper at a general dentist than at an endodontist, because specialists generally charge higher fees. Many routine front-tooth and premolar root canals are done well by general dentists.

An endodontist can be the better value for complex teeth, such as molars with curved or hidden canals, teeth that have had a previous root canal, or cases where the diagnosis is unclear. Specialists use operating microscopes and focus only on this work, which can improve the chance of success the first time. A failed root canal that needs retreatment costs more than paying a specialist at the start.

If your general dentist refers you, ask why the case needs a specialist and request a written estimate from both. The difference in fee is often smaller than people expect once the crown is included.

How can you pay less for a root canal without insurance?

You can pay less for a root canal without insurance by comparing providers and using programs designed for self-pay patients. A few options are worth checking.

  • Ask for a cash-pay price: many offices offer a discount for paying in full.
  • In-house membership plans: yearly plans often include a percentage off treatment.
  • Dental schools: supervised students and residents often charge lower fees, though appointments take longer.
  • Community health centers: some offer sliding-scale fees based on income.
  • Payment plans and medical credit: spread the cost, but read interest terms carefully.
  • Discount dental plans: reduced fees at participating offices for an annual fee.

Delaying a needed root canal to save money can backfire. An infection that spreads can turn a savable tooth into an extraction plus a replacement tooth, which costs much more.

Is extraction cheaper than a root canal without insurance?

Extraction is usually cheaper than a root canal on the day of treatment, but it can cost more in the long run if you replace the tooth. A missing tooth often leads to an implant, bridge or partial denture, each with its own significant cost.

Leaving the gap empty is also an option for some back teeth, but neighbouring teeth can shift and the opposing tooth can over-erupt. Ask your dentist to compare the total cost of saving the tooth with the total cost of removing and replacing it.

What if you get dental insurance later?

If you plan to buy dental insurance before a root canal, check the waiting periods first. Delta Dental reports that insurance commonly covers 50%–80% of a root canal after the deductible, but many new plans make you wait months before paying for basic or major services.

A painful or infected tooth usually cannot wait that long. In that case, treat the tooth now and use a plan later for ongoing care. Learn more about what a root canal procedure involves, how long a root canal takes and root canal pain.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a root canal without insurance?

A root canal without insurance costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, $1,300 for a premolar and $1,500 for a molar, based on Delta Dental's out-of-network data. CareCredit reports averages of $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar. The crown is extra.

How much is a root canal and crown without insurance?

A root canal and crown without insurance costs the root canal fee plus a separate crown fee, and often a core build-up fee too. Delta Dental notes its root canal figures exclude the final crown or filling. Ask your dentist for each fee listed separately.

Why is a molar root canal more expensive?

A molar root canal is more expensive because molars usually have three or four canals, which are often narrow or curved and harder to reach. More canals mean more time and skill. Delta Dental puts a molar at about $1,500 out-of-network compared with about $1,200 for a front tooth.

Can I get a cheap root canal?

Cheaper root canals are often available at dental schools, community health centers with sliding-scale fees, and through in-house membership or discount dental plans. Many offices also give a discount for paying in full. Choose a provider for quality as well as price, because a failed root canal costs more to fix.

Is it cheaper to pull a tooth than get a root canal?

Pulling a tooth is usually cheaper than a root canal on the day, but replacing the missing tooth with an implant or bridge often costs more overall. Saving the natural tooth is generally preferred when it can be restored.
