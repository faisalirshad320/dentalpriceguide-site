How Much Does a Root Canal Cost With Insurance?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 28, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

With dental insurance, a root canal is commonly covered at 50%–80% after the deductible, according to Delta Dental, so you pay the remaining share. Full out-of-network fees run about $1,200 for a front tooth, $1,300 for a premolar and $1,500 for a molar, and the crown is billed separately.

Key takeaways
  • Insurance commonly covers 50%–80% of a root canal after the deductible (Delta Dental).
  • Your share depends on your plan's category for root canals, your deductible and annual maximum.
  • Full fees: about $1,200 (front), $1,300 (premolar), $1,500 (molar) out-of-network.
  • The crown is a separate charge and is often covered at a lower percentage.
  • A pre-treatment estimate shows your exact out-of-pocket cost.

How much does insurance cover for a root canal?

Dental insurance commonly covers 50%–80% of a root canal after the deductible, according to Delta Dental. The exact percentage depends on how your plan classifies root canals.

Many plans list root canals (endodontics) as a basic service, often paid at 80%. Others classify them as major services, often paid at 50%. Your plan's benefits summary will say which category applies. Some plans also have waiting periods before they pay for basic or major work, so newer members may not yet be covered.

After the plan pays its percentage, you pay the rest, called coinsurance. The plan stops paying once you reach your annual maximum for the year.

How do you calculate your root canal cost with insurance?

You can estimate your root canal cost with insurance in a few steps, using numbers from your plan and the dentist's fee.

  1. Find the allowed fee: in-network dentists charge a negotiated fee; out-of-network fees may be higher.
  2. Subtract any deductible you have not yet met this year.
  3. Apply your coverage percentage for root canals, such as 80% or 50%.
  4. Your share is the remaining percentage plus the deductible.
  5. Check your annual maximum: if the plan's payment would exceed what is left, you pay the difference.

Because full fees vary by tooth, a molar costs you more than a front tooth even at the same percentage. Ask the dental office to calculate this for you; they do it daily.

How much is a root canal and crown with insurance?

A root canal and crown with insurance usually costs noticeably more out of pocket than the root canal alone, because the crown is a separate procedure. Delta Dental notes that its root canal fees do not include the final crown or filling.

Crowns are commonly classified as major services, often covered at about 50%, which is a lower percentage than many plans pay for the root canal. A core build-up to rebuild the tooth before the crown may also be billed. Back teeth usually need a crown after a root canal, as the Cleveland Clinic explains, while front teeth may need only a filling.

Ask for the root canal, build-up and crown listed separately on your estimate so you can see the total.

Does an in-network dentist lower root canal cost?

An in-network dentist usually lowers your root canal cost with insurance because they accept the plan's negotiated fee. You pay your percentage of that lower fee and cannot be billed the difference above it.

An out-of-network dentist may charge more than the plan considers reasonable. Your plan pays its percentage of its own allowed amount, and you may owe the rest. If you are referred to an endodontist, check whether the specialist is in your network before booking.

What if a root canal goes over your annual maximum?

If a root canal and crown go over your annual maximum, you pay everything above the limit yourself. Annual maximums are often modest compared with the cost of several major procedures in one year.

When treatment can safely wait, some patients do the root canal in one plan year and the crown early in the next so two maximums apply. A tooth with a temporary filling is fragile, though, so ask your dentist how long it is safe to delay the crown before planning around benefit years.

Should you get a pre-treatment estimate?

A pre-treatment estimate is worth getting for any root canal that is not an emergency. The dental office sends the planned procedures to your insurer, which replies with what it expects to pay and what you will owe.

Estimates typically take a short time to come back and are not a guarantee, because your eligibility and remaining maximum can change. They are still the best way to avoid surprises. For painful emergencies, treatment usually goes ahead first, and the office can still give you an approximate share.

What if your plan does not cover root canals yet?

If your plan does not yet cover root canals because of a waiting period, you can pay out of pocket at the plan's discounted in-network fee in some cases, or ask about payment plans. Delaying treatment for an infected tooth is risky, because the infection can spread and the tooth may become impossible to save.

Our page on root canal cost covers self-pay prices and savings options. CareCredit reports averages of $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar, and Pearl lists a national range of $685 to $1,800 for root canal codes.

What other charges appear on a root canal bill with insurance?

Several charges besides the root canal itself commonly appear on the bill, and insurance may treat each one differently. Knowing them helps you read your explanation of benefits.

  • Exam and X-rays: often covered as diagnostic or preventive care at a high percentage.
  • Emergency or palliative visit: a fee for relieving pain before the full treatment.
  • Root canal: billed by tooth type, front, premolar or molar.
  • Core build-up: rebuilds missing tooth structure before a crown; often a major service.
  • Crown: usually a major service.
  • Specialist referral: an endodontist's fee if your dentist refers you.

Each line has its own code and coverage percentage. When an explanation of benefits arrives from your insurer, compare it with the dentist's estimate. If a procedure was denied or paid at a different rate than expected, the dental office can often resubmit with more information, such as X-rays or notes showing why treatment was needed.

Is a root canal worth it even with insurance costs?

A root canal is usually worth it when the tooth can be saved, even after insurance. The alternative, extraction followed by an implant or bridge, generally costs more in total and involves more treatment.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a root canal with insurance?

With insurance, you usually pay 20%–50% of the root canal fee after your deductible, because plans commonly cover 50%–80% according to Delta Dental. Full fees run about $1,200 for a front tooth to about $1,500 for a molar out-of-network, so your share depends on the tooth and plan.

Is a root canal a basic or major procedure for insurance?

Root canals are classified as basic services by many dental plans, often paid at 80%, and as major services by others, often paid at 50%. Check your plan's benefits summary under endodontics to see which applies.

Does insurance cover the crown after a root canal?

Many dental plans cover crowns after a root canal as a major service, often at about 50%, subject to the deductible and annual maximum. The crown is billed separately from the root canal, so ask for both on your estimate.

How can I lower my root canal cost with insurance?

You can lower your root canal cost with insurance by choosing an in-network dentist, getting a pre-treatment estimate, timing the crown to use a new year's annual maximum when safe, and using HSA or FSA funds for your share.

Why is my root canal bill higher than expected with insurance?

A root canal bill can be higher than expected if your deductible was not met, you reached your annual maximum, the dentist is out of network, the plan classifies root canals as major services, or separate charges such as X-rays, a build-up and the crown were added.
