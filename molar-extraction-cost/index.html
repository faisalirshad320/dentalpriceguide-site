How Much Does a Rotten or Molar Tooth Extraction Cost?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated November 2, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A rotten or molar tooth extraction costs about $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363. Badly decayed molars often need the surgical approach, and sedation, X-rays and bone grafts add to the bill.

Key takeaways
  • Simple extraction: $150 to $310 per tooth out-of-network (Delta Dental), $177 average (CareCredit).
  • Surgical extraction: $260 to $460 per tooth (Delta Dental), $363 average (CareCredit).
  • Rotten molars often break apart and need surgical removal.
  • Oral surgeons may charge more, especially with sedation.
  • Insurance often covers extractions at 80% as basic care.

How much does it cost to pull a rotten tooth?

Pulling a rotten tooth costs $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit's procedure cost list reports averages of $177 for a simple extraction and $363 for a surgical extraction.

Which category applies depends on the tooth's condition. A rotten tooth that is still solid and fully visible can often be removed simply. A tooth that has broken down to the gumline, crumbles easily, or has curved or fused roots usually needs a surgical extraction. Our main tooth extraction cost guide covers every type.

Why do molar extractions often cost more?

Molar extractions often cost more than front-tooth extractions because molars have two or three roots, sit further back in the mouth and are anchored in thicker bone. Removing them can take more time and force.

Badly decayed molars are especially likely to fracture during removal, and the dentist may need to section the tooth into pieces and remove each root separately, which is billed as a surgical extraction. Upper molars sit close to the sinus, and lower molars near the nerve, so complex cases are sometimes referred to an oral surgeon.

How much does an oral surgeon charge for an extraction?

An oral surgeon may charge more than a general dentist for an extraction, especially for complex, impacted or badly broken-down teeth. Surgeons also commonly offer IV sedation or general anesthesia, which are billed separately.

The specialist fee often reflects more difficult cases rather than a markup on simple ones. If your general dentist refers you, ask why and request an itemised estimate from the surgeon that lists the extraction code, imaging and any sedation, so you can see the full cost.

What other costs can come with a molar extraction?

Other costs can come with a molar extraction besides the extraction fee itself. Ask for each one on your estimate.

  • Exam and X-rays, sometimes a 3D scan for teeth near nerves or sinuses.
  • Sedation or general anesthesia if you choose to be sedated.
  • Bone graft to preserve the socket for a future implant; CareCredit averages an allograft at $811.
  • Prescriptions for pain relief or antibiotics.
  • Follow-up visits, often included.
  • Tooth replacement later, such as an implant or bridge.

When should a rotten tooth be pulled instead of saved?

A rotten tooth should usually be pulled when it cannot be restored. That includes decay that has destroyed most of the crown or reached below the gumline, a crack running into the root, severe bone loss from gum disease, or repeated infections despite treatment.

When enough tooth remains, a root canal and crown can often save it. Saving a molar keeps your bite stable, but it costs more upfront. Your dentist will compare the two, including the cost of replacing the tooth if it is removed. The Cleveland Clinic's tooth extraction guide explains when extraction is recommended.

What happens during a molar extraction?

During a molar extraction, the dentist numbs the area with local anesthetic and, if requested, provides sedation. For a simple extraction, the tooth is loosened with an instrument called an elevator and removed with forceps.

For a surgical extraction, the dentist may open the gum, remove a little bone and divide the tooth into sections so each root comes out separately. The socket is cleaned, a bone graft may be placed, and stitches may close the gum. You bite on gauze to control bleeding. The procedure usually takes from a few minutes to under an hour.

What is recovery like after a molar extraction?

Recovery after a molar extraction usually involves a few days of soreness and swelling, which peak around the second or third day. Most people return to normal activities within a few days.

Protect the blood clot by avoiding smoking, straws and vigorous rinsing for the first days, and eat soft foods. Dry socket, where the clot is lost, is more common after lower molar extractions; the Cleveland Clinic explains the signs. See what to eat after tooth extraction, smoking after tooth extraction and what a normal socket looks like after tooth extraction.

Contact your dentist for bleeding that will not stop with firm pressure, pain that worsens after day three, fever or pus. Seek emergency care for facial swelling that spreads or trouble swallowing or breathing.

Does insurance cover a rotten tooth extraction?

Insurance usually covers a rotten tooth extraction as a basic procedure. Delta Dental notes that basic procedures such as extractions are often covered at 80% on a 100-80-50 plan, after the deductible, in its coverage guide.

Some plans classify surgical extractions or sedation differently, and bone grafts may fall under major or oral surgery benefits. Check whether your plan has waiting periods. A pre-treatment estimate is useful for surgical cases.

Should you replace a pulled molar?

Replacing a pulled molar is often recommended, because the neighbouring teeth can drift into the gap and the opposing tooth can over-erupt, changing your bite. Chewing may also become less efficient on that side.

Options include a dental implant, a bridge or a partial denture. If you plan an implant, a socket bone graft at the time of extraction can help preserve bone; the Cleveland Clinic explains grafts. Leaving a back-tooth gap may be acceptable in some cases; ask your dentist.

What happens if you leave a rotten tooth in your mouth?

Leaving a rotten tooth in your mouth risks infection spreading to the surrounding bone and tissues, forming an abscess. Pain may come and go, but decay and infection usually continue to progress.

An untreated infected tooth can cause swelling and, rarely, serious spreading infections. It can also affect neighbouring teeth and make later treatment harder and more expensive. Even if the tooth does not hurt, have it checked and treated promptly.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to pull a rotten tooth?

Pulling a rotten tooth costs $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction per tooth out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $177 and $363. Sedation, X-rays and grafts add to the cost.

How much does a molar extraction cost?

A molar extraction usually falls in the same ranges as other extractions, $150 to $310 simple or $260 to $460 surgical out-of-network per Delta Dental, but molars are more likely to need a surgical extraction because of their multiple roots.

How much does an oral surgeon charge to pull a tooth?

Oral surgeons often charge more than general dentists, especially for complex or impacted teeth, and sedation is billed separately. Ask for an itemised estimate showing the extraction code, imaging and anesthesia fees.

Does insurance cover tooth extraction?

Many dental plans cover extractions as basic procedures, often at 80% on a 100-80-50 plan after the deductible, according to Delta Dental. Surgical extractions, sedation and bone grafts may be covered differently.

Is it better to pull a rotten tooth or save it?

Saving a rotten tooth with a root canal and crown is usually preferred when enough healthy structure remains, because it keeps your bite stable. Pulling is better when the tooth cannot be restored. Your dentist can compare the total costs of each option.
