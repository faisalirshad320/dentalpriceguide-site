Root Canal Cost in 2026: What You'll Pay by Tooth, With and Without Insurance

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Root canal cost in the US usually falls between $685 and $1,800 per tooth, with a national average near $1,050, according to Pearl dental cost data based on ADA fee surveys. Front teeth cost least and molars cost most. The crown or filling that follows is billed separately, and local prices vary.

Key takeaways
  • A root canal typically costs $685 to $1,800 per tooth nationally, averaging about $1,050 (Pearl, ADA fee survey based).
  • Molars cost the most: about $1,500 out-of-network per Delta Dental, versus about $1,200 for a front tooth.
  • Dental insurance commonly covers 50%–80% of a root canal after the deductible.
  • The crown or filling that seals the tooth afterward is a separate charge.
  • Waiting rarely saves money: an infected tooth tends to need more treatment, not less.

How much is a root canal?

A root canal costs roughly $685 to $1,800 per tooth in the US, with an average of about $1,050, based on Pearl dental cost data drawn from ADA fee surveys for CDT codes D3310–D3330. Those figures are national averages, and the price in your town can sit above or below them.

Root canal pricing climbs with the number of canals inside the tooth. A front tooth usually has one canal, a premolar one or two, and a molar three or more. More canals mean more chair time, more instruments and irrigation, and more filling material, so molars land at the top of the range.

Root canal fees quoted by most offices cover the endodontic treatment only. Delta Dental notes that its figures do not include the final crown or filling, which is billed as a separate procedure. When you compare quotes, ask whether the number covers the root canal alone or the root canal plus the restoration.

Budget rule: price the root canal and the crown together. For a back tooth, the crown can add as much again or more to the bill, so a low root canal quote is only half the picture.

How much does a root canal cost on a front tooth, premolar or molar?

Root canal cost by tooth, using Delta Dental's 2025 out-of-network data, is about $1,200 for a front tooth, about $1,300 for a premolar and about $1,500 for a molar. Those are amounts charged when a patient sees a dentist outside the insurer's network, before any plan discount.

  • Front tooth (incisor or canine): about $1,200 out-of-network (Delta Dental); $984 average in the CareCredit cost study.
  • Premolar: about $1,300 out-of-network (Delta Dental).
  • Molar: about $1,500 out-of-network (Delta Dental); $1,337 average in the CareCredit cost study.

Molar root canals take longer because the canals are narrower, more curved and harder to reach at the back of the mouth. Some molars also hide an extra canal that has to be found and treated, and a missed canal is a common reason treatment fails later. That extra complexity is what the higher fee pays for.

Root canal treatment on a tooth that has already had one, called retreatment, generally costs more than the first procedure. The old filling material has to be removed before the canals can be cleaned again, and many general dentists refer these cases to an endodontist.

Why do root canal price estimates differ between sources?

Root canal price estimates differ because each source measures something different. Delta Dental reports what out-of-network dentists charge, CareCredit reports results from the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study, and Pearl builds a national range from ADA fee survey data.

Out-of-network fees tend to run higher because they reflect a dentist's full list price, before any insurer discount. That helps explain why Delta Dental's molar figure of about $1,500 sits above CareCredit's molar average of $1,337. Survey averages blend many practices and regions, which pulls the number toward the middle.

Root canal figures from all three sources point the same way: front teeth cost least and molars cost most. A quote far outside Pearl's $685 to $1,800 range deserves a question about what it includes, such as a crown, a buildup, sedation or a specialist fee.

Use these numbers as a reality check, not a promise. Your actual fee depends on your dentist, your city and the condition of the tooth.

What changes the price of a root canal?

The price of a root canal depends mainly on which tooth is treated, who treats it, where you live and how complicated the canals are. Several smaller items can also appear on the bill.

  • Tooth type: molars cost more than premolars, and premolars more than front teeth.
  • General dentist vs endodontist: endodontists are specialists who focus on root canals. Their fees are usually higher, but they handle curved, calcified or previously treated canals every day.
  • Location: fees in large metro areas typically run above those in smaller towns.
  • Complexity: calcified canals, unusual anatomy, a cracked tooth or a previous root canal all add chair time.
  • Imaging and extras: X-rays, a 3D cone-beam scan, an emergency visit to relieve pain, or sedation may be billed separately.
  • Core buildup: a tooth missing much of its structure may need a buildup before a crown, which is another line item.
  • The restoration: a crown or filling after treatment is a separate fee.

Root canal quotes should list each code and fee. Ask for a written treatment plan with CDT codes so you can compare offices line by line and send the plan to your insurer for a pre-treatment estimate.

Do you need a crown after a root canal, and does it add to the cost?

A crown after a root canal is usually recommended for molars and premolars, and it is billed separately from the root canal itself. Back teeth take heavy chewing forces, and a tooth that has lost its pulp and much of its structure is more likely to crack without full coverage.

Front teeth with small access openings can sometimes be sealed with a filling instead. Your dentist decides based on how much healthy tooth remains. Cleveland Clinic explains that crowns cap and protect weakened teeth, which is the main reason they follow so many root canals.

Root canal and crown costs together form the real budget for a back tooth. Our guide to root canal and crown cost breaks down the combined price, crown materials and how insurance treats each part.

Delaying the crown is a common way people try to spread the cost. Ask your dentist how long a temporary filling can safely stay in place, because a tooth that fractures while waiting may no longer be savable.

Does dental insurance cover root canals?

Dental insurance usually covers root canals, commonly paying 50%–80% of the cost after you meet your deductible, according to Delta Dental. Plans classify endodontic treatment as either a basic or a major service, and that category decides which percentage applies.

Root canal coverage has limits that catch people off guard:

  • Annual maximum: most plans cap what they pay per year. A root canal plus crown in the same year can use up much of it.
  • Waiting periods: new plans may not cover basic or major work for several months.
  • Network: in-network dentists accept a negotiated fee, so your share is calculated on a lower base.
  • Crown classification: the crown is often a major service with a lower coverage percentage than the root canal.

To see how these rules play out, read our guide to root canal cost with insurance. If you have no coverage at all, our page on how much a root canal costs without insurance covers self-pay discounts and cash pricing.

Before treatment, ask the office to submit a pre-treatment estimate, also called a predetermination. The insurer replies with what it expects to pay, which takes most of the guesswork out of your share.

How can you pay less for a root canal?

The most reliable ways to pay less for a root canal are using an in-network dentist, asking about cash discounts, getting treatment at a dental school, and spreading the cost with a payment plan or tax-advantaged account.

  1. Stay in network. In-network fees are pre-negotiated and generally sit below the out-of-network figures Delta Dental reports.
  2. Ask for a self-pay price. Many practices offer a discount for paying in full at the visit, and some sell in-house membership plans.
  3. Try a dental school. Accredited dental schools offer root canals performed by students or residents under specialist supervision, usually at reduced fees. Appointments take longer.
  4. Use HSA or FSA money. Root canals are a qualifying medical expense, so pre-tax dollars stretch further.
  5. Spread the payments. Offices often offer payment plans, and medical credit cards are another option. Read promotional terms carefully, because deferred interest can be charged back if the balance is not cleared in time.
  6. Time the work. If your annual maximum is nearly used, ask whether the crown can safely be scheduled after your benefits reset.
  7. Check community clinics. Federally qualified health centers often charge on a sliding scale based on income.

Avoid trying to save money by waiting. An infected tooth does not heal by itself, and delay can turn a root canal into an extraction plus a replacement tooth, which costs more in the end.

Why do I need root canal treatment?

Root canal treatment is needed when the pulp inside a tooth, the soft tissue holding nerves and blood vessels, becomes infected or inflamed and cannot recover. Deep decay, a crack, repeated dental work on the same tooth, or an injury can all let bacteria reach the pulp.

Common warning signs include lingering sensitivity to hot or cold, pain when biting, a darkened tooth, swollen or tender gums near one tooth, or a small pimple-like bump on the gum. Some infected teeth cause no pain at all and only show up on an X-ray. Our article on root canal symptoms and how to avoid a root canal covers the signs and prevention in more detail.

Delta Dental describes the goal of treatment as removing the damaged pulp while saving the natural tooth. For a fuller walkthrough, see what a root canal procedure involves. Only a dentist can confirm whether a tooth needs one, usually with an exam, X-rays and tests of how the tooth responds to cold or tapping.

See a dentist the same day, or go to urgent care or an emergency room, if you have facial swelling, fever, swelling that spreads toward the eye or neck, trouble swallowing or breathing, or severe pain that pain relievers do not control. These can be signs of a spreading infection.

What happens during a root canal?

A root canal involves numbing the tooth, opening the crown, cleaning and shaping the canals, and sealing them with a filling material called gutta-percha. Most treatments take one or two visits.

  1. The dentist takes X-rays and gives local anesthetic.
  2. A thin rubber sheet called a dental dam isolates the tooth to keep it dry and clean.
  3. A small opening is made in the top of the tooth to reach the pulp chamber.
  4. The infected pulp is removed and the canals are cleaned, shaped and disinfected.
  5. The canals are filled and the opening is sealed with a temporary or permanent filling.
  6. A crown is placed at a later visit when needed.

Root canal appointments are shorter for a front tooth and longer for a molar. Our guide on how long a root canal takes gives typical times by tooth. If fear of pain is holding you back, read about root canal pain; most people compare the procedure to getting a filling, with some soreness for a few days afterward.

Recovery is usually quick. Follow your dentist's instructions on pain relief and chewing, and avoid biting hard on the tooth until it is fully restored. You can see what to expect at each stage in our root canal before and after guide.

Is a root canal or an extraction cheaper in the long run?

A root canal usually costs more on the day than pulling the tooth, but an extraction is often more expensive over time once you replace the missing tooth. A gap left unfilled can let nearby teeth drift and change your bite, and replacing a tooth with an implant or bridge typically costs considerably more than a root canal.

Extraction can still be the right choice when a tooth is cracked below the gum line, has too little structure left to restore, or has a poor long-term outlook. Our comparison of root canal vs extraction covers costs, recovery and when each option makes sense.

Root canals succeed in most cases, but some do fail, often because of a missed canal, a leaking restoration or a new crack. Pain, swelling or a gum bump returning months or years later is a reason to go back. Read about root canal treatment side effects and failed root canals to know the warning signs and what retreatment involves.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a root canal cost without insurance?

A root canal without insurance typically costs $685 to $1,800 per tooth nationally, averaging about $1,050, according to Pearl's ADA fee survey based data. Delta Dental reports out-of-network fees of about $1,200 for a front tooth and about $1,500 for a molar. The crown or filling is extra, so ask for a combined quote and any self-pay discount.

Does dental insurance cover root canals?

Yes, most dental plans cover root canals, commonly paying 50%–80% of the cost after the deductible, according to Delta Dental. Your share depends on whether the plan treats endodontics as basic or major, your annual maximum, any waiting period, and whether your dentist is in network. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate before you book.

Why is a molar root canal more expensive?

A molar root canal costs more because molars have three or more canals that are often narrow and curved, and they sit at the back of the mouth where access is harder. Treatment takes longer and uses more materials. Delta Dental puts an out-of-network molar at about $1,500, compared with about $1,200 for a front tooth.

Is the crown included in the root canal price?

No, the crown is usually not included in the root canal price. Delta Dental notes its root canal fees do not include the final crown or filling. Molars and premolars usually need a crown afterward, so budget for both procedures and check how your insurance classifies each one, since crowns often get lower coverage.

Is it cheaper to pull a tooth than get a root canal?

Pulling a tooth is usually cheaper on the day, but often costs more over time. A missing tooth is commonly replaced with an implant or bridge, which costs more than a root canal, and leaving a gap can shift neighboring teeth. Extraction may still be best when a tooth cannot be restored, so discuss both options with your dentist.

Can I get a cheaper root canal at a dental school?

Yes, many dental schools offer root canals at reduced fees, performed by students or residents under supervision from licensed dentists and endodontists. Expect longer and more frequent appointments, and a screening visit to see whether your case suits the teaching program. Complex molars or retreatments may be assigned to graduate residents.

How long can I wait to get a root canal?

You should not wait long once a dentist recommends a root canal. An infected tooth does not heal on its own, and delay can lead to an abscess or a tooth that can no longer be saved. Get same-day care for facial swelling, fever, spreading swelling or severe pain that medicine does not control.
