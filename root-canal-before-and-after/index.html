Root Canal Before and After: What Your Tooth Looks and Feels Like

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 26, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Before a root canal, the tooth is often painful, sensitive or swollen because the pulp inside is inflamed or infected. After the root canal, that pain usually settles within a few days, and once a crown or filling is placed the tooth looks and works like a normal tooth. On X-rays, the canals appear filled with bright sealing material (Delta Dental).

Key takeaways
  • Before treatment, the tooth may ache, throb, react to hot and cold or have swelling.
  • Right after treatment, mild tenderness for a few days is normal.
  • A crown or filling restores the tooth's look and protects it from cracking.
  • On X-rays, a dark area at the root tip from infection often shrinks as bone heals.
  • Delta Dental's out-of-network costs run from about $1,200 (front) to about $1,500 (molar), crown extra.

What does a tooth look like before a root canal?

Before a root canal, a tooth may look normal from the outside even though the pulp inside is inflamed or infected. In other cases there are visible clues: a large cavity, a broken or cracked tooth, an old filling that has leaked, or a tooth that has turned grey or darker than its neighbours after an injury.

The gum near the tooth may be swollen or have a small pimple-like bump that leaks fluid, a sign that infection has formed an abscess. On an X-ray, the dentist may see decay reaching toward the pulp or a dark area at the tip of the root where infection has dissolved some bone.

Why do root canal before and after photos look so similar?

Root canal before and after photos often look similar because the work happens inside the tooth, not on its visible surface. The main visible change usually comes from the final restoration rather than the root canal itself.

In photos, the "before" picture may show a broken tooth, a large dark cavity, a grey discoloured front tooth or a swollen gum. The "after" picture shows the same tooth restored with a crown or a tooth-coloured filling, with healthy pink gum around it. For a discoloured front tooth, the difference can be dramatic once internal whitening or a crown is completed.

Clinical before and after images that dentists rely on are X-rays. Comparing an X-ray taken at diagnosis with one taken six to twelve months later shows whether the bone around the root has healed. That comparison is the true measure of a successful root canal, even when the tooth looks the same in a mirror.

How does a tooth feel before a root canal?

A tooth that needs a root canal often feels painful in ways that are hard to ignore. Typical sensations include sharp pain to hot or cold that lingers after the trigger is gone, throbbing that starts on its own, and pain when biting or tapping on the tooth.

Some teeth feel tall or loose when you bite because inflammation at the root tip pushes the tooth slightly upward. Others stop hurting completely because the nerve has died, even though infection continues. That variety is why a dentist relies on tests and X-rays rather than pain alone.

What happens to the tooth during the procedure?

During a root canal, the dentist numbs the area, isolates the tooth with a rubber dam, and makes a small opening through the top of the tooth. The inflamed or infected pulp is removed, the canals are cleaned and shaped with fine instruments, and disinfecting solutions flush out bacteria.

The empty canals are then filled with a rubber-like material called gutta-percha and a sealer, and the opening is closed with a temporary or permanent filling. The full steps are explained in what a root canal procedure is, and timing in how long a root canal takes.

What does a tooth feel like right after a root canal?

Right after a root canal, the tooth and gum may feel tender or bruised for a few days. Biting may feel uncomfortable at first, especially if there was infection at the root tip before treatment. This is usually much milder than the pain that led to treatment.

Over-the-counter pain relievers, taken as directed on the label, usually manage the soreness. Chew on the other side until the permanent restoration is in place, because a tooth with only a temporary filling is weaker and can crack. Our page on root canal pain explains what is normal.

Call your dentist if pain gets worse after a few days, swelling appears or increases, the temporary filling falls out, or your bite feels high. Seek urgent care for facial swelling with fever or trouble swallowing.

What does a tooth look like after a root canal and crown?

After a root canal and crown, the tooth looks like a natural tooth. A modern crown is shaded to match the neighbouring teeth, so most people cannot tell which tooth was treated.

Back teeth such as molars and premolars usually need a crown after a root canal because they bear heavy chewing forces and can become brittle. Front teeth with enough remaining structure may need only a tooth-coloured filling. The Cleveland Clinic's dental crowns guide covers crown types and materials. A tooth that darkened before treatment may need a crown, veneer or internal whitening to restore its colour.

What does a root canal look like on an X-ray before and after?

On an X-ray before a root canal, the pulp chamber and canals appear as dark lines running down the centre of the roots, and infection may show as a dark halo at the root tip. Deep decay appears as a dark area eating into the tooth.

After a root canal, the canals appear as bright white lines because the filling material shows up clearly on X-rays. Your dentist may take follow-up X-rays months later to check that the dark area at the root tip is shrinking as bone heals, which is a sign of success.

How long does a tooth last after a root canal?

A tooth can last many years, often for life, after a root canal that is well done and properly restored. The most important factors are a good seal inside the canals, a well-fitting crown or filling that keeps bacteria out, and good daily oral hygiene.

Root canals can fail if new decay develops, the crown leaks or breaks, the tooth cracks, or a hidden canal was missed. A failed root canal can often be retreated, sometimes by an endodontist. Regular check-ups catch these problems early.

How much does a root canal cost, before and after the crown?

A root canal costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, about $1,300 for a premolar and about $1,500 for a molar out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit reports averages of $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar, and Pearl gives a national range of $685 to $1,800.

Delta Dental notes these fees do not include the final crown or filling. Insurance commonly covers 50%–80% after the deductible. See our full root canal cost guide.

Frequently asked questions

What does a tooth look like after a root canal?

After a root canal and final restoration, a tooth looks like a normal tooth. A crown or tooth-coloured filling matches the neighbouring teeth. On X-rays, the treated canals show up as bright white lines from the filling material.

Is it normal to have pain after a root canal?

Mild tenderness for a few days after a root canal is normal, especially when biting. It usually responds to over-the-counter pain relievers. Pain that gets worse after several days, swelling or a high bite should be checked by your dentist.

Do you need a crown after a root canal?

Back teeth usually need a crown after a root canal because they take heavy chewing forces and can crack. Front teeth with enough remaining structure can often be restored with a filling instead. Your dentist decides based on how much healthy tooth remains.

Can a root canal tooth turn dark?

A root canal treated tooth can darken, especially if it was injured or the nerve died before treatment. Front teeth are most noticeable. Internal whitening, a veneer or a crown can restore the colour. Ask your dentist which option suits the tooth.

How long does a tooth last after a root canal?

A tooth often lasts many years, sometimes for life, after a well-done root canal with a good crown or filling. New decay, a leaking crown or a crack can cause failure, so regular check-ups and good oral hygiene are important.
