Maryland Bridge Dental: What It Is, What It Costs and Who It Suits

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated November 2, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

A Maryland bridge is a false tooth held in place by small wings bonded to the backs of the neighbouring teeth, so those teeth need little or no drilling. It costs about $1,569 on average for a single tooth, compared with $5,197 for a traditional single-tooth bridge, according to CareCredit.

Key takeaways
  • A Maryland bridge uses bonded metal or ceramic wings instead of crowns.
  • CareCredit averages: $1,569 for a Maryland bridge vs $5,197 for a traditional single-tooth bridge.
  • Little or no enamel removal from neighbouring teeth.
  • Best for front teeth with healthy neighbours and a gentle bite.
  • Insurance typically covers 50% to 80% of bridge costs (Delta Dental).

What is a Maryland bridge?

A Maryland bridge, also called a resin-bonded bridge, is a fixed dental bridge that replaces a missing tooth using small wings attached to the false tooth. The wings, made of metal or ceramic, are bonded with resin cement to the back surfaces of the teeth on either side of the gap.

Because the wings sit on the tongue side of the neighbouring teeth, those teeth usually need only minimal preparation, if any. That makes a Maryland bridge more conservative than a traditional bridge, which requires shaping the neighbouring teeth for full crowns. Our guide to dental bridges explains all bridge types.

How much does a Maryland bridge cost?

A Maryland bridge costs about $1,569 on average for a single missing tooth, according to CareCredit's procedure cost list. That is considerably less than the $5,197 average CareCredit reports for a traditional single-tooth bridge.

Delta Dental reports an out-of-network average of about $3,600 for a three-unit bridge, made of two crowns and a false tooth, in its bridge cost guide. The difference reflects how much less lab work and tooth preparation a Maryland bridge needs. Material choice, location and the dentist's fees affect your quote. What are the advantages of a Maryland bridge?

The main advantages of a Maryland bridge are lower cost and preservation of healthy teeth. Because the neighbouring teeth are not crowned, more natural tooth structure is kept, and the procedure is often reversible in a way traditional bridges are not.

  • Lower cost than traditional bridges and implants.
  • Minimal drilling of neighbouring teeth.
  • No surgery, unlike an implant.
  • Quick treatment, usually two visits.
  • Good appearance for front teeth, especially with ceramic wings.

What are the disadvantages of a Maryland bridge?

The main disadvantage of a Maryland bridge is that it relies on a resin bond, which can fail. Maryland bridges can come loose, or "debond", especially under heavy biting forces, and may need to be re-cemented.

  • Less strength: not ideal for back teeth that take heavy chewing forces.
  • Debonding risk: the bridge may detach and need re-cementing.
  • Metal show-through: metal wings can make thin front teeth look slightly grey.
  • Bite limitations: a deep bite or grinding can stress the wings.
  • Bone loss under the gap continues, as with any bridge.

Who is a good candidate for a Maryland bridge?

A good candidate for a Maryland bridge has a single missing front tooth, healthy neighbouring teeth with enough enamel for bonding, and a bite that does not put heavy force on the area. Maryland bridges are also popular for teenagers and young adults who are not yet ready for an implant because their jaws are still growing.

People who grind their teeth heavily, have a deep overbite, or need to replace a back tooth may be better served by a traditional bridge or implant. Your dentist will check your bite and the neighbouring teeth before recommending it.

What happens when you get a Maryland bridge?

Getting a Maryland bridge usually takes two visits. At the first visit, the dentist lightly prepares the back surfaces of the neighbouring teeth if needed and takes a scan or impression. A lab then makes the bridge to match your teeth.

At the second visit, the dentist checks the fit and colour, cleans and etches the neighbouring teeth, and bonds the wings in place with resin cement. The bite is adjusted so the bridge does not take too much force. Little or no anesthetic is usually needed because there is minimal drilling.

How long does a Maryland bridge last?

A Maryland bridge can last many years, but it is more likely than a traditional bridge to come loose at some point. When it debonds, it can often be cleaned and re-cemented if the bridge and teeth are undamaged.

Lifespan depends on bite forces, habits like biting nails or opening packages with teeth, and how well the bonded surfaces are cared for. Avoid biting hard foods directly with the bridge, wear a night guard if you grind, and clean under the false tooth daily.

Is a Maryland bridge better than an implant?

A Maryland bridge is not better than an implant overall, but it can be a better fit for some people. It costs less, avoids surgery and preserves neighbouring teeth, while an implant generally lasts longer, preserves bone and does not depend on bonding to other teeth.

Maryland bridges are often used as a long-term temporary option for young people until they are old enough for an implant. Healthline's implant versus bridge guide and the Cleveland Clinic's prosthodontics guide discuss these options.

Does insurance cover a Maryland bridge?

Insurance often covers a Maryland bridge as a major restorative service. Delta Dental notes that insurance typically covers 50% to 80% of bridge costs, depending on the plan, subject to your deductible and annual maximum.

Some plans have a missing tooth clause or waiting periods for major work. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate. See dental bridge cost without insurance for self-pay options and before and after dental bridge examples.

How do you look after a Maryland bridge?

Looking after a Maryland bridge focuses on keeping the bond strong and the area clean. Brush twice daily and clean under the false tooth every day with a floss threader, super floss or a water flosser, because plaque trapped under the bridge can inflame the gum and lead to decay on the supporting teeth.

Avoid biting directly into hard foods such as apples, crusty bread or nuts with the bridged tooth; cut food into pieces and chew with your back teeth instead. Do not use your teeth to open packages or bite fingernails. If the bridge ever feels loose or moves, stop chewing on it and call your dentist promptly. A loose Maryland bridge can often be re-bonded, but waiting can allow decay to start under the wing or the bridge to come off and be lost.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a Maryland bridge cost?

A Maryland bridge costs about $1,569 on average for a single missing tooth, according to CareCredit. That is much less than a traditional single-tooth bridge, which CareCredit averages at $5,197. Your price depends on materials, location and dentist.

How long does a Maryland bridge last?

A Maryland bridge can last many years but is more prone to coming loose than a traditional bridge. If it debonds, it can often be cleaned and re-cemented. Avoiding hard foods on the bridge and wearing a night guard help it last.

Is a Maryland bridge permanent?

A Maryland bridge is a fixed bridge that you cannot remove yourself, but it is less permanent than a traditional bridge because it relies on bonding. It can come loose and may need re-cementing, and it can usually be removed by a dentist without major damage to the neighbouring teeth.

Can a Maryland bridge be used for back teeth?

Maryland bridges are mainly used for front teeth. Back teeth take heavier chewing forces, which can cause the bonded wings to fail. A traditional bridge or implant is usually recommended for missing molars.

Does insurance cover Maryland bridges?

Many dental plans cover Maryland bridges as major services. Delta Dental notes insurance typically covers 50% to 80% of bridge costs depending on the plan, subject to the deductible and annual maximum. Request a pre-treatment estimate.
