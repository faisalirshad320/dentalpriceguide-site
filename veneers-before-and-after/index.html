Veneers Before and After: What Changes, What It Costs and How to Spot Bad Veneers

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Veneers before and after results show thin porcelain or composite shells covering stained, chipped, gapped or uneven front teeth for a brighter, more even smile. Porcelain veneers usually need a thin layer of enamel removed, so the change is permanent. A porcelain veneer costs $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, according to Delta Dental.

Key takeaways
  • Veneers change the color, shape and apparent alignment of front teeth, but not the bite or the health of the tooth underneath.
  • Porcelain veneers usually require removing a thin layer of enamel, so the decision cannot be reversed.
  • Bad veneers often look too white, too bulky or too uniform, or leave inflamed gums.
  • Porcelain veneers cost $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth (Delta Dental) and are usually not covered by insurance.

What do veneers look like before and after?

Veneers before and after typically show the same teeth with a new front surface: stains hidden, chips and worn edges rebuilt, small gaps closed and uneven lengths evened out. A veneer is a thin shell bonded to the front of a tooth, so it changes shape and color, not the tooth's root or your bite.

Veneers can improve:

  • Deep stains that whitening cannot lift, such as tetracycline or fluorosis staining.
  • Chipped, worn or slightly broken edges.
  • Small gaps between front teeth.
  • Teeth that are short, pointed or uneven in size.
  • Mildly crooked teeth, when orthodontic treatment is not wanted.

Veneers cannot fix gum disease, active decay, a deep bite or severe crowding. Those problems need treatment first, or a different plan altogether. Cleveland Clinic describes veneers alongside bonding, whitening and crowns, which helps put them in context. If you are starting from scratch, read what veneers are and the materials used.

Before and after photos online are chosen to show the best results. Ask your dentist for photos of their own cases, ideally similar to yours, rather than relying on marketing images.

What happens to your teeth before veneers are placed?

Teeth before veneers are usually reshaped by removing a thin layer of enamel from the front surface, so the veneer sits flush and does not look bulky. Because enamel does not grow back, those teeth will always need a veneer or other covering afterward.

  1. Consultation and planning: exam, X-rays, photos and a discussion of shape and shade. Many dentists create a wax-up or digital preview, and some place a temporary mock-up so you can see the result in your own mouth.
  2. Treating problems first: cavities, gum inflammation and grinding habits are addressed. Whitening of the other teeth is usually done before the veneer shade is chosen.
  3. Preparation: under local anesthetic, a small amount of enamel is removed. Minimal-prep or no-prep veneers remove little or none, but they are not right for every tooth.
  4. Impressions and temporaries: a mold or scan goes to the lab, and temporary veneers protect the teeth.
  5. Bonding: the dentist tries in the veneers, checks color and fit, then bonds them with resin cement and adjusts the bite.

Expect some sensitivity to hot and cold for a few days after preparation and bonding. Follow your dentist's instructions, and seek prompt care for facial swelling, fever or severe pain.

What do bad veneers look like?

Bad veneers usually look too white, too thick, too uniform or poorly fitted at the gum line, and they can cause problems beyond looks. A veneer that is technically sound can still look unnatural if its shade and shape were not matched to the face.

  • Too bright or opaque: a flat, chalky white that does not match the lower teeth.
  • Bulky or long: teeth that look oversized or push the lips forward.
  • Identical "piano key" teeth: natural teeth vary slightly, and perfect sameness looks artificial.
  • Dark lines or gaps at the gum line: open margins can trap plaque and stain.
  • Red, puffy or bleeding gums: often a sign of overhanging edges or a poor fit.
  • Chips or veneers coming off: may point to bite problems or grinding.

Bad veneers before and after replacement cases show that most can be redone, but replacement means paying again and possibly removing more tooth structure. Choose a dentist who shows you a preview, explains how much enamel will be removed and agrees the shade with you before final bonding. If a tooth is heavily filled or broken, a crown may be the better fit; see our comparison of veneers vs crowns.

How long do veneer results last?

Veneer results last many years with good care, but veneers are not lifelong and will eventually need replacing. Porcelain resists staining and wear better than composite, which tends to chip and discolor sooner.

Composite veneers are built up directly on the tooth in one visit and usually need little or no enamel removal. Their before-and-after change can look excellent at first, but composite needs polishing and touch-ups more often and is more likely to pick up stains from coffee, tea and red wine. Porcelain veneers cost more up front but hold their color and gloss far better.

Veneers last longest when you brush and floss daily, avoid biting hard objects, wear a night guard if you grind, and keep regular checkups. Because enamel is removed, the commitment is permanent even though each set of veneers is not. Read are veneers permanent for what affects lifespan and the signs that replacement is due.

What does it cost to get a veneers before-and-after result?

Getting veneers costs $1,180 to $2,185 per porcelain laminate veneer without insurance, according to Delta Dental's 2024 data. The CareCredit cost study puts a single porcelain veneer at $1,765 on average and a full set of six to eight porcelain veneers at $15,486 on average.

The two sources measure different things: Delta Dental reports a per-tooth price range, while CareCredit reports averages from a procedure cost survey. Both are national figures, and local prices vary with the dentist's experience, the lab and the material.

Veneers are usually treated as cosmetic and are often not covered by insurance, Delta Dental notes. Coverage may apply only when a tooth is being restored after damage, so ask your insurer before treatment. Our veneers cost guide covers financing and ways to lower the bill.

What are the alternatives to veneers?

The main alternatives to veneers are teeth whitening, dental bonding, orthodontic treatment and crowns, and some can give a similar before-and-after change for less money or with less tooth removed.

  • Bonding: tooth-colored resin sculpted onto the tooth in one visit, at $431 per tooth on average in the CareCredit study. Little or no enamel is removed, but bonding stains and chips more easily than porcelain.
  • Whitening: lightens natural teeth without changing their shape. Works best for surface stains.
  • Clear aligners or braces: fix crowding and gaps by moving teeth rather than covering them.
  • Crowns: cover the whole tooth and suit heavily filled or root-canal-treated teeth.

Healthline explains the main veneer types and who they suit. Your dentist's assessment of your bite, gums and enamel should guide the final choice, and their instructions take priority over general advice.

Frequently asked questions

What do your teeth look like under veneers?

Teeth under veneers look slightly smaller and thinner than before, because a thin layer of enamel is usually removed from the front surface. While you wait for the final veneers, temporary veneers cover the prepared teeth. Minimal-prep veneers remove less enamel, but your dentist will decide what suits your teeth and bite.

Can bad veneers be fixed?

Yes, bad veneers can usually be replaced or adjusted. A dentist can remove the old veneers, check the teeth underneath and make new ones with a better shade, shape and fit. Replacement means paying for new veneers, and a little more tooth structure may be lost, so choose carefully and ask to see a preview first.

Are veneers permanent?

Veneers are a permanent commitment but not a permanent restoration. Removing enamel means the teeth will always need some covering. The veneers themselves wear, chip or come loose over time and eventually need replacing. Good hygiene, a night guard for grinding and avoiding biting hard objects help them last longer.

How much do veneers cost per tooth?

Porcelain veneers cost $1,180 to $2,185 per tooth without insurance, according to Delta Dental's 2024 data. CareCredit's cost study reports an average of $1,765 for a single porcelain veneer and $15,486 for a full set of six to eight. Insurance usually treats veneers as cosmetic and often does not cover them.

Do veneers ruin your teeth?

Veneers do not ruin healthy teeth when planned and placed well, but they do require removing some enamel, which cannot be reversed. Poorly fitted veneers can trap plaque and inflame the gums. Ask how much enamel will be removed, whether minimal-prep veneers suit you, and whether bonding or whitening could give a similar result.

How long does it take to get veneers?

Porcelain veneers usually take two or three visits over a few weeks: a consultation, a preparation appointment with impressions and temporaries, and a bonding visit once the lab finishes. Composite veneers can often be done in a single visit. Your timeline may be longer if gum or decay problems need treatment first.
