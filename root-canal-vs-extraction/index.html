Root Canal vs Extraction: Which Is Better for an Infected Tooth?

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS, M.Phil Chemical PathologyUpdated November 4, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

A root canal saves the natural tooth by cleaning out infected pulp, while an extraction removes the tooth entirely. Extraction is cheaper on the day, but replacing the tooth later with an implant or bridge usually costs more overall. A root canal costs about $1,200 for a front tooth to about $1,500 for a molar out-of-network, before the crown (Delta Dental).

Key takeaways
  • A root canal keeps your natural tooth; extraction removes it.
  • Extraction costs less upfront but may lead to replacement costs.
  • Root canal fees: about $1,200 (front), $1,300 (premolar), $1,500 (molar) out-of-network.
  • Extraction is better when the tooth is cracked below the gum or cannot be restored.
  • Keeping a tooth protects your bite and neighbouring teeth.

What is the difference between a root canal and an extraction?

A root canal treats an infected or inflamed tooth from the inside. The dentist removes the pulp, cleans and disinfects the canals, fills and seals them, and then restores the tooth with a filling or crown. The tooth stays in your mouth and keeps working.

An extraction removes the whole tooth, including its roots. The infection source goes with it, and the socket heals over several weeks. Afterward, you have a gap that may need to be filled with an implant, bridge or partial denture. Our guide to what a root canal procedure is explains the treatment step by step.

Which is cheaper, a root canal or an extraction?

An extraction is usually cheaper than a root canal on the day of treatment. A root canal costs about $1,200 for a front tooth, $1,300 for a premolar and $1,500 for a molar out-of-network, according to Delta Dental, and that excludes the crown or filling. CareCredit reports averages of $984 for a front tooth and $1,337 for a molar, and Pearl lists $685 to $1,800.

Extractions generally cost less, but the comparison should include what happens next. A crown after a root canal and a replacement tooth after an extraction both add significant cost. See our root canal cost guide.

Which costs more in the long run?

Extraction often costs more in the long run if you replace the missing tooth. Implants, bridges and partial dentures each carry their own substantial fees, and implants may need bone grafting if the jawbone shrinks after extraction.

If you leave the gap empty, neighbouring teeth can drift and the opposing tooth can over-erupt, which may cause bite problems, food traps and future dental work. For back teeth, some people accept a gap after discussion with their dentist, but for front teeth replacement is almost always wanted.

When is a root canal the better choice?

A root canal is usually the better choice when the tooth has enough healthy structure to restore, the roots are intact, and the surrounding bone and gums are healthy. Saving a natural tooth preserves your bite, chewing efficiency and bone, and avoids the need for a replacement.

Root canals have high success rates when done well and properly restored with a crown or filling. Most people find the procedure similar to having a large filling; see root canal pain and how long a root canal takes.

When is extraction the better choice?

Extraction is the better choice when the tooth cannot be saved or restored predictably. Common reasons include a crack that runs down the root, decay that extends below the bone, severe bone loss from gum disease, a tooth that has failed previous root canal treatment, or a wisdom tooth with no useful function.

Cost and personal priorities also matter. If a root canal, build-up and crown would be very expensive for a back tooth with a questionable outlook, some people choose extraction. Your dentist should explain the long-term outlook for each option.

Which has the easier recovery?

Recovery from a root canal is usually quicker and easier than from an extraction. After a root canal, most people have mild tenderness for a few days and return to normal activities immediately.

After an extraction, the socket needs to heal, and you must protect the blood clot for several days to avoid dry socket. Swelling and soreness are typically greater, especially after surgical extractions. Eating is restricted to soft foods for a while.

How does each option affect your bite and other teeth?

A root canal keeps your bite intact because the tooth remains in place. An extraction leaves a gap that can lead to changes over time: neighbouring teeth may tilt into the space, the opposing tooth may grow into it, and chewing may shift to the other side.

These changes can create new problems, including uneven wear and gum pockets around tilted teeth. Replacing the missing tooth prevents most of them.

How does insurance cover each option?

Insurance commonly covers root canals at 50%–80% after the deductible, according to Delta Dental. Extractions are often covered as basic services too. Crowns after root canals and replacements after extractions are usually major services covered at a lower percentage.

Ask for a pre-treatment estimate comparing both paths, including the crown or replacement, so you see the complete picture. The Cleveland Clinic's crowns guide explains why crowns are usually needed on back teeth after a root canal.

What should you ask your dentist before deciding?

Before deciding between a root canal and an extraction, ask questions that clarify the outlook and costs.

  1. How likely is a root canal to succeed on this tooth?
  2. Will the tooth need a crown, and what will the total cost be?
  3. If I extract, what are the replacement options and costs?
  4. What happens if I leave the gap?
  5. Would a specialist endodontist give a better chance of success?

Delta Dental's root canal guide provides helpful background.

Does the answer change for back teeth and wisdom teeth?

The answer can change for back teeth and wisdom teeth. Wisdom teeth that become infected are usually extracted rather than treated with a root canal, because they are hard to reach, often poorly positioned and rarely essential for chewing. Removing them avoids treating a tooth with a limited long-term role.

Second molars and first molars are different. They do much of the chewing work, so saving them with a root canal and crown is usually worthwhile when the tooth can be restored. The decision becomes closer when a molar has a questionable outlook, such as a deep crack or advanced bone loss, and the total cost of saving it is high. In those cases, discussing an extraction and the long-term plan for the gap is reasonable.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to get a root canal or pull the tooth?

A root canal is usually better when the tooth can be restored, because keeping your natural tooth protects your bite and avoids replacement costs. Extraction is better when the tooth is cracked below the gum, too damaged to restore or has a poor outlook.

Is extraction cheaper than a root canal?

Extraction is usually cheaper on the day than a root canal, which costs about $1,200 for a front tooth and about $1,500 for a molar out-of-network according to Delta Dental, before the crown. Replacing an extracted tooth with an implant or bridge often makes extraction more expensive overall.

Can I just pull a tooth instead of getting a root canal?

You can choose extraction instead of a root canal. Discuss the long-term effects with your dentist, including drifting teeth, bite changes and the cost of replacing the tooth with an implant, bridge or partial denture.

Which hurts more, a root canal or an extraction?

Both are done under local anesthetic and should not hurt during treatment. Recovery after an extraction usually involves more swelling and soreness than after a root canal, and extraction carries a risk of dry socket.

How long does a root canal tooth last compared with an implant?

A well-done root canal with a good crown can last many years, often for life. Implants can also last decades. Keeping a healthy natural tooth is usually preferred when possible, with implants as a strong option when a tooth cannot be saved.
