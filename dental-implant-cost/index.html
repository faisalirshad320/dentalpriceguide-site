Dental Implant Cost in 2026: What One Tooth, Several Teeth and a Full Arch Really Cost

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

The typical dental implant cost for one tooth is $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental. That range usually covers the implant post, abutment and crown. Bone grafts, extractions and 3D scans add to the bill, and local prices vary widely from these national figures.

Key takeaways
  • Delta Dental puts one implant at $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits; CareCredit's survey averages $2,143 for a single full-size implant and $4,344 for a standard implant per tooth.
  • A full-arch All-on-4 restoration averages $15,176 in CareCredit's survey, often less per tooth than replacing every tooth individually.
  • Add-ons such as a cone-beam CT scan, extractions and bone grafting can raise the total by hundreds or thousands of dollars.
  • Dental insurance often pays about 50% of major work, but only up to an annual maximum that is frequently modest.
  • Ask for an itemized written quote that lists the post, abutment, crown, imaging and any grafting separately.

How much does a single tooth implant cost?

A single tooth implant usually costs $2,800 to $5,600 when you pay without dental benefits, based on Delta Dental 2022 internal data. That range reflects what dentists across the country charge for one complete tooth replacement, and it is a national figure, so your local quote can land above or below it.

A second national source, the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study published by CareCredit, lists a single full-size implant at a national average of $2,143 and a standard endosteal implant at an average of $4,344 per tooth. The lower figure tends to describe the implant procedure on its own, while the higher one is closer to a fuller treatment fee. Neither number is wrong; they simply measure slightly different things.

For most people asking how much one tooth implant costs, the practical answer is this: budget for the Delta Dental range for the finished tooth, then add any extra steps your dentist says you need. A tooth that has been missing for years, a front tooth in a tricky spot, or a mouth that needs an extraction first will usually sit toward the top of the range.

Quick rule: when comparing quotes, confirm whether the price is for the implant post only or for the post, abutment and crown together. A low headline price that excludes the crown is not a complete tooth.

Why do dental implant price estimates differ so much?

Dental implant price estimates differ because each publisher measures a different slice of the bill. Delta Dental reports what dentists charge for the full single-tooth treatment without dental benefits, while CareCredit's cost study reports averages for individual procedure codes collected from a survey.

Implant treatment is billed as several separate services. The surgical placement of the post is one fee, the abutment (the connector) is another, and the implant crown is a third. Imaging, extractions and grafts are billed on top. A source that averages only the surgical step will show a lower number than a source that totals every step.

Geography matters too. Dental fees in large coastal cities are often well above fees in rural areas or smaller towns, and a national average blends all of them together. Specialist practices, such as oral surgeons and periodontists, may also charge differently from general dentists who place implants.

The useful takeaway is to treat the national figures as a reality check, not a price list. If a quote for one finished tooth is far below the lowest figure here, ask what is missing. If a quote is well above the top of the range, ask what extra work is included and why.

What is included in the price of a dental implant?

The price of a dental implant normally includes three parts: the titanium or zirconia post placed in the jawbone, the abutment that attaches to the post, and the implant crown that looks like a tooth. Whether all three are bundled into one fee depends on the practice, so read the quote line by line.

Several other items often appear on the same treatment plan:

  • Consultation and imaging. Many dentists plan implants with a cone-beam CT scan, which averages $466 according to CareCredit. The scan shows bone height, bone width and the position of nerves and sinuses.
  • Tooth extraction. If the damaged tooth is still in place, removing it is a separate fee.
  • Bone grafting. CareCredit lists an allograft bone graft (donor bone material) at an average of $811 and an autograft (your own bone) at an average of $2,779. Our guide to dental bone graft cost explains when each is used.
  • Temporary tooth. Some patients wear a temporary replacement while the implant heals.
  • Sedation. IV sedation or general anesthesia is usually billed separately from local numbing.

A bone graft is common when a tooth has been missing for a long time, because the jawbone shrinks without a tooth root to stimulate it. The Cleveland Clinic describes how grafts rebuild bone so an implant has enough support.

Ask for an itemized quote that lists each procedure separately. An itemized plan makes it far easier to compare two dentists and to see what your insurance may pay toward each step.

What changes the cost of a tooth implant?

The cost of a tooth implant changes mainly with how much preparation your mouth needs, where you live, who places the implant, and which materials are used for the crown. Two people getting a single implant on the same day can receive very different bills.

  • Bone and gum condition. Thin or soft bone may need grafting, and an upper back tooth may need a sinus lift. Each added procedure adds cost and healing time.
  • Tooth location. Front teeth demand careful shaping of gum and crown for appearance, while back teeth take heavier chewing forces. Both can raise the fee in different ways.
  • Provider type. Oral surgeons, periodontists, prosthodontists and general dentists all place or restore implants, and their fees differ.
  • Crown material. Porcelain, zirconia and metal crowns are priced differently. A gold crown on an implant depends partly on the metal content and current gold prices, so a gold tooth implant needs a custom quote rather than an average.
  • Region. Practices in high-rent metro areas usually charge more than practices in smaller cities.
  • Sedation. Local numbing is typically part of the surgery fee, while deeper sedation is extra.

The number of visits also affects the total. A standard implant is placed, allowed to fuse with the bone over a few months, and then restored with a crown. Same-day dental implants, where a temporary tooth goes on the implant at the surgery visit, are only suitable when the bone is strong enough, and the same-day dental implants cost depends on whether a final crown is still needed later.

How much do dental implants cost by type?

Dental implants cost different amounts by type, from a single endosteal implant averaging $4,344 per tooth to a full-arch All-on-4 restoration averaging $15,176, according to CareCredit. The right type depends on how many teeth are missing and how much bone you have.

  • Standard endosteal implant. The most common type, placed directly in the jawbone to replace one tooth. CareCredit's per-tooth average is $4,344.
  • Implant-supported bridge. Two implants can hold a bridge that replaces three teeth in a row. A 3 tooth implant bridge cost is usually lower than three separate implants because fewer posts are placed, but the exact price needs a dentist's quote.
  • All-on-4 full arch. Four implants support a full row of fixed teeth in one jaw. The CareCredit average is $15,176 per arch.
  • Mini implants. Narrower posts sometimes used to stabilize a lower denture. They usually cost less per post but are not suitable for every situation.
  • Implant-retained dentures. A removable denture that snaps onto a small number of implants, which is often less costly than a fixed full arch.

For a full breakdown of each design and who it suits, see our guide to types of dental implants. If you are missing most or all of your teeth, our page on the cost of full mouth dental implants compares full-arch options for one or both jaws.

When people ask how much dental implants cost per tooth, the per-tooth price tends to fall as more teeth are replaced together, because a few implants can support many teeth. Replacing every missing tooth with its own implant is rarely necessary.

Does dental insurance cover dental implants?

Dental insurance often covers part of a dental implant, typically around 50% for major work, but only up to the plan's annual maximum. Delta Dental explains that many plans follow a 100-80-50 structure, paying the most for preventive care and about half for major services, with an annual maximum such as $1,500 used as an example.

The annual maximum is the detail that matters most for implants. Even when a plan pays half, the plan stops paying once you reach the yearly cap, so most of a single implant bill can still fall on you. Some patients split treatment across two plan years, for example placing the implant in one year and the crown in the next, so two annual maximums apply.

Before you commit, check these points in your plan documents:

  • Whether implants are covered at all, or only the crown on top of the implant.
  • Any waiting period for major services on a new plan.
  • A missing tooth clause that excludes teeth lost before coverage began.
  • Whether the dentist is in-network, which usually means a lower negotiated fee.
  • Whether a pre-treatment estimate is available, so the insurer confirms its share in writing.

Original Medicare generally does not cover routine dental work such as implants, although some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits. Our guide to what insurance covers for dental implants walks through plan rules and example claims in more detail.

How can you pay less for dental implants?

You can pay less for dental implants by using an in-network dentist, getting treatment at a dental school, spreading costs over insurance plan years, paying with pre-tax HSA or FSA money, and comparing itemized quotes. Each option trims the bill in a different way.

  1. Use in-network providers. Insured patients who see an in-network dentist usually pay a negotiated fee rather than the full charge.
  2. Try a dental school. University dental schools often offer implant treatment at reduced fees, performed by supervised students or residents. Expect more appointments and longer timelines.
  3. Use HSA or FSA funds. Dental implants that restore function are generally an eligible medical expense, so paying with pre-tax dollars lowers the real cost.
  4. Ask about payment plans. Many practices offer in-house plans or third-party financing. Read the terms carefully, especially any deferred-interest promotion that adds back interest if the balance is not cleared in time.
  5. Get a second opinion. A second itemized quote shows whether a recommended graft or extra implant is necessary.
  6. Look into assistance programs. Some nonprofits, clinical trials and charity events help with implant costs. Our page on free dental implants and grants lists where to look and what eligibility usually requires.

Be cautious with very low advertised prices. Ads for 399 dollar dental implants usually cover only the implant post, not the abutment, crown, imaging or exam, so the finished tooth costs far more than the headline. Always ask for the total price of a completed tooth before booking.

What does the dental implant procedure involve?

The dental implant procedure involves an exam and scan, surgical placement of the implant post, a healing period while the post fuses with the jawbone, and finally attachment of the abutment and crown. The whole process usually takes several months from first visit to finished tooth.

  1. Consultation. The dentist checks your gums, bite and medical history, and often orders a cone-beam CT scan to plan the exact position of the implant.
  2. Extraction and grafting, if needed. A failing tooth is removed, and bone may be added so the implant has enough support. Grafts usually need time to heal before the implant goes in.
  3. Implant placement. Under local anesthesia, with or without sedation, the post is placed into the bone.
  4. Healing. Over a few months, the bone grows around the implant in a process called osseointegration.
  5. Restoration. The abutment and custom implant crown are attached, and the bite is adjusted.

Most people report soreness and swelling for a few days that is managed with over-the-counter pain relief, but experiences vary. Our article on whether dental implants hurt covers recovery and signs of a bad dental implant. You can also see realistic results in our before and after dental implants guide.

Contact your dentist or oral surgeon promptly if you have swelling that keeps getting worse after the first few days, fever, bleeding that does not slow with firm pressure, severe or increasing pain, pus, or an implant that feels loose. Seek emergency care for swelling that affects breathing or swallowing. Always follow your dentist's aftercare instructions.

Implants are not right for everyone. Uncontrolled diabetes, heavy smoking, active gum disease and some medications can affect healing, so your dentist will review your health before recommending surgery.

Are dental implants worth the cost compared with bridges and dentures?

Dental implants are often worth the higher upfront cost for people who want a fixed, long-lasting replacement that does not rely on neighboring teeth, but bridges and dentures cost less at the start and suit many patients well. The best choice depends on your bone, your remaining teeth and your budget.

A traditional bridge anchors a false tooth to crowns on the teeth on either side. A bridge is usually quicker to complete and needs no surgery, but the neighboring teeth must be reshaped. Healthline compares how long each lasts and how each is maintained, and our guide to dental bridge vs implant compares the costs side by side.

Dentures replace several or all teeth at a lower starting price, but they can move during eating and need relining as the jaw changes. Healthline also covers comfort and upkeep differences, and our comparison of dentures vs implants looks at lifetime costs.

If implants are out of reach right now, our list of cheap options for missing teeth covers partial dentures, flippers and resin-bonded bridges. A prosthodontist, a specialist in replacing teeth described by the Cleveland Clinic, can help compare every option for complex cases.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a single tooth implant?

A single tooth implant typically costs $2,800 to $5,600 without dental benefits, according to Delta Dental. CareCredit's survey lists a single full-size implant at a national average of $2,143 and a standard endosteal implant at $4,344 per tooth. Local prices vary, and extra steps like grafting or extraction add to the total.

How much do dental implants cost per tooth?

Dental implants cost roughly $2,800 to $5,600 per tooth when each tooth gets its own implant, based on Delta Dental figures. The per-tooth cost usually drops when several teeth are replaced together, for example with an implant bridge or a full-arch All-on-4, which CareCredit averages at $15,176 per arch.

Does the dental implant price include the crown?

The dental implant price sometimes includes the crown and sometimes does not. Many practices bill the implant post, abutment and implant crown as separate fees. Ask for a written, itemized quote that shows the total cost of a finished tooth, including imaging, any extraction, grafting and sedation.

Will insurance pay for a tooth implant?

Insurance may pay part of a tooth implant. Delta Dental notes that many plans cover about 50% of major work, but only up to an annual maximum, with $1,500 given as an example. Some plans exclude implants or have waiting periods, so request a pre-treatment estimate before surgery.

How much does a bone graft add to the cost of an implant?

A bone graft adds a separate fee to implant treatment. CareCredit lists an allograft bone graft at an average of $811 and an autograft using your own bone at an average of $2,779. Not every patient needs a graft; your dentist decides based on bone volume shown on imaging.

Are same day dental implants more expensive?

Same day dental implants are not always more expensive, but pricing depends on what is included. A same-day approach places a temporary tooth at surgery, and a final crown is often still made later. Only patients with enough healthy bone qualify, so ask for the full cost through the final restoration.
