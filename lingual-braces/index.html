Lingual Braces: How They Work, What They Cost and Who They Suit

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 26, 20265 price sources
Quick answer

Lingual braces are metal brackets and wires bonded to the back (tongue side) of your teeth, so they are almost invisible from the front. They are the priciest braces option: CareCredit reports an average of $9,221, and Delta Dental cites $8,000 to $10,000.

Key takeaways
  • Lingual braces are fixed braces bonded behind the teeth, hidden from normal view.
  • CareCredit's average for lingual braces is $9,221, the highest of the common braces types.
  • Expect a few weeks of tongue soreness and a slight lisp while you adapt.
  • Not every orthodontist offers them, and not every bite problem suits them.
  • Ask whether your dental plan has an orthodontic benefit before you start.

What are lingual braces?

Lingual braces are fixed orthodontic braces whose brackets are glued to the inner surfaces of the teeth, the side that faces your tongue, instead of the front. The wires and brackets do the same job as conventional braces: they apply steady, controlled pressure that moves teeth into a better position over time.

Lingual braces are usually custom-made. The orthodontist takes a detailed scan or impression, and a lab designs brackets shaped to the back of each individual tooth. That custom fit is a large part of why lingual braces cost more and why fewer practices offer them.

Lingual braces appeal most to adults and older teens who want fixed braces but do not want them seen at work, in photos or on video calls. From a normal speaking distance, most people will not notice them at all.

How much do lingual braces cost?

Lingual braces cost about $9,221 on average, according to the CareCredit procedure cost list, which draws on the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study. Delta Dental's parent guide gives a similar picture, with lingual braces at $8,000 to $10,000.

Lingual braces sit at the top of the price range for braces. CareCredit reports metal braces at $6,343, ceramic braces at $5,834 and clear aligners at $5,108 on average, so choosing lingual braces usually adds a few thousand dollars over standard metal braces. These are national averages; your local fee depends on the city, the length of treatment and how complex your bite is.

For context, Pearl's dental cost data puts comprehensive orthodontic treatment (CDT code D8080) at $3,680 to $9,650 nationally, with an average of $5,645. Lingual cases tend to land toward the upper end of that kind of range. You can compare every option on our guide to how much braces cost.

Most orthodontists quote one total fee that covers the brackets, adjustment visits and removal. Ask in writing whether retainers, emergency repairs and records (X-rays and scans) are included.

What are the pros and cons of lingual braces?

The main advantage of lingual braces is that they are hidden. People who would avoid treatment entirely rather than wear visible braces often find lingual braces the option that makes treatment possible.

  • Hidden from view: brackets sit behind the teeth, so your smile looks normal.
  • Fixed in place: unlike aligners, lingual braces work around the clock and do not depend on you wearing trays.
  • Front enamel protected: any decalcification marks from poor brushing form on the back of the teeth, not the visible front.

The trade-offs of lingual braces are real and worth weighing before you commit.

  • Higher cost: lingual braces are the most expensive common braces type.
  • Tongue irritation: the tongue rubs against the brackets, and soreness is common for the first few weeks.
  • Speech changes: many people develop a mild lisp at first that usually fades as the tongue adapts.
  • Harder cleaning: you cannot see the brackets in a mirror easily, so brushing and flossing take more care.
  • Fewer providers: lingual braces need extra training, so you may need to travel to find an orthodontist who offers them.

Who are lingual braces a good fit for?

Lingual braces suit people who need fixed braces, care strongly about appearance, and can afford the higher fee. They can treat many of the same crowding, spacing and bite problems as conventional braces.

Lingual braces may be a poor fit in a few situations. Very short back teeth, a deep overbite that pushes lower teeth against the upper brackets, or certain severe bite problems can make lingual placement difficult. Your orthodontist decides this after an exam and scans, so treat any online advice, including this page, as general information.

If cost is the sticking point, ceramic braces are a middle ground. Ceramic brackets sit on the front of the teeth but blend with the tooth colour. Read our guide to clear braces for how ceramic options and aligners compare.

What is it like to wear lingual braces?

Wearing lingual braces feels strange for the first one to three weeks, mainly because the tongue keeps finding the brackets. Most people adapt faster than they expect, and speech usually settles within a few weeks.

Soft foods help in the first days after placement and after each adjustment. Orthodontic wax over sharp spots, warm salt-water rinses and over-the-counter pain relief taken as directed on the label can ease soreness. Hard, sticky and chewy foods can loosen brackets, the same as with front braces.

Lingual braces are adjusted every several weeks, and appointments can take a little longer than with front braces because the work happens behind the teeth. Treatment length depends on your case, much as it does with any braces, and your orthodontist will give you an estimate after planning.

Call your orthodontist promptly if a wire is cutting your tongue or cheek, a bracket comes loose, or you have swelling or pain that does not ease. Your own orthodontist's instructions always come first.

Does insurance cover lingual braces?

Dental insurance may cover part of lingual braces only if your plan includes an orthodontic benefit. Many adult plans exclude orthodontics, while plans that include it often have a separate lifetime maximum for orthodontic care.

When a plan does pay, it usually pays the same benefit it would for conventional braces, and you pay the difference for the lingual upgrade. Delta Dental's coverage guide explains how deductibles, coinsurance and maximums work. Ask your orthodontist's office to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer before you sign.

Without coverage, most orthodontic offices offer monthly payment plans, and health savings or flexible spending accounts can usually be used for braces. Our page on how much braces cost without insurance covers payment options in more detail.

Frequently asked questions

How much do lingual braces cost?

Lingual braces cost about $9,221 on average according to CareCredit's procedure cost list, and Delta Dental cites a range of $8,000 to $10,000. They are the most expensive common type of braces because the brackets are custom-made and fitted behind the teeth. Local prices vary with your city and case complexity.

Are lingual braces really invisible?

Lingual braces are nearly invisible in everyday situations because the brackets sit on the tongue side of the teeth. Someone looking closely inside your mouth, or a wide yawn or laugh, may reveal them, but at normal talking distance most people will not notice you are wearing braces.

Do lingual braces affect speech?

Lingual braces often cause a mild lisp at first because the tongue touches the brackets when forming certain sounds. For most people the lisp improves within a few weeks as the tongue adapts. Reading aloud and talking normally tend to speed up the adjustment.

Do lingual braces take longer than regular braces?

Lingual braces generally move teeth on a timeline similar to conventional braces, although it depends on the case. Your orthodontist estimates treatment length after studying your scans. Adjustment appointments for lingual braces may take a little longer because the work is done behind the teeth.

Can anyone get lingual braces?

Not everyone is a candidate for lingual braces. Short back teeth, a deep overbite or some complex bite problems can make placement behind the teeth difficult. Only an orthodontist who offers lingual treatment can confirm suitability after an exam, X-rays and scans.

Are lingual braces harder to clean?

Lingual braces are harder to clean because you cannot easily see the brackets in a mirror. An orthodontic toothbrush, interdental brushes and a water flosser help. Thorough cleaning matters because plaque left around brackets can lead to decay and gum inflammation during treatment.
