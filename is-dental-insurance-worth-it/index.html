Is Dental Insurance Worth It? How to Work Out If a Plan Pays Off

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 31, 20263 price sources
Quick answer

Dental insurance is worth it when the care you expect to need, minus what you would pay out of pocket under the plan, exceeds the yearly premium. A typical plan pays 100% for preventive care, 80% for basic care such as fillings and 50% for major care such as crowns, up to an annual maximum, for example $1,500, according to Delta Dental.

Key takeaways
  • Typical plans follow a 100-80-50 structure: preventive, basic and major care.
  • Annual maximums cap what the plan pays each year; Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example.
  • Insurance is usually worth it for families, people with ongoing dental needs and employer-subsidised plans.
  • It may not pay off for people who only need cleanings and pay the full premium themselves.
  • Waiting periods and exclusions can limit value in the first year.

How does dental insurance work?

Dental insurance works differently from health insurance. Most dental plans are designed to keep routine care affordable and share the cost of bigger treatment, rather than to protect you from very large bills.

Delta Dental describes a common 100-80-50 structure: preventive care such as cleanings and exams covered at 100%, basic care such as fillings, root canals and extractions at 80%, and major care such as crowns, bridges and implants at 50%. You usually pay a deductible before basic and major coverage starts. Each year, the plan pays up to an annual maximum; Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example in its annual maximum guide. After that, you pay the full cost.

When is dental insurance worth it?

Dental insurance is usually worth it when you expect to use more care than the premium costs. It tends to pay off in these situations:

  • Your employer pays part of the premium, which makes the plan cheap for you.
  • You have a family, especially children who need cleanings, sealants and fillings.
  • You know you need treatment such as fillings, a root canal or a crown, and the plan's waiting periods have passed or do not apply.
  • You have a history of cavities or gum disease and need frequent visits.
  • You value predictable costs and regular preventive care.

When might dental insurance not be worth it?

Dental insurance may not be worth it when the premium is higher than the care you are likely to use. That can happen if you have healthy teeth, rarely need anything beyond cleanings, and pay the whole premium yourself.

It may also disappoint if you need expensive treatment right away. Waiting periods, often several months for basic care and longer for major care, mean a new plan may not pay for urgent work. Annual maximums can also be used up by a single crown or implant, leaving you paying most of a large bill.

How do you work out if dental insurance pays off for you?

You can work out if dental insurance pays off by comparing two totals for the coming year.

  1. Without insurance: estimate what your expected care would cost at self-pay prices, such as two cleanings and exams plus any known treatment.
  2. With insurance: add the yearly premium, your deductible, and your share of each procedure based on its coverage percentage.
  3. Apply the annual maximum: if the plan's share would exceed it, you pay the excess.
  4. Compare: if the "with insurance" total is lower, the plan pays off.

Use real fee estimates from a dental office and the plan's actual terms. Our guide to how much dental insurance costs covers typical premiums.

What fine print should you check before buying?

Before buying dental insurance, check the details that most affect value.

  • Waiting periods for basic and major care.
  • Annual maximum and whether unused benefits roll over.
  • Deductible and whether it applies to preventive care.
  • Network: in-network dentists charge negotiated fees.
  • Exclusions: cosmetic work, orthodontics and implants are often excluded.
  • Missing tooth clause for teeth lost before coverage began.
  • Frequency limits, such as two cleanings a year or one crown per tooth every several years.

Does dental insurance cover cosmetic work?

Dental insurance usually does not cover cosmetic work such as whitening and veneers, because these are elective rather than health-related. If your main goal is a cosmetic smile makeover, insurance will likely contribute little.

See our guide to cosmetic dentistry cost for typical prices and payment options. Preventive and restorative care needed before cosmetic work, such as cleanings and fillings, is often covered.

What are the alternatives to dental insurance?

Alternatives to dental insurance include in-house dental membership plans, discount dental plans and saving in a health savings account or flexible spending account.

Membership plans are offered by individual practices and usually include cleanings and exams for a yearly fee plus discounts on other treatment. Discount plans are not insurance but provide reduced fees at participating dentists. HSAs and FSAs let you pay for dental care with pre-tax money. CareCredit's procedure cost list can help you estimate self-pay prices when comparing.

So, is dental insurance worth it?

Dental insurance is worth it for many people, especially with an employer contribution, a family, or known treatment needs. It is less clearly worth it for individuals with healthy teeth paying the full premium, or for people needing major work immediately on a new plan with waiting periods.

Run the numbers for your own situation, read the fine print, and remember that keeping up with preventive care, whether insured or not, is the best way to keep dental costs low over a lifetime.

What are some examples of when insurance pays off?

Looking at common situations shows when dental insurance tends to pay off and when it does not. These are illustrations of the logic rather than exact figures, since prices and premiums vary.

  • Healthy adult, cleanings only: the plan covers two cleanings and exams at 100%, but if the premium is higher than the self-pay cost of those visits, the plan loses money.
  • Adult needing a filling and a crown: the plan pays 80% of the filling and 50% of the crown after the deductible, which often outweighs the premium, provided waiting periods have passed.
  • Family with two children: several cleanings, sealants and the occasional filling add up, so a family plan frequently pays off.
  • Adult needing implants: the annual maximum caps the benefit, so most of the cost is still paid out of pocket.

The common thread is that insurance helps most with steady, moderate care across the year. For a single large procedure, the annual maximum often limits how much it can help.

Frequently asked questions

Is dental insurance worth it?

Dental insurance is worth it when the care you are likely to use, minus your share under the plan, is greater than the yearly premium. It often pays off for families, people with employer-subsidised plans and people with ongoing dental needs.

What does dental insurance usually cover?

Dental insurance usually covers preventive care at 100%, basic care such as fillings at 80% and major care such as crowns at 50%, according to Delta Dental's typical plan structure, up to an annual maximum such as $1,500.

Is dental insurance worth it if I only get cleanings?

Dental insurance may not be worth it if you only get cleanings and pay the full premium yourself. Compare the yearly premium with the self-pay cost of two cleanings and exams. An in-house membership plan may be cheaper.

Can I buy dental insurance and use it right away?

Many new dental plans cover preventive care immediately but have waiting periods of several months or more for basic and major care. Check the waiting periods before buying if you need treatment soon.

What is an annual maximum in dental insurance?

An annual maximum is the most a dental plan will pay toward your care in a benefit year. Delta Dental uses $1,500 as an example. Once you reach it, you pay the full cost of further treatment until the next benefit year.

This page is general information, not dental advice. Ask your dentist for a written treatment plan and cost estimate, and ask your insurer for a pre-treatment estimate before major work.
