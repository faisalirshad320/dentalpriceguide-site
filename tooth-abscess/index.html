Tooth Abscess: What Treatment Costs, Your Options and When It Is an Emergency

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20262 price sources
Quick answer

A tooth abscess is a pocket of infection that needs a dentist to drain it and remove the source. Saving the tooth with a root canal costs about $1,200 (front) to $1,500 (molar) out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. Pulling the tooth usually costs less up front.

Key takeaways
  • A tooth abscess will not heal on its own; antibiotics can calm the infection but the source still has to be treated.
  • The two main fixes are a root canal to save the tooth or an extraction to remove it.
  • Delta Dental puts an out-of-network root canal at about $1,200 (front) to $1,500 (molar), and an extraction at $150 to $310 simple or $260 to $460 surgical.
  • Facial swelling, fever, trouble swallowing or breathing are emergency signs that need same-day care.
  • Insurance, in-network dentists, dental schools and HSA or FSA funds can all lower what you pay.

How much does it cost to treat a tooth abscess?

Treating a tooth abscess usually costs about $1,200 to $1,500 if the tooth is saved with a root canal, or $150 to $310 for a simple extraction and $260 to $460 for a surgical extraction if the tooth is removed. These are national out-of-network averages published by Delta Dental for root canals and Delta Dental for extractions. Prices in your city can be higher or lower.

The root canal figure depends on which tooth is involved. Delta Dental reports about $1,200 for a front tooth and about $1,500 for a molar, because molars have more canals and take longer to clean and seal.

The headline figure is rarely the whole bill. A tooth abscess visit often includes an exam, one or more X-rays, possible drainage and a prescription. A tooth that has had a root canal, especially a back tooth, usually needs a crown afterward, which adds a separate and often larger charge. An extracted tooth may later be replaced with a bridge, partial denture or implant, and each of those costs more again.

Ask the dental office for a written treatment plan with procedure codes and an estimate for each step. That one document makes it much easier to compare offices and to check what your insurance will pay.

What is a tooth abscess?

A tooth abscess is a collection of pus caused by a bacterial infection inside a tooth or in the gum and bone around it. The infection does not clear by itself, and the pressure of the pus is what causes the deep, throbbing pain many people describe.

A periapical abscess forms at the tip of the root. It usually starts when untreated decay or a crack lets bacteria reach the pulp, the soft tissue in the center of the tooth. Cleveland Clinic explains how cavities progress from the enamel inward until they reach the nerve.

A periodontal abscess forms in the gum pocket beside the root and is more common in people with gum disease. Cleveland Clinic describes this type as an infection of the supporting tissues rather than the tooth's inner pulp. A gingival abscess stays in the gum tissue only, often after something like a popcorn hull gets trapped.

The type of tooth abscess matters for cost. A periapical abscess usually means a root canal or extraction. A periodontal abscess may be treated with drainage and deep cleaning of the pocket, sometimes alongside ongoing gum disease care. Our guide to tooth abscess stages explains how the infection develops over time.

Tooth abscess symptoms and when it is an emergency

The most common tooth abscess symptom is a severe, throbbing toothache that can spread to the jaw, ear or neck. Other signs include sensitivity to hot and cold, pain when biting, a bad taste in the mouth, a bump on the gum that may leak fluid, and swollen glands under the jaw.

Sudden relief from pain does not mean the tooth abscess has healed. Pain can fade when the nerve inside the tooth dies or when the abscess drains through the gum, but the infection is still there and can keep spreading into the bone.

Get same-day emergency care, at a dentist or an emergency room, if you have swelling of the face or under the jaw, fever, trouble swallowing, trouble breathing, a swollen eye, or you feel generally unwell. A dental infection that spreads into the face or neck can become life-threatening.

For milder symptoms, call a dentist the same day and ask for an urgent appointment. Many offices keep emergency slots for pain and swelling. Until you are seen, over-the-counter pain relievers taken as the label directs and warm salt-water rinses can make you more comfortable. Do not put aspirin directly on the gum, and do not try to pop a gum bump yourself.

Tooth abscess treatment options and what each costs

Tooth abscess treatment has two goals: drain the infection and remove its source. Depending on the tooth and the type of abscess, a dentist will usually recommend one of the options below. The full step-by-step guide to tooth abscess treatment covers each one in more detail.

  • Root canal. The dentist or an endodontist cleans out the infected pulp, disinfects the canals and seals them. Delta Dental puts the out-of-network average at about $1,200 for a front tooth to $1,500 for a molar. A crown is often needed afterward.
  • Simple extraction. A visible, intact tooth is loosened and lifted out. Delta Dental reports $150 to $310 out-of-network.
  • Surgical extraction. A broken-down tooth, or one with complicated roots, may need a gum incision or bone removal. Delta Dental reports $260 to $460 out-of-network.
  • Incision and drainage. The dentist makes a small cut to let pus out. Drainage relieves pressure quickly but is usually a first step before a root canal or extraction, not a full fix.
  • Antibiotics. A prescription may be given when infection is spreading or you have fever or swelling. Antibiotics alone do not remove the source, so the tooth abscess tends to return if the tooth is left untreated.

Treatment by a specialist usually costs more than treatment by a general dentist. Molars needing an endodontist, or extractions needing an oral surgeon and sedation, sit at the higher end.

What changes the cost of tooth abscess treatment?

The cost of tooth abscess treatment changes most with the tooth involved, the treatment chosen and who does the work. Front teeth have one canal and are quicker to treat, while molars can have three or four canals and cost more.

  • Tooth type. Delta Dental's root canal averages rise from about $1,200 for a front tooth to about $1,500 for a molar.
  • Simple versus surgical removal. A tooth that breaks apart or has curved roots moves from the $150 to $310 simple range into the $260 to $460 surgical range.
  • Specialist fees. Endodontists and oral surgeons generally charge more than general dentists, but complex cases may need them.
  • Emergency or after-hours visits. Weekend and walk-in emergency appointments often carry an extra fee.
  • Sedation. Local anesthesia is usually part of the procedure fee; IV sedation or general anesthesia is billed separately.
  • Follow-up work. A crown after a root canal, or a bridge, partial denture or implant after an extraction, can cost more than treating the abscess itself.
  • Location. Fees in large metro areas tend to be higher than in smaller towns.

Waiting usually raises the price. A small infection caught early may be treated with a single root canal, while a long-standing tooth abscess can damage enough bone or tooth structure that extraction becomes the only choice.

Tooth abscess treatment cost with and without insurance

Tooth abscess treatment costs much less with dental insurance, because most plans treat root canals and extractions as basic services and pay a large share after the deductible. Without insurance, you pay the dentist's full fee, which is close to the Delta Dental out-of-network averages of about $1,200 to $1,500 for a root canal and $150 to $310 for a simple extraction or $260 to $460 for a surgical one.

With insurance, the share you pay depends on your plan's coinsurance level, your deductible, whether the dentist is in-network, and how much of your annual maximum is left. A crown after a root canal is usually a major service with lower coverage, so expect a bigger out-of-pocket amount for that step.

Medical insurance may help in some cases. If an abscess spreads and needs hospital care, emergency room treatment, imaging or IV antibiotics, those charges usually go through medical coverage rather than dental. The dental procedure itself is still billed to dental insurance.

If you have dental insurance, ask the office to send a pre-treatment estimate before a planned root canal or crown. In a true emergency, get treated first and sort out billing afterward.

How to pay less for an abscessed tooth

The most reliable ways to pay less for an abscessed tooth are using an in-network dentist, getting treated early, and asking for a written estimate before agreeing to the full plan. Small choices add up when a root canal and crown are involved.

  • Stay in-network. In-network dentists agree to discounted fees with your insurer, so both your share and the total bill are lower.
  • Try a dental school. Dental school clinics often charge less, with students supervised by licensed faculty. Appointments take longer, and emergency access varies by school.
  • Community health centers. Federally funded health centers offer dental care on a sliding fee scale based on income in many areas.
  • Payment plans. Many offices offer in-house payment plans, and third-party dental financing is widely available. Read the terms; promotional plans can charge back-dated interest if the balance is not paid off on time.
  • HSA or FSA. Root canals, extractions, crowns and prescriptions are qualified medical expenses, so you can pay with pre-tax health savings or flexible spending dollars.
  • Dental discount plans. A discount plan is not insurance, but it gives reduced fees at participating dentists, often with no waiting period.

Do not skip the crown to save money if your dentist recommends one. A root-canal-treated back tooth without a crown can crack, and a cracked tooth often has to be pulled, which wastes what you already paid.

What happens at a tooth abscess appointment?

A tooth abscess appointment starts with an exam and X-rays so the dentist can find which tooth is infected and how far the infection has spread. The dentist may tap on teeth, test them with cold, and check the gums and the glands in your neck.

If you need a root canal, the area is numbed, a small opening is made in the top of the tooth, and the infected pulp is removed. The canals are cleaned, disinfected and filled. Some root canals are finished in one visit; others need a second visit after medication has been left inside the tooth. A temporary filling protects the tooth until the final crown or filling is placed.

If you need an extraction, the area is numbed and the tooth is loosened and removed. The dentist will give gauze to bite on and instructions for the first days of healing. Surgical extractions may need stitches.

Most people feel noticeably better within a few days, once the pressure is relieved. Some tenderness after a root canal or extraction is normal. Call your dentist if swelling increases after the first few days, if you develop a fever, if bleeding will not stop with firm pressure, or if pain gets worse instead of better. Always follow your dentist's instructions for medicines and aftercare.

Root canal or extraction for an abscessed tooth: which costs less?

An extraction costs less on the day of treatment, but a root canal is often the cheaper choice over time when the missing tooth would need replacing. Delta Dental's figures show an extraction at $150 to $310 simple or $260 to $460 surgical, compared with about $1,200 to $1,500 for a root canal.

The comparison changes once you add what comes next. A root canal on a back tooth usually needs a crown. An extracted tooth leaves a gap that can let neighboring teeth drift and the opposite tooth over-erupt. Replacing that gap with an implant or bridge typically costs far more than the root canal and crown would have.

Extraction can still be the right call. A tooth with a vertical root fracture, very little remaining structure, or severe bone loss may not be worth saving. Wisdom teeth and teeth with no opposing partner are often removed rather than treated.

Ask your dentist two questions: what is the long-term outlook if this tooth is saved, and what would replacing it cost if it is pulled? The answers usually make the choice clear.

How to prevent a tooth abscess

The best way to prevent a tooth abscess is to treat cavities and cracked teeth while they are still small. Most periapical abscesses start as untreated decay that reaches the nerve, and a filling costs far less than a root canal.

  • Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and clean between teeth daily.
  • Keep regular checkups and cleanings so decay and gum pockets are found early. Most insurance plans cover preventive visits at a high level.
  • Limit sugary drinks and frequent snacking, which feed the bacteria that cause decay.
  • See a dentist promptly for a chipped or cracked tooth, a lost filling, or a tooth that hurts when you bite.
  • Treat gum disease, which raises the risk of a periodontal abscess.
  • Wear a mouthguard for contact sports and, if your dentist recommends it, for night-time grinding.

A tooth that has already had a root canal can occasionally develop a new tooth abscess if bacteria re-enter through a leaking crown or a missed canal. Regular X-rays at checkups help catch that early.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tooth abscess go away on its own?

A tooth abscess does not go away on its own. The pain may ease if the abscess drains or the nerve dies, but the infection remains and can spread into the jaw or surrounding tissues. A dentist needs to drain the infection and treat the source with a root canal or extraction.

How much does it cost to drain a tooth abscess?

Draining a tooth abscess is usually billed as part of an emergency visit, root canal or extraction rather than as a stand-alone price. Delta Dental's out-of-network averages for the definitive treatment are about $1,200 to $1,500 for a root canal and $150 to $310 for a simple extraction. Local prices vary, so ask for an itemized estimate.

Is a tooth abscess an emergency?

A tooth abscess is a dental emergency when it comes with facial swelling, fever, trouble swallowing or breathing, or swelling near the eye. Those signs mean the infection may be spreading and need same-day care at a dentist or emergency room. Without those signs, call a dentist for an urgent appointment the same day.

Will antibiotics cure a tooth abscess?

Antibiotics do not cure a tooth abscess by themselves. They can slow a spreading infection and reduce swelling, but the bacteria living inside the tooth or gum pocket remain. The tooth abscess usually returns unless the source is removed with a root canal, extraction or, for a gum abscess, drainage and deep cleaning.

Does insurance cover tooth abscess treatment?

Dental insurance usually covers tooth abscess treatment such as root canals and extractions as basic services, after your deductible and up to the annual maximum. A crown after a root canal is often a major service with lower coverage. Hospital care for a spreading infection usually goes through medical insurance instead.

Is it cheaper to pull an abscessed tooth?

Pulling an abscessed tooth is cheaper on the day, at $150 to $310 for a simple extraction or $260 to $460 for a surgical one in Delta Dental's out-of-network data. Replacing the missing tooth later with an implant or bridge, however, usually costs more than saving the tooth with a root canal and crown.
