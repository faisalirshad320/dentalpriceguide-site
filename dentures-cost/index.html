Dentures Cost in 2026: What Full, Partial and Implant Dentures Really Cost

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBSUpdated October 21, 20264 price sources
Quick answer

Dentures cost $1,115 to $2,425 for a full lower denture and $1,220 to $2,540 for a full upper out-of-network, according to Delta Dental. Partials, premium sets and implant-supported dentures cost more. These are national figures, and prices in your area may be higher or lower.

Key takeaways
  • A full lower denture runs $1,115 to $2,425 and a full upper $1,220 to $2,540 out-of-network, per Delta Dental 2024 data.
  • CareCredit's cost survey puts basic upper and lower dentures at an average of $452, traditional at $1,968 and premium at $6,514.
  • Implant-supported full dentures cost far more, about $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance according to GoodRx.
  • Extractions, bone work, relines and replacements are usually billed separately, so ask for a full treatment-plan quote.
  • Dental insurance often pays part of the cost of dentures as a major service, subject to waiting periods and annual maximums.

How much do dentures cost?

Dentures cost between $1,115 and $2,425 for a full lower denture and between $1,220 and $2,540 for a full upper denture when billed out-of-network, based on Delta Dental 2024 internal data. A person replacing every tooth in both jaws pays for two arches, so the upper and lower fees are added together on the bill.

Partial dentures sit in a similar band. Delta Dental lists a partial lower denture at $1,425 to $2,490 and a partial upper denture at $1,435 to $2,785. Partials often cost as much as or more than a full denture because they need a metal or flexible framework and clasps that fit precisely around the natural teeth you are keeping.

Dentures at the very low and very high ends of the market fall outside those ranges. CareCredit reports an average of $452 for basic upper and lower dentures and $6,514 for premium upper and lower dentures. Implant-supported full dentures, which clip onto implants placed in the jawbone, cost $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance according to GoodRx.

All figures on this page are national averages or ranges from the named publishers. Your local price depends on your region, the dentist or prosthodontist, the lab they use and the work your mouth needs before the denture is made.

Denture prices by type

Denture prices depend mostly on the type of denture, because each type uses different materials, lab time and chair time. A quick tour of the main options, with the locked figures we have for each:

  • Complete (full) dentures replace every tooth in one arch. Delta Dental puts a full lower at $1,115 to $2,425 and a full upper at $1,220 to $2,540 out-of-network.
  • Partial dentures fill gaps when some natural teeth remain. Delta Dental lists $1,425 to $2,490 for a lower partial and $1,435 to $2,785 for an upper partial. Our page on before and after partial dentures covers the options in detail.
  • Immediate dentures are made in advance and placed the same day teeth are removed. GoodRx lists an immediate full denture at $1,900 without insurance.
  • Flexible dentures use a bendable nylon base instead of hard acrylic and metal. GoodRx lists a flexible nylon partial at $1,700 without insurance. See our guide to flexible dentures.
  • Implant-supported dentures snap or lock onto dental implants. GoodRx lists implant-supported full dentures at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance. Our snap in dentures page breaks down that cost.

Denture quality tiers also matter. CareCredit separates basic ($452 average), traditional ($1,968 average) and premium ($6,514 average) upper and lower sets. Premium dentures typically use better-matched, more natural-looking teeth, a more customized fit and more appointments for adjustment. For a side-by-side comparison of every option, read our overview of types of dentures and cost.

A single tooth denture, often called a flipper, is a small removable partial that replaces one missing tooth. None of our sources publishes a separate national price for a single tooth denture, so ask your dentist for a written quote; flippers are usually priced as a small partial and are often used as a temporary replacement while an implant site heals.

Why do denture cost figures differ so much between sources?

Denture cost figures differ between sources because each publisher measures something different. Delta Dental reports the range of out-of-network fees charged for dentures in its claims data, which reflects what dentists actually billed. CareCredit reports averages from a procedural cost study, which groups dentures into basic, traditional and premium tiers and prices upper and lower together.

Neither number is wrong. The CareCredit basic average of $452 reflects the most economical end of the market, where dentures use stock teeth and fewer fitting visits. The Delta Dental ranges describe what a typical general dentist or prosthodontist charges for a single conventional arch. GoodRx figures, such as $1,900 for an immediate full denture, are stated as typical prices without insurance.

The practical lesson is to compare like with like. When you collect quotes, ask each office whether the price covers one arch or both, whether extractions and follow-up adjustments are included, and what kind of teeth and base material the lab will use.

What changes the price of dentures?

The price of dentures changes mainly with the type and quality of the denture, the number of arches, the preparatory work your mouth needs and where you live. Two people quoted for "full dentures" can receive very different bills for good reasons.

  • Materials and teeth. Acrylic teeth on a standard base cost less than layered, custom-shaded teeth and a characterized gum color.
  • Lab and fitting time. Premium dentures often involve extra try-in visits so the bite, lip support and tooth position can be adjusted before the final denture is processed.
  • One arch or two. Many patients need only an upper or only a lower, which roughly halves the bill compared with a full set.
  • Extractions and surgery. Removing remaining teeth, smoothing bone ridges or treating infection adds separate fees.
  • Provider type. Prosthodontists, who specialize in replacing teeth, usually charge more than general dentists, and fees vary by city and region.
  • Implants. Adding implants to stabilize a denture moves the cost into a different tier entirely.

Denture quotes that look unusually low sometimes leave out relines, adjustments or the temporary denture you will wear while gums heal. Read the treatment plan line by line before you commit.

How much does it cost to get dentures if teeth need to be pulled?

The cost to get dentures when teeth need to be pulled is the denture fee plus separate fees for each extraction and any related surgery. Few people arrive for full dentures with an empty mouth, so extractions are a large and often overlooked part of the total.

Dentures after extractions usually follow one of two paths. With an immediate denture, the dentist removes the teeth and places a pre-made denture the same day, so you are never without teeth; GoodRx lists an immediate full denture at $1,900 without insurance. With a conventional denture, the gums heal for several weeks to a few months before final impressions are taken, which often means a better long-term fit but a period without teeth or with a temporary appliance.

Immediate dentures almost always need relining as the gums and bone shrink during healing. GoodRx puts a chairside reline at about $350 or more. Some patients eventually replace the immediate denture with a new conventional one, so ask whether your plan treats the immediate denture as temporary or final. Our detailed guide to the cost of full dentures with extractions walks through a typical treatment plan.

After extractions, contact your dentist promptly or seek urgent care for bleeding that will not stop with firm gauze pressure, swelling that keeps increasing after the first few days, fever, or severe pain that pain relievers do not control.

Does dental insurance cover dentures?

Dental insurance often covers part of the cost of dentures, usually under the major services category of a plan. The exact share depends on your policy, and the Delta Dental figures on this page are out-of-network fees, so in-network patients typically pay less once the plan's negotiated fee and coverage apply.

Dental insurance coverage for dentures comes with limits worth checking before you book:

  • Waiting periods. Many plans make new members wait before major services such as dentures are covered.
  • Annual maximums. Plans cap what they pay each year, and a full set of dentures plus extractions can use up the whole yearly limit.
  • Replacement rules. Plans usually cover a new denture only after a set number of years has passed since the last one.
  • Missing tooth clauses. Some plans exclude replacing teeth that were lost before coverage began.
  • Implant exclusions. Implant-supported dentures may be only partly covered or not covered at all.

Medicare is a separate question. Original Medicare generally does not cover routine dental care or dentures, while some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits. Medicaid coverage for adult dentures varies from state to state. Ask your dentist's office to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer so you see the expected out-of-pocket amount in writing.

If you have no dental coverage at all, our guide on how much dentures cost without insurance covers self-pay pricing and discount options.

How can I pay less for dentures?

The most reliable ways to pay less for dentures are using an in-network dentist, getting care at a dental school, and spreading the cost with tax-advantaged accounts or payment plans. Combining two or three of these can lower the total noticeably.

  1. Stay in-network. In-network dentists charge your plan's negotiated fee, which is usually lower than the out-of-network ranges reported by Delta Dental.
  2. Try a dental school. Dental schools and prosthodontic residency clinics often charge reduced fees. Appointments take longer because students work under faculty supervision.
  3. Check community clinics. Federally funded health centers and some nonprofit clinics offer dental care on a sliding fee scale based on income.
  4. Use HSA or FSA money. Dentures are generally an eligible medical expense for health savings accounts and flexible spending accounts, which lets you pay with pre-tax dollars.
  5. Ask about payment plans. Many offices offer in-house plans or third-party financing. Read the terms carefully, because deferred-interest offers can become expensive if the balance is not paid in time.
  6. Time treatment across plan years. If you need extractions and dentures, splitting work across two benefit years can let you use two annual maximums.
  7. Ask about tiers. A traditional denture may meet your needs at a lower price than a premium set. CareCredit's averages show how wide that gap can be: $1,968 versus $6,514 for upper and lower.

Dentures sold at very low prices are not automatically poor, but the fit matters more than the sticker price. A denture that rubs or slips will cost more in adjustments and relines over time.

What does getting dentures involve?

Getting dentures involves an exam, impressions, a series of try-in appointments, delivery of the finished denture and several adjustment visits afterward. A conventional denture usually takes several appointments spread over a few weeks once the gums are ready.

  1. Exam and planning. The dentist checks the gums, remaining teeth and jawbone, often with X-rays, and discusses which type of denture suits you.
  2. Impressions. Molds or digital scans of the mouth are sent to the dental lab.
  3. Bite records and tooth selection. The dentist records how your jaws meet and helps you choose tooth shape and shade.
  4. Try-in. A wax version lets you and the dentist check fit, appearance and speech before the final denture is processed.
  5. Delivery and adjustments. Sore spots are common in the first weeks, and small adjustments are part of normal care. Follow your dentist's instructions on wearing the denture at night and cleaning it.

Dentures take time to get used to. Speaking and chewing usually improve over the first several weeks. Cleveland Clinic explains how prosthodontists plan dentures, bridges and implants. To see what results look like, browse our before and after dentures examples.

See your dentist rather than adjusting a denture yourself if it causes sores that do not heal, persistent pain, or a loose fit that makes eating difficult.

What do dentures cost after the first year?

Dentures carry ongoing costs after the first year, mainly for relines, repairs and eventual replacement. Budgeting for upkeep from the start avoids surprises later.

Denture relines reshape the fitting surface to match changes in your gums and bone. GoodRx puts a chairside reline at about $350 or more; lab relines, which take longer, are priced separately by each office. Immediate dentures usually need at least one reline in the first months.

Denture repairs cover cracked bases, broken clasps or teeth that pop out. Replacement becomes necessary as the jaw continues to change and the denture teeth wear down; most dentists recommend regular checkups so they can judge when a reline is no longer enough. Cleaning supplies and adhesive, if you use it, are small but ongoing expenses.

Implant-supported dentures also need maintenance. The attachments that hold a snap-in denture wear and are replaced periodically, and implants need regular professional cleaning and checks.

Are dentures cheaper than implants?

Conventional dentures are much cheaper than implant-based options at the start. A full upper denture runs $1,220 to $2,540 out-of-network per Delta Dental, while GoodRx lists implant-supported full dentures at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance.

Implant-supported dentures cost more because they involve surgery, implant components and often bone grafting, but they stay in place better and reduce the movement that causes sore spots. They also help preserve the jawbone where implants are placed. Conventional dentures rest on the gums, and the bone underneath gradually shrinks, which is why relines and replacements become part of the long-term cost.

Dentures versus implants is a clinical decision as well as a financial one. Bone volume, general health, smoking and how well you can care for implants all matter. Healthline compares the two approaches, and your dentist or prosthodontist can tell you which options suit your mouth.

Frequently asked questions

How much is a full set of dentures?

A full set of dentures means an upper and a lower denture. Delta Dental lists a full lower at $1,115 to $2,425 and a full upper at $1,220 to $2,540 out-of-network, billed separately. CareCredit's survey averages $1,968 for traditional upper and lower dentures and $6,514 for premium sets. Local prices vary.

Does dental insurance cover dentures?

Dental insurance often covers part of the cost of dentures as a major service. Coverage depends on your plan and is limited by waiting periods, annual maximums, replacement-frequency rules and sometimes missing tooth clauses. Ask your dentist to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer before treatment begins.

How much does a single tooth denture cost?

A single tooth denture, often called a flipper, is a small removable partial. None of the national sources we use publishes a separate price for it, and fees vary by office. Ask for a written quote; flippers are commonly used as a temporary tooth while an implant site or extraction heals.

What are the cheapest dentures?

The cheapest dentures are basic sets with stock teeth and fewer fitting visits. CareCredit reports an average of $452 for basic upper and lower dentures. Dental schools and community clinics can also lower the price. A cheaper denture that fits poorly may need more adjustments and relines later.

How much do implant-supported dentures cost?

Implant-supported full dentures cost $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance, according to GoodRx. The price includes implant surgery, components and the denture itself. Bone grafting, extractions and sedation can add to that total, and dental insurance may cover only part of implant work or none of it.

How long do dentures last before they need replacing?

Dentures last several years with good care, but the jaw keeps changing and the teeth wear down, so relines and eventual replacement are normal. Regular dental checkups let your dentist judge when a reline is enough and when a new denture is needed. Follow your dentist's cleaning and wearing instructions.
