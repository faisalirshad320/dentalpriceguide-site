How much do snap in dentures cost?

Snap in dentures cost $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance for implant-supported full dentures, according to GoodRx. That range reflects the full package: surgery to place the implants, the attachments that hold the denture, and the custom denture that snaps on top.

Snap in dentures cost several times more than conventional dentures. Delta Dental's 2024 internal data shows out-of-network ranges of $1,115 to $2,425 for a full lower denture and $1,220 to $2,540 for a full upper. The CareCredit cost survey lists traditional upper and lower dentures at an average of $1,968 and premium dentures at $6,514.

These sources measure different things. GoodRx gives self-pay ranges for implant-supported dentures, Delta Dental's figures come from out-of-network claims for single conventional dentures, and CareCredit's are survey averages for a full upper and lower set. Use them together to see where snap in dentures sit on the price ladder, not as one exact quote. All are national figures; local prices vary.

What are snap in dentures and how do they differ from permanent dentures?

Snap in dentures are removable full dentures that clip onto dental implants placed in the jawbone, so they stay put while you eat and talk but can still be taken out for cleaning. They are also called snap on dentures, implant-retained dentures or overdentures.

Snap in dentures usually rest on a small number of implants, often two on the lower jaw and more on the upper jaw, where the bone is softer. Attachments such as ball connectors or locator-style fittings on the implants match sockets inside the denture. The denture still rests partly on the gums, which keeps the number of implants and the cost lower than a fixed option.

Permanent dentures are different. A fixed implant denture, sometimes called a full-arch or hybrid denture, is screwed onto several implants and can be removed only by a dentist. Permanent dentures usually need more implants and stronger materials, so they generally cost more than snap in dentures, often at or above the top of the GoodRx range. Cleveland Clinic explains how prosthodontists plan these different designs.

What affects the price of implant-supported dentures?

The price of implant-supported dentures depends mainly on how many implants you need, whether you need extractions or bone grafting first, and the materials used for the denture. Here is what typically moves a quote.

  • Number of implants: each implant adds surgery and parts. A lower snap in denture on two implants costs less than an upper on four.
  • One arch or both: treating upper and lower jaws roughly doubles the work.
  • Extractions: remaining teeth often need to come out first, which adds fees and healing time.
  • Bone grafting: thin or shrunken bone may need grafting before implants can be placed.
  • Temporary dentures: many patients wear an immediate denture during healing; GoodRx lists an immediate full denture at $1,900 without insurance.
  • Denture materials: premium acrylics, reinforced bases and higher-grade teeth cost more but tend to look and wear better.
  • Attachment type: different attachment systems have different part costs.
  • Provider and region: oral surgeons and prosthodontists in high-cost areas generally charge more.

Snap in dentures also carry upkeep costs. The nylon inserts inside the attachments wear and need replacing over time, and the denture may need relining as your gums change. GoodRx lists a chairside reline at about $350 or more. Ask your dentist which maintenance costs to expect each year.

Does insurance cover snap in dentures?

Dental insurance coverage for snap in dentures varies by plan: many policies help with the denture portion as a major service, but coverage for the implants themselves is limited or excluded on some plans. Your out-of-pocket share depends on your plan's annual maximum, waiting periods and network rules.

Snap in denture coverage often runs into the annual maximum. Because the full treatment can cost far more than a typical yearly cap, even a plan that covers implants will usually pay only part of the bill. Ask your dentist to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer so you know exactly what the plan will pay before surgery.

Original Medicare generally does not cover routine dental care such as dentures or implants. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits, so check your plan documents. You can usually use HSA or FSA funds for implant dentures, which lets you pay with pre-tax money.

How can you lower the cost of snap in dentures?

The most effective ways to lower the cost of snap in dentures are choosing fewer implants when that is clinically sound, getting care at a dental school, and spreading payments over time. Several other options can help.

  1. Ask about a two-implant lower overdenture. For many people, the lower jaw causes the most denture trouble, and a lower denture on two implants is often the most affordable implant option.
  2. Convert your existing denture. In some cases, a dentist can retrofit a well-fitting current denture with attachments instead of making a new one.
  3. Visit a dental school. Prosthodontic and surgical residency programs often charge reduced fees under faculty supervision.
  4. Use payment plans. Many implant practices offer monthly plans or accept medical credit cards; read the interest terms closely.
  5. Use HSA or FSA funds. Paying with pre-tax money lowers your effective cost.
  6. Stage treatment. Some patients do one arch first and the second later, spreading costs across benefit years.
  7. Compare quotes. Ask each office for an itemized estimate covering implants, attachments, extractions, grafting, temporary dentures and follow-up visits.

If implants are out of reach for now, well-made conventional or flexible dentures can be a sensible first step. People with some healthy teeth left may be better served by a partial; see before and after partial dentures for costs and examples.

Are snap in dentures worth the cost?

Snap in dentures are worth the cost for many people who struggle with loose conventional dentures, especially on the lower jaw, because the implants hold the denture firmly and make chewing and speaking easier. Whether they are worth it for you depends on your bone, health and budget.

Snap in dentures offer clear advantages: less slipping, little or no need for adhesive, better chewing of firmer foods, and implants that help preserve the jawbone they sit in. Healthline compares these benefits with conventional dentures.

The trade-offs include implant surgery, a healing period of several months before the final denture is attached, and ongoing maintenance. Conditions such as uncontrolled diabetes, heavy smoking or certain medications can affect implant healing, so a full medical history is part of planning.

After implant surgery, contact your dentist promptly for bleeding that does not stop with pressure, fever, swelling that worsens after the first few days, pus, or an implant that feels loose. Follow your surgeon's aftercare instructions closely.

Frequently asked questions

How much do snap-in dentures cost?

Snap-in dentures, or implant-supported full dentures, cost $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance, according to GoodRx. The price includes implant surgery, attachments and the denture. Your total depends on how many implants you need, whether you need extractions or bone grafts, and local prices.

How much are permanent dentures?

Permanent dentures, meaning fixed implant dentures that only a dentist can remove, usually cost more than removable snap-in dentures because they need more implants and stronger materials. GoodRx lists implant-supported full dentures at $10,500 to $21,500 without insurance, and fixed designs often fall at or above the top of that range.

Are snap-in dentures cheaper than implants for every tooth?

Snap-in dentures are generally far cheaper than replacing every missing tooth with its own implant and crown, because a few implants support a whole arch. That efficiency is why implant overdentures are a common choice for people who have lost all their teeth on one or both jaws.

Does Medicare pay for snap-in dentures?

Original Medicare generally does not pay for snap-in dentures or dental implants because it excludes routine dental care. Some Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits that may help with dentures or implants, usually with annual limits. Check your plan's evidence of coverage before starting treatment.

How long does it take to get snap-in dentures?

Getting snap-in dentures usually takes several months, because implants need time to bond with the jawbone before the final denture is attached. Extractions or bone grafting can add more time. Many people wear a temporary denture during healing. Your dentist will give you a timeline based on your bone and health.

Can my current denture be converted to snap-in?

Your current denture can sometimes be converted to a snap-in denture by adding attachments that clip onto new implants. This works best when the existing denture fits well and is in good condition. Your dentist will check its fit, wear and design before recommending a conversion or a new denture.
