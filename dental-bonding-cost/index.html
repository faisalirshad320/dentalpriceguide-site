How Much Is Teeth Bonding? Dental Bonding Cost Per Tooth in 2026

How much is dental bonding per tooth?

Dental bonding usually costs $300 to $600 per tooth, according to Healthline. A separate cost study published by CareCredit puts the national average at $431 per tooth, which falls inside that range.

Dental bonding is priced per tooth because the dentist sculpts tooth-colored composite resin by hand on each tooth. A small chip on one front tooth sits at the lower end of the range. Rebuilding a broken corner, closing a gap between two teeth or covering a whole front surface takes longer and uses more material, so the fee moves toward the upper end.

Teeth bonding costs add up when several teeth are treated. If you want bonding on four or six front teeth to change their shape or color, multiply the per-tooth fee by the number of teeth and ask whether the office discounts multi-tooth treatment. Many dentists quote a combined fee for a smile makeover rather than a strict per-tooth price.

Both figures are national numbers. A cosmetic dental practice in a large city may charge more than the range, and a general dentist in a smaller town may charge less. Your written estimate from your own dentist is the number that matters.

Why do dental bonding cost estimates differ?

Dental bonding cost estimates differ because Healthline and CareCredit report different kinds of figures. Healthline gives a typical price range of $300 to $600 per tooth, while CareCredit reports a single average of $431 per tooth from the Synchrony Average Procedural Cost Study.

A range shows the spread you are likely to see from one office to the next. An average from a cost study shows where typical prices cluster across many patients and practices. The two numbers agree well: the CareCredit average sits close to the middle of the Healthline range.

Use the range to judge whether a quote is in a normal band, and use the average as a rough midpoint when you are budgeting. If your quote is well above the range, ask what makes your case different, such as the number of surfaces being covered or extra shaping work.

What changes the cost of tooth bonding?

The cost of tooth bonding changes with the size of the repair, the number of teeth, the tooth's location, the dentist's cosmetic experience and your local market. These factors explain why two bonding quotes for what looks like the same chip can differ.

  • Size and complexity: Filling a small chip or a tiny gap uses less resin and chair time than reshaping a whole tooth surface.
  • Number of teeth: Each tooth is usually billed separately, though multi-tooth cases may be quoted as a package.
  • Tooth location: Front teeth need careful color layering and polishing so the repair blends in. Back-tooth bonding is less common because chewing forces are higher there.
  • Dentist and practice type: Dentists who focus on cosmetic work may charge more for their artistry and time.
  • Region: Fees in high-cost cities tend to be higher than in rural areas.
  • Extra steps: Some patients whiten their teeth before bonding so the resin can be matched to the brighter shade. Whitening is billed separately.

Ask for an itemized estimate that lists each tooth being bonded. That makes it easier to compare offices fairly and to see where any savings might come from.

What is dental bonding used for, and does the use change the price?

Dental bonding is used to repair chipped or cracked teeth, close small gaps, cover stains that whitening cannot lift, reshape short or uneven teeth, and protect exposed root surfaces, and the price depends on how much resin and time each job needs. Cleveland Clinic describes bonding as a cosmetic and minor restorative option for these problems.

  • Chipped tooth: A small chip on an edge is one of the quickest and least costly bonding jobs.
  • Gaps between teeth: Closing a gap means adding resin to the sides of two teeth, so the fee often covers both.
  • Discoloration: Covering a dark or stained area may mean bonding over most of the front surface, which takes longer.
  • Shape changes: Lengthening a short tooth or evening out a smile line involves more sculpting.
  • Exposed roots: When gums recede, bonding can cover sensitive root surfaces. Because this treats a dental problem, insurance is more likely to help.

Does insurance cover teeth bonding?

Insurance usually does not cover teeth bonding done only to improve appearance, but many dental plans will help pay when bonding repairs damage, such as a chipped or broken tooth, or treats decay or sensitive exposed roots. The deciding factor is whether the bonding is considered cosmetic or restorative.

Dental bonding that fills a cavity is often billed as a tooth-colored (composite) filling, which most plans treat as basic care. Bonding to fix an accidental chip may also qualify. Bonding to close a natural gap or change the shape of healthy teeth is generally treated as cosmetic and paid out of pocket.

Ask your dentist's office to send a pre-treatment estimate to your insurer before bonding. The insurer will state in writing what, if anything, it expects to pay. Remember that your deductible and annual maximum still apply to any covered portion.

If your dentist recommends bonding after an injury, mention the injury when the claim is filed. Documentation of the accident can help show that the bonding is a repair rather than a cosmetic choice.

How much is tooth bonding without insurance?

Tooth bonding without insurance usually costs $300 to $600 per tooth based on Healthline, with CareCredit reporting a national average of $431 per tooth. Because most cosmetic bonding is not covered anyway, many patients pay these prices whether or not they have a dental plan.

Paying out of pocket for dental bonding is still one of the more affordable ways to change the look of a tooth. Cleveland Clinic lists bonding among common cosmetic dentistry options alongside veneers, crowns and whitening, and bonding typically requires the least change to the natural tooth.

Think about the long-term cost as well as the first bill. Healthline notes that bonding is usually replaced about every 5 to 10 years, and the resin can stain or chip along the way. A small touch-up or repolish costs less than a full redo, so regular checkups help keep lifetime costs down.

Dental bonding also compares well on price with the main alternatives. Porcelain veneers and crowns are made in a dental lab, usually take two or more visits and cost several times more per tooth than bonding. Veneers and crowns resist staining better and tend to last longer, so the cheaper option up front is not always the cheaper option over a decade. Ask your dentist to compare the expected lifespan and upkeep of each choice for your specific teeth, not just the first-visit fee.

How can you pay less for dental bonding?

You can pay less for dental bonding by comparing quotes, using in-network dentists when the work is restorative, paying with pre-tax HSA or FSA funds where eligible, and looking at dental schools or payment plans. The right mix depends on whether your bonding is cosmetic or repairs damage.

  1. Get two quotes. Bonding fees vary from office to office. Ask each dentist to show photos of their own bonding work.
  2. Use an in-network dentist for repairs. If bonding fixes a chip or decay and your plan covers it, an in-network dentist charges the plan's negotiated fee.
  3. Check HSA and FSA rules. Bonding that treats a dental problem generally qualifies as a medical expense. Purely cosmetic bonding usually does not, so confirm with your account administrator.
  4. Consider a dental school. Supervised students often provide bonding at reduced fees, though visits take longer.
  5. Ask about membership or discount plans. In-office membership plans and discount dental plans can lower the fee on cosmetic work that insurance excludes.
  6. Ask about payment plans. Many offices let you spread the cost. Read any financing terms closely, especially deferred-interest offers.

Bundling treatment can also save money. If you need bonding on several teeth, having them done in one visit may cost less than separate appointments.

How long does dental bonding last?

Dental bonding typically lasts about 5 to 10 years before it needs replacing, according to Healthline. How long your bonding lasts depends on where it is, how much of the tooth it covers and your habits.

Composite resin is softer than tooth enamel and porcelain. Biting nails, chewing pens or ice, opening packages with your teeth and grinding at night can all chip bonded edges. Coffee, tea, red wine and tobacco can stain the resin over time, and unlike natural teeth, bonding does not respond to whitening.

  • Brush twice a day with a non-abrasive toothpaste and clean between teeth daily.
  • Limit staining drinks, or rinse with water after them.
  • Avoid using bonded teeth to bite hard objects.
  • Ask about a night guard if you grind or clench.
  • Keep regular checkups so your dentist can polish or touch up small chips early.

When bonding wears out, the dentist can usually remove and replace it without major work on the natural tooth. Some patients later move to veneers or crowns for a longer-lasting result.

What happens during a dental bonding appointment?

A dental bonding appointment usually takes one visit, often without anesthesia, as the dentist roughens the tooth surface, applies tooth-colored resin, shapes it and hardens it with a curing light. Cleveland Clinic notes that each tooth typically takes a short appointment slot, so several teeth can be done in one sitting.

  1. Shade matching: The dentist uses a shade guide to choose a resin color that matches your teeth.
  2. Preparation: The tooth surface is lightly roughened and a conditioning liquid is applied so the resin bonds well.
  3. Applying resin: The dentist places the putty-like resin and sculpts it into shape.
  4. Curing: A special light hardens each layer.
  5. Finishing: The dentist trims, shapes and polishes the bonding so it blends with the rest of the tooth.

For a fuller explanation of the materials and who is a good candidate, read our guide on what dental bonding is.

Most people return to normal activities right away. Follow your dentist's instructions, which often include avoiding strongly staining foods and drinks for the first day or two.

When should you see a dentist about bonded teeth?

You should see a dentist if bonding chips, cracks, feels rough or sharp, or if your bite feels uneven after the appointment. Small problems with bonding are usually quick to fix when caught early.

Some sensitivity right after bonding can happen, especially if the dentist worked close to the gumline. Sensitivity that keeps getting worse, or pain when biting, is worth a call.

Seek urgent dental or medical care for facial swelling, fever, bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure, or severe tooth pain. These symptoms are not typical after bonding and can point to an infection or a cracked tooth that needs prompt attention.

If you knocked out or badly broke a tooth, see a dentist the same day. Bonding may be part of the repair later, but the first priority is protecting the tooth and its nerve.

Frequently asked questions

How much does dental bonding cost per tooth?

Dental bonding usually costs $300 to $600 per tooth, according to Healthline. CareCredit reports a national average of $431 per tooth. These are national figures, so prices in your area may be higher or lower, and larger repairs or multi-tooth cases cost more than a small chip.

How long does dental bonding last?

Dental bonding usually lasts about 5 to 10 years before it needs replacing, according to Healthline. Bonding can last longer with good habits such as avoiding hard objects, limiting staining drinks and wearing a night guard if you grind. Regular checkups help catch small chips early.

Is teeth bonding cheaper than veneers?

Teeth bonding is usually much cheaper than porcelain veneers. Bonding is done directly on the tooth in one visit with composite resin, while veneers are custom-made in a lab and often need enamel removal. The trade-off is that bonding stains and chips more easily and needs replacing sooner.

Does insurance pay for dental bonding?

Insurance may pay for dental bonding when it repairs a chipped tooth, fills a cavity or covers exposed roots. Bonding done only to improve appearance, such as closing a natural gap, is usually treated as cosmetic and not covered. Ask for a pre-treatment estimate before your appointment.

Does tooth bonding hurt?

Tooth bonding usually does not hurt, and many people need no numbing at all because the dentist adds material rather than drilling deep into the tooth. Anesthesia may be used if bonding fills a cavity or works near a nerve. Mild, short-lived sensitivity afterward is possible.

Can bonded teeth be whitened?

Bonded teeth cannot be whitened, because composite resin does not respond to whitening products. If you want whiter teeth, whiten first and then have the bonding matched to the new shade. Stained bonding can sometimes be polished, but heavy staining usually means replacing the resin.
